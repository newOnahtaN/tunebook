# Atlanta-Sandy Springs-Roswell, GA: the folk scene, October 2026

*Phase-1 dossier for the "Folk scenes of the top 20 metros" project. MSA population 6,482,182 (2025), 29 counties from Bartow and Pickens in the north to Lamar, Pike and Meriwether in the south, and from Haralson and Carroll on the Alabama line to Morgan and Jasper in the east. Items just outside (Athens, Gainesville, Dahlonega, Rome, the north Georgia mountains, LaGrange) are flagged `outside_msa`. Structured data: `atlanta.json`.*

## Scene in a nutshell

Atlanta is where commercial country fiddling began: the Georgia Old-Time Fiddlers' Conventions (1913-35) filled the Municipal Auditorium, and Fiddlin' John Carson's June 1923 OKeh record, from the South's first recording session, launched the hillbilly-record industry. The Sacred Harp still lives here: its publisher, the 1852 Chattahoochee Convention and about 20 annual all-day singings sit in the MSA's western counties. Today's scene splits between intown Decatur and Candler Park (the Frank Hamilton School, founded in 2015 by a former Weaver; Eddie's Attic; Friday and Tuesday contras; church coffeehouses running since the 1980s) and a vast suburban belt of church, barn and cafeteria bluegrass jams and modern western square-dance clubs. About 110 verified series meet roughly 200 times a month: about 41 bluegrass jams, 35 square-dance club nights and 32 open mics, but only about 11 Irish sessions and 10 old-time jams. Heritage life is big and suburban: Gwinnett's Korean festivals, Clayton's garba, Clarkston's refugee mosaic and Stone Mountain's Highland Games.

## The 10 most interesting things

1. **The cradle of recorded country fiddling, razed for a resort.** The Georgia Old-Time Fiddlers' Conventions (1913-1935) packed the Municipal Auditorium; in June 1923 Ralph Peer recorded Fiddlin' John Carson at 152 Nassau Street, the first recording session in the South, and the "pluperfect awful" record sold out in days. The building was demolished in 2019 for a Margaritaville resort despite a restraining order. ([New Georgia Encyclopedia](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/georgia-old-time-fiddlers-conventions/), [Carson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiddlin%27_John_Carson))
2. **Convention lore is wonderful.** Carson won in 1914 with his hound Old Trail howling along to "The Mockingbird"; the emcee "Alec Smart" kept order with a cowbell and megaphone and his real name is unknown; the most frequent champion, A. A. "Mule" Gray of Tallapoosa (inside today's MSA), is barely remembered; the only woman champion, Anita Sorrells Wheeler (1931, 1934), called Carson "a big bag o' wind". Lowe Stokes kept fiddling after losing his bowing hand in 1930.
3. **The Sacred Harp heartland is inside the MSA.** The Sacred Harp Publishing Company (Carrollton/Bremen), the Chattahoochee Musical Convention (1852, the oldest surviving Sacred Harp convention) and about 20 annual all-day singings sit in Carroll, Haralson, Heard and DeKalb counties. The 2025 Edition, the first revision in 34 years, debuted at the United Convention in Atlanta with singers from 35 states and 6 countries, and 10,000 copies went out in six months. ([Sacred Harp Publishing Co.](https://sacredharp.com/about/), [Minutes](https://fasola.org/minutes/))
4. **Singers visit the compiler's grave.** B. F. White, who compiled *The Sacred Harp* (1844), is buried under a magnolia in Oakland Cemetery, and singers go to sing to him after conventions. Two Decatur singings are among the last anywhere to use J. L. White's early-1900s "White Book".
5. **A second, African American shape-note world.** The United and Associated Note Singers sing seven-shape gospel books on a Sunday circuit of west and south metro churches, slower and more improvised than Sacred Harp; Carroll County is also Thomas A. Dorsey's birthplace, and Douglasville's Claude Joseph Johnson (NEA Fellow) sold a gold record of lined hymns in 1970.
6. **A Weaver founded the folk school.** Frank Hamilton, co-founder of Chicago's Old Town School (1957) and an early-1960s Weaver, co-founded the [Frank Hamilton School](https://frankhamiltonschool.org/) in Decatur in 2015. At about 92 he was still teaching in 2026; the school runs about 45 weekly classes per term and hosts the city's only weekly old-time jams.
7. **Bluegrass swamps Irish.** About 41 verified bluegrass jam meetings a month, in churches, a family barn ([Everett's Music Barn](http://www.everettsmusicbarn.net/), a duplex rebuilt in a Suwanee cornfield around 1970), a beer garden and a meat-and-three cafeteria, against about 11 Irish session meetings. Roswell Music Jam grew from 8 people in 2022 to about 70 and spawned church-hosted sister jams across the suburbs.
8. **Square dancing outnumbers contra three to one.** About 35 modern western square-dance club nights a month against about 11 contra and English dances; one of the busiest clubs is [Hotlanta Squares](https://iagsdc.org/hotlanta-squares), an LGBTQ+ club. Meanwhile the Tuesday Night Contra dances about half its nights to recorded music and has staged an "Electro Contra".
9. **Church coffeehouses and a 53-year radio show.** Lena's Place (since 1983-84, each month's door to a different charity) and AAFFM's Fiddler's Green (1986) run in churches; WRFG co-founder Harlon Joye has hosted Fox's Minstrel Show since 1973. Eddie's Attic's Open Mic Shootout has crowned Jennifer Nettles, John Mayer and Tyler Childers.
10. **Heritage life is big and suburban.** The St. Patrick's parade (1858) predates the 1864 burning of Atlanta; the Burns Club (1896) meets in a replica of Burns' cottage; garba runs nine nights at a Clayton County temple; Gwinnett's Korean Festival draws about 100,000; Clarkston's World Refugee Day features Karen singers and a Lao ranat; and the first Native-led powwow inside city limits came only in 2022.

## Character: what makes Atlanta different

- **Two cities in one.** The intown folk scene is compact and walkable: Decatur (Frank Hamilton School, Eddie's Attic, the Friday contra, Waller's and Napoleon's open mics, First Thursday Sacred Harp), Candler Park (the First Existentialist Congregation: English dance, WaltzPlay, Fiddler's Green) and nearby Grant Park, Ormewood Park and Little Five Points (WRFG). The suburbs and exurbs are a different scene: church-hall and barn bluegrass jams in Roswell, Cumming, Woodstock, Fayetteville, Suwanee and Tucker; modern western square-dance clubs from Cartersville to Stockbridge; RSCDS classes in Marietta, Cartersville and Lawrenceville; and the Sacred Harp country of Carroll, Haralson and Heard counties an hour west.
- **Heritage deep, revival thin.** Atlanta's strongest traditions are the ones that never needed a revival: Sacred Harp, African American note singing and gospel, bluegrass jamming and Scottish-heritage societies. Revival-style trad (Irish sessions, old-time jams, contra, English) is real but small for a 6.5-million metro, and several staples of other cities (morris, Scottish/Cape Breton sessions, klezmer, Scandinavian dance, son jarocho) could not be found at all.
- **The old-time lineage broke.** The city that made the first country records now has only the Frank Hamilton School's weekday open jams as dependable weekly old-time; the north Georgia fiddle contests moved to the mountains (Hiawassee, Blairsville), outside the MSA.
- **Faith buildings again.** As in Philadelphia's meetinghouses, Atlanta's surviving folk series live in churches: the Existentialist congregation, Central Congregational (Lena's Place), Primitive Baptist churches for Sacred Harp, Baptist churches for note singing, and church fellowship halls for suburban bluegrass jams.
- **Who shows up.** Sacred Harp singings mix multi-generation Georgia singing families with newer urban singers, and report an influx since the 2025 edition; AAFFM worries about an aging audience; the intown contras are younger and progressive; suburban jams skew retired.

## A visiting fiddler's week

- **Monday:** Eddie's Attic songwriter open mic (biweekly); third-Monday Sacred Harp in Grant Park; Lola's Jam (blues) in West Midtown.
- **Tuesday:** the Best Damn Session in the Land at Thos. O'Reilly's in Sandy Springs, or Trad Music Tuesdays at the Marlay House in Decatur (it drifts from Irish into old-time, Cajun and Balkan; ask first); Roswell Music Jam; Tuesday Night Contra (Ormewood Church); Frank Hamilton School open jam.
- **Wednesday:** bluegrass night at Ormsby's (West Midtown); Frank Hamilton School open jam; Woodstock Music Jam (1st Wednesday).
- **Thursday:** Frank Hamilton School Irish session (3rd Thursday); Cumming (2nd/4th) or Fayette (2nd) Music Jam; Matthews Cafeteria jam in Tucker; First Thursday Sacred Harp in Decatur; RSCDS class in Marietta.
- **Friday:** the Atlanta Contra Dance in Decatur.
- **Saturday:** Everett's Music Barn in Suwanee (picking in the farmhouse rooms, a band in the barn); Fiddler's Green or Lena's Place coffeehouse (monthly); in season, an all-day Sacred Harp singing out west.
- **Sunday:** English country dance in Candler Park (2nd/4th), Red's Beer Garden bluegrass (1st/3rd), WaltzPlay (1st), or an all-day singing with dinner on the grounds.
- **Annual must-dos:** the Chattahoochee Musical Convention (August) and United Convention (September), Atlanta Dance Weekend (November, since 1977), Stone Mountain Highland Games (October), IrishFest Atlanta (November), and just outside the MSA the Georgia Mountain Fair fiddle contests (Hiawassee) and Camp Fasola.

### 2. Old-time

Atlanta is where commercial old-time began. The first Georgia Old-Time Fiddlers' Convention was held at the Municipal Auditorium on April 1, 1913, and Fiddlin' John Carson placed fourth. Ralph Peer recorded Carson for Okeh in a Nassau Street building on June 19, 1923 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiddlin%27_John_Carson)). Yet today's dedicated old-time jams are few and mostly daytime:

- **FHS open jams: Tuesday and Wednesday at 11:30 am, weekly.** These follow morning fiddle and banjo classes, including "Intro to Old-Time Jam Sessions". FHS says they are eclectic, but "you'll find there are a lot of old-time fiddle tunes" ([events](https://frankhamiltonschool.org/events/), [Know Your Jam](https://frankhamiltonschool.org/know-your-jam/)).
- **FHS old-time classes.** John Salyer teaches old-time fiddle repertoire. Other classes include "Tunes for Old-Time Jams: The Chestnuts / Beyond the Foundations", Old-Time Band, Old-Time Mandolin and old-time harmonica. FHS also runs a periodic Clairmont Place jam and plays the Chomp & Stomp festival jam.
- **Toco Hills Old-Time Jam, Thursday at 7 pm** on the sidewalk outside Spiller Park Coffee, run by Maura (Nicholson) of FHS. The SEBA entry dates from Nov 2023 and was not reconfirmed (low confidence) ([SEBA](https://sebabluegrass.org/events/toco-hills-old-time-jam-484/)).
- **Slopes BBQ (formerly McDaniel's QN2), Sandy Springs.** An "Old Time and Bluegrass Jam" on Saturdays 2-5 pm, with Doc Byrd. A visiting Elkins, WV fiddler commented that the Saturday BBQ jam was old-time (low confidence) ([SEBA list](https://www.davidbelcher.net/seba_web/jams.php)).
- **Monroe: third Saturday, 1-4 pm**, at the Monroe-Walton Center for the Arts, run by Doug Olsen (medium confidence) ([SEBA](https://sebabluegrass.org/events/old-time-music-open-jam-721-186-174/)).
- **Marlay House Tuesdays** are partly old-time.
- **Dahlonega Appalachian Jam** (outside the MSA) meets on Saturdays April-October on the Gold Museum lawn.

## Part 2. Folk song, listening rooms, radio and traditional singing

*Atlanta-Sandy Springs-Roswell MSA (29 counties). Research as of 2026-10-30. Companion data: `atlanta-song.json`.*

### Part A: Traditional singing (Layer 3)

#### A1. Why Atlanta is the Sacred Harp heartland

*The Sacred Harp* was published in 1844 by B. F. White and E. J. King, both Georgia residents ([Atlanta Sacred Harp](https://atlantasacredharp.org/)). White moved to Georgia in 1842, retired to DeKalb County, and died in 1879 after a fall on the street. He is buried in **Oakland Cemetery** (Section 4, Block 125, under a southern magnolia). Local singers keep a page with directions to the grave, its coordinates and accessibility notes, and they will come and sing there with visitors ([B. F. White's grave](https://atlantasacredharp.org/resources/bfwhitegrave.html)). His son James Landrum "J. L." White, editor of the "White Book" revisions, died in Decatur in 1925.

The **United Sacred Harp Musical Association** was founded in Atlanta by J. L. White and Joe S. James after large singings at 1904 revival tents. It was "based in that city for most of its early history" ([Warren Steel, Ole Miss](https://home.olemiss.edu/~mudws/unitedhistory.html)). Steel dates it 1905 and SHPC dates it 1904. Its 120th session, in 2025, came back to Atlanta for the debut of the new edition.

The **Sacred Harp Publishing Company** was founded in 1933 and became a nonprofit in 1974. It keeps its headquarters and the Sacred Harp Museum at 162 Oak Grove Rd, Carrollton ([SHPC About](https://sacredharp.com/about/)). Its 2026 board is chaired by Atlanta's John Plunkett, with Jesse P. Karlsberg as vice president and Bill DuPre among the members. Its president from 2002 to 2025, Mike Hinton, a grandson of T. J. Denson, died on July 18, 2025, just before the launch ([tribute](https://sacredharp.com/2025/08/05/in-memoriam-mike-hinton/)).

**Hugh McGraw** (1931-2017) of Bremen became an NEA National Heritage Fellow in the program's first year, 1982. He was the company's executive secretary and treasurer from 1958 to 2002 while managing a clothing plant, chaired the 1991 revision, and founded the National Sacred Harp Convention in 1980 ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/hugh-mcgraw); [NEA statement](https://www.arts.gov/news/press-releases/2017/national-endowment-arts-statement-death-hugh-mcgraw-1982-nea-national-heritage-fellow)). He also acted in *The Long Riders* (1980) and *Lawless* (2012) ([Hymnary](https://hymnary.org/person/McGraw_H)). His best-known line, as quoted by AAFFM's Doug Allison: *"I wouldn't cross the street to listen to this music but I'd cross the country to sing it."*

#### A2. The 2025 Edition

According to John Plunkett, the revision conversation began in 2014 and work started in 2018. David Ivey, from a northeast Alabama singing family, chaired the committee. About 1,200 songs were submitted and about 113 were chosen, and the layout was tightened so the book did not grow ([Atlanta Folk #20](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-20-sacred-harp-singing)). After the debut, SHPC reported 10,000 copies distributed and that "attendance at singings is up". A third printing appeared in February 2026. Books cost $20, cases of eight cost $160, and a case of loaners costs $80 ([SHPC](https://sacredharp.com/2026/04/08/third-printing-sacred-harp-2025-edition/)). The SHMHA Minutes editor wrote: *"Wow!! What a wonderful time to be a Sacred Harp singer"* ([Minutes 2025, p. 3](https://fasola.org/minutes/OnlineMinutesPDFFiles/SacredHarpMinutesOnline2025.pdf)). Not every singing switched books. Poplar Springs Primitive Baptist Church (Bowdon, started in 2018) recorded its 2025 session as a "1991 Edition" singing, and atlantasacredharp.org still lists it under the 1991 book.

Media followed. GPB aired a radio program on the convention and the debut on March 20, 2026, and Atlanta Interfaith Broadcasters filmed local singings for *Sacred Sounds and Spaces* ([AtlantaSinging, Mar 2026](https://groups.google.com/g/atlantasinging/c/Fkig91wnaTg)).

**Character.** The urban and rural communities depend on each other. The Atlanta cohort fills offices at the rural singings (Bill DuPre gives opening prayers at Holly Springs, and Plunkett, Shawn Taylor and Lisa Bennett lead and arrange). The rural churches host most of the days. Plunkett's own path shows the link. He started in the early 1990s by driving to Friday-night singings at **Big Creek Primitive Baptist Church**, Alpharetta, which was then farmland before GA 400 was finished. The older singers there were his parents' generation, men "who'd farmed the land Alpharetta now sits on" ([#20](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-20-sacred-harp-singing)). Big Creek hosted the Georgia State Convention in 1997, 1999, 2007 and 2016 ([fasola search](https://fasola.org/minutes/search/?n=2523); [GPB 2016](https://www.gpb.org/news/2016/03/29/make-joyful-noise-the-art-of-sacred-harp)). It does not appear on the 2026 calendars, and the Alpharetta June Singing now meets at City Hall.

**The urban singers' social program.** The 2026 Union Convention schedule shows the newer culture: a Friday "Songs Abound" night on *The Social Harp* (an 1850s Georgia tunebook) at a member's home, a Saturday tour of the DeKalb Farmers Market, a Sunday-morning birding walk in Legacy Park, an after-singing "Composium" of new songs, and a visit to B. F. White's grave ([Union 2026](https://atlantasacredharp.org/events/union2026.html)). In 2025 singers also opened the "Spring Sprang Sprunk" festival of spontaneous music at Baby's Place, a southeast Atlanta venue (Minutes 2025).

**The Seed & Feed connection.** Plunkett met Sacred Harp in the choir **Schola Nova**, which grew out of Kelly Morris's **Seed & Feed Theatre** (the same circle as Schola Cantorum and the Seed & Feed Marching Abominable). Morris had picked up Sacred Harp in Vermont ([#20](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-20-sacred-harp-singing)). The annual **Seed and Feed singing** (47th session in 2025) keeps the name, and Morris led song 535 at it in 2025. This is a revival lineage that came back to the heartland by way of New England.

#### A5. African American seven-shape "note singing"

This is a separate and less-documented tradition. Black singers in west and middle Georgia sing from seven-shape gospel books, including the Church of God *Church Hymnal* (1951) and photocopies. They sit in the hollow square and sing the notes first, but their singing is "slower" with "more improvisation" ([UWG Center for Public History](https://www.shapenote.uwgcph.org/united-shape-note-singers/united-shape-note-singers/)). The University of West Georgia project describes the **United Shape Note Singers** traveling "from church to church on most Sundays of the year with suitcases full of songbooks" ([project home](https://www.shapenote.uwgcph.org/)). The **Associated Note Singers, Inc.** was incorporated on July 23, 1985 at 985 Oakland Ave/Dr SW, Atlanta, the site the 2019 schedule calls "The Center" ([registry](https://www.georgiacompanyregistry.com/companies/associated-note-singers-inc/)). The corporation is now listed as administratively dissolved, which does not by itself mean the singing stopped.

Atlanta Sacred Harp's [note-singing page](https://atlantasacredharp.org/notesing.html), last updated in 2019, lists about **52 singings in 2019**. I count about 40 of them inside MSA counties: southwest Atlanta (St. Luke AME, Macedonia Baptist, Springfield MBC, Mt. Ephraim, Bible Way, The Center), Hiram, Dallas, Yorkville, Douglasville, Winston, Villa Rica (Mt. Prospect Baptist, 133 Thomas Dorsey Drive), Griffin, Zebulon, Molena, Williamson, Barnesville, Millner, Jackson, Hampton, Fayetteville, Marietta, Buchanan, Tallapoosa, Canton and Temple. The year ended with two Christmas banquets. For 2026 I could confirm only the **Clyde Williamson Memorial Note Singing**, on Nov 8, 2026 at Griffin Chapel UMC in Temple ([Nov 2026 calendar](https://atlantasacredharp.org/monthly.html)). The circuit therefore stays in the data but is excluded from the monthly totals (about 3.3 a month if it is still at 2019 strength; low confidence).

### Part B: Folk song and listening scene (Layer 2)

#### B1. AAFFM and Fiddler's Green

The **Atlanta Area Friends of Folk Music** formed in 1976 "to host monthly sing-alongs". Members put on concerts in borrowed space, "sometimes covering losses at the door with money from their own coffers" (1985 *Atlanta Journal*, quoted in [#10](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-10-fifty-years-ago-in)). Its first concert was in December 1976 at Garden Hills Recreation Center. Early bookings at the Unitarian church included Trapezoid (1981), Mike Seeger and Jean Ritchie (1983), Malcolm Dalglish and Grey Larsen (1983), a 1984 season opener with Bruce Molsky, the Hurt Dog String Band and Elise Witt, and D. L. Menard (1985).

**Fiddler's Green**, AAFFM's monthly coffeehouse, began in **December 1986**, so 2026 is both the society's 50th year and the series' 40th. It now runs on **3rd Saturdays at 7pm** at the First Existentialist Congregation, 470 Candler Park Dr NE. Admission is **$10, or $8 for members**, and "all proceeds beyond our rent go to the performers" ([AAFFM](https://aaffm.org/aaffm-events/)). The talent schedule is posted through December 2027. Typical bills pair two acts across old-time, cowboy song, Celtic, songwriters and a mandolin orchestra. Recent nights: Mick Kinney & Friends (Oct 17, 2026); Vega Verde Wranglers with Pickers Anonymous, a 2026 IBMA Ramble showcase trio (Nov 21); a potluck and jam before the Solstice Sisters (Dec 19). The 2026 shows are in AAFFM's [events API](https://aaffm.org/wp-json/tribe/events/v1/events).

The prices show how the scene has aged. In 1990 Fiddler's Green cost $3 at Garden Hills ([#18](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-18-where-were-you-in)). In 1983 shows "traditionally culminate[d] in a midnight jam". Now "everyone's on the road home before 10" ([#21](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-21-lenas-place-coffeehouse)). In 1990-91 AAFFM also ran monthly Saturday Night Get-Togethers in homes (one a pool party with Irish Arts of Atlanta), monthly newsletter folding parties at the Freight Room, a Hard Labor Creek campout with Sunday-morning shape-note and gospel singing, a Spring Fling folk festival at **Redwine** (Chris Moser's land in Lithonia), and an all-day Sacred Harp singing at the DeKalb County Courthouse ([#22](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-22-musing-on-aaffms); [#7](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-7-time-machine-1988)). The current president, **Linda Hoopes**, writes the *Atlanta Folk* Substack (issue #22, Oct 29, 2026) and is asking openly what the society's role should be. Membership numbers are not published, and the Facebook page has about 649 followers. AAFFM recently joined Folk Alliance International as an organizational member.

### Part C: History in brief

- **1844-1905.** *The Sacred Harp* is published by Georgians (1844). The Chattahoochee Convention is organized (1852). B. F. White dies in Atlanta (1879). The United Convention is founded in Atlanta after large 1904 tent-revival singings.
- **1920s-30s.** Atlanta becomes a country recording and radio center (Wayne Daniel, *Pickin' on Peachtree*, 1990, cited in [#18](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-18-where-were-you-in)). The Sacred Harp Publishing Company is founded (1933). Thomas A. Dorsey, born in Villa Rica in 1899, is creating gospel in Chicago.
- **1960s-70s folk wave.** "Acoustic music clubs like The Bistro, The Great Southeastern Music Hall, Twelfth Gate, the Bottom of the Barrel and the Catacombs hosted local folk-accented bands... As the wave ebbed the clubs disappeared" (1985 AJ). The Bistro (1102 W. Peachtree) hosted Jerry Jeff Walker around 1966-67 ([recollection](https://www.beaufortcountynow.com/post/41936)). The Great Southeast Music Hall opened Oct 30, 1972 in Broadview Plaza (capacity about 530), where Steve Martin played banjo and the Sex Pistols played their first US show on Jan 5, 1978 ([Seal Place](https://sealplace.blogspot.com/2014/12/the-great-southeast-music-hall.html); [Alley 61](https://www.alley61.com/landmarks/great-southeast-music-hall-first-sex-pistols-show-in-america)). WRFG goes on air in 1973, AAFFM forms in 1976, and the Seed and Feed singing begins around 1979.
- **1980s.** Moonshadow Saloon (1980-86, near Emory; early R.E.M. and Indigo Girls; [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonshadow_Saloon)). Musikworks School of Traditional Music (instrument shop 1981; school with Frank Hamilton on faculty; closed Aug 15, 1986). The Freight Room in the Decatur depot (restaurant 1981, music club by the late 1980s, closed Jan 1999; [railga](https://railga.com/depots/decatur.html)). Lena's Place (1983/84), Fiddler's Green (1986), the Upstage Cafe (1988).
- **1990s-2000s.** The 1991 Denson revision (McGraw). Eddie's Attic (1992). Smith's Olde Bar (1994). *An Eclectic Harmony* (1999). The urban Sacred Harp cohort grows from Oakhurst Baptist and the Seed & Feed circle into Big Creek and the west-Georgia churches. The Emory singing begins around 2007.
- **2015-2026.** The Frank Hamilton School (2015). Waller's (2019). Kavarna closes (2023). The 2025 Edition debuts in Atlanta. In 2026 the First Thursday singing moves to Holy Trinity, Monks Meadery's pub sing begins, and AAFFM turns 50.

### 1. Scottish and Irish: the old Celtic establishment

**Stone Mountain Highland Games and Scottish Festival.** The 54th games ran **Oct 16-18, 2026**: Friday events at the Hilton Atlanta Northeast, then two days in the meadows of Stone Mountain Park with pipe bands, heavy athletics, piping and drumming contests, Highland dance, clan tents, Scottish country dancing and fiddling ([smhg.org](https://smhg.org/); [RSCDS page](https://rscds-atlanta.org/stone-mountain/)). The 55th is already set for Oct 15-17, 2027. Sources disagree on the founding year: Stone Mountain Park says "since 1972" and the Atlanta Pipe Band says it played "the first Stone Mountain Highland Games in 1972", while the games' social handles use "SMHG1973" and a 54th edition in 2026 implies 1973 if the games were never skipped. No published attendance figure exists beyond "thousands" (low confidence). An online archive of programs back to 1973 survives at [Electric Scotland](https://www.electricscotland.com/familytree/stone/1973/index.htm).

What a fiddler would notice: the games carry a live-music Scottish country dance strand. The RSCDS Atlanta Branch runs three 45-minute platform sessions each day, a parade-field demonstration, and two evening dances (Friday Fall Gala, Saturday Tartan Ball) at the host hotel with **Mara Shea (fiddle), Julie Gorka (piano) and Tom Pixton (accordion)**. The 2026 programs include dances with Atlanta names, such as "The Stone Mountain Reel", "The Swans of Atlanta", "Sylvia, Priestess of Atlanta" and "The Cherokee Rose" ([RSCDS](https://rscds-atlanta.org/stone-mountain/)).

**Pipe bands.**
- The **Atlanta Pipe Band** is in its 56th year (formally founded 1970). It began in 1967 when Emory student Henry Frantz was photographed playing bagpipes by the AJC and other pipers called him; Frantz stayed in the band until he died in 2025. It fields Grade 3 and Grade 4 competition bands, has been US Open Grade 3 champion, has IMDb credits (*Into the Wild*; *Celtic Woman: Believe* at the Fox) and runs the APB Academy for learners aged 8 and up ([APB](https://www.atlantapipeband.com/about)).
- **John Mohr MacKintosh Pipes and Drums** (1977), the games' host band, rehearses Saturday mornings and welcomes beginners. Its crest puts a Creek feather war bonnet on a black panther, nodding to Captain John Mohr MacKintosh's colonial Georgia story and to the Scottish-Creek McIntosh lineage ([SMHG host band](https://www.smhg.org/hostBand.php)).

**RSCDS Atlanta Branch (1976, 50th anniversary in 2026).** It runs classes in Marietta, Cartersville, Atlanta, Lawrenceville and Athens (the Cartersville class meets on the 1st and 3rd Monday). For the anniversary it published a book of **26 new dances with 14 original tunes**, plus an album by Copious Notes (Mara Shea and Julie Gorka) ([50th](https://rscds-atlanta.org/50th-anniversary/); [book](https://rscds-atlanta.org/50th-anniversary-book/)). This is the place where Atlanta's heritage scene most directly creates new fiddle repertoire.

**Burns Club of Atlanta (1896).** It is a literary society limited to 100 members (men only by tradition; guests welcome by invitation) that meets in the Burns Cottage, completed in 1911 as an exact replica of Burns' birthplace. Burns Suppers have been held every year since 1898, and the club ranks 53rd in seniority in the World Burns Federation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burns_Club_of_Atlanta)). The cottage's NRHP coordinates are 33.7269, -84.3555.

**Irish.**
- The **Hibernian Benevolent Society** (1858, about 130 members) meets on the 2nd Thursday of each month at a Dunwoody pizzeria and maintains the Hibernian Rifles plot at Oakland Cemetery, where members can still be buried ([HBSA](https://www.hibernianatl.com/our-vision)).
- The **St. Patrick's parade** remained a small annual affair for a century. In 1999 Msgr. E. J. Dillon pushed for a coalition, and the Parade Committee now includes the AOH, Clan na nGael, the Fire and Police Emerald Societies and the Irish Information Center ([history](https://atlantastpats.com/parade-info/history/)). The 142nd parade was in Midtown in March 2026.
- **Irish step-dance schools** (the Drake School is the best known) march in the parade. Irish sessions such as the Tuesday session at Decatur's Marlay House belong to Layer 1 ([ArtsATL world-music agenda](https://www.artsatl.org/arts-agenda-world-music-spring-2025/)).

**Vitality:** thriving (Scottish), active (Irish heritage societies). Both are run by older, mostly white, suburban volunteer networks. The pipe bands and RSCDS clearly recruit (academies, beginner sessions); the Burns Club is closed by design.

### 2. African American and African diaspora traditions

#### West African dance and drum (diaspora reclamation)
Atlanta's strongest heritage-dance practice is not immigrant transmission. It is African American study and teaching of West African and Caribbean forms, dating from the Black Arts era.

- **Giwayen Mata** formed in 1993 out of community jam sessions (founders include Omelika Kuumba, Osunlade Fatunmise, Sarahn Henderson and others); Tambra Omiyale Harris is artistic director. Every member dances and drums. Its **bi-weekly Sunday 3-4:30 pm West African dance class** at Moving in the Spirit (1458 La France St NE, by Candler Park MARTA) is taught to a live djembe orchestra and covers Baga, Bamana, Malinke and Susu repertoire; classes are listed for Nov 8 and Nov 22, 2026 ([class page](https://www.giwayenmata.org/dance-class)). The 32nd-anniversary special in March 2025 included a Haitian dance class and a drum class ([event](https://www.giwayenmata.org/event-details/32nd-anniversary-dance-drum-classes-2025-03-30-15-00)). Every **Dec 30** the group stages *Kwanzaa Freshii*; it has run a Kwanzaa program for 15+ years, and youth may join right up to show time ([ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/giwayen-mata-keeps-the-spirit-of-kwanzaa-alive-through-dance/)). Founding director Omelika Kuumba (Brooklyn-raised, ex-director of the Barefoot Children's Dance Ensemble) now leads her own band ([ArtsATL 2024](https://www.artsatl.org/omelika-kuumba-will-bring-her-soaring-high-concert-to-atlantas-artsxchange/)).
- **Atlanta African Dance & Drum Festival** (Afrikan Djeli Cultural Institute, East Point). The 16th edition (July 25-27, 2025) offered workshops in Haitian, Afro-Cuban/Orisha, Sabar, Guinean, Malian and Ivorian dance, with a finale by Afriky Lolo of St. Louis (40+ performers aged 6-60). The 17th edition (July 2026) was headlined by Brooklyn's Asase Yaa ([2025](https://www.artsatl.org/afrikan-djeli-cultural-institute-and-afriky-lolo-bring-west-african-dance-and-culture-to-atlanta/); [2026](https://www.artsatl.org/performance-headlining-this-weekends-atlantas-african-dance-and-drum-festival-will-explore-west-africas-influence-on-brooklyn/)).
- **Pan African Festival, Decatur** (Beacon Hill Black Alliance; 6th annual on **Aug 15, 2026** on Decatur Square). Performers included **Kilombo Drum/Dance, Each One Teach One Drum/Dance, Pan People Steelband and Unidos Atlanta Samba** ([BHBA](https://beaconhillblackalliance.org/2026-pan-african-festival-08-15-26/)).
- **Ballethnic Dance Company** (East Point, 30+ years) blends ballet with African dance concepts "sur la pointe" ([Ballethnic](https://www.ballethnic.org/)). It is a professional company, so it adjoins rather than belongs to folk practice.

**Vitality: thriving.** There are multiple weekly or bi-weekly classes, intergenerational ensembles, an annual festival and public funding (Fulton County and the Mayor's Office of Cultural Affairs fund Giwayen Mata).

#### Ring shout, praise houses and lined hymns
- **Praise House Project** (Charmaine Minniefield). A movable one-room praise house with a ring-shout projection and sound installation, first shown at Oakland Cemetery in 2021 to honor 800+ unmarked graves in the African American Burial Grounds, then at Emory (Oct-Dec 2023) and Decatur's Beacon Hill (from March 2024) ([ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/how-charmaine-minniefields-praise-house-was-nearly-demolished-but-survived/)). This is an artistic revival, not a congregation practicing the shout. The living shout tradition, the **McIntosh County Shouters** (NEA 1993), is on the coast, outside the MSA.
- **Lined hymns.** NEA Fellow **Claude Joseph Johnson** (1987) was born in Douglasville in 1913 to a shape-note singing-school father and a grandmother known for lined hymns. He led his first shape-note song at five, was "the boy preacher from Atlanta", and recorded 20+ Savoy records of unaccompanied hymns and country spirituals with his congregation, in church rather than a studio; "I Wanna Go Where Jesus Is" (1970) sold gold ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/claude-joseph-johnson)). I found **no 2024-26 documentation** of Atlanta congregations still lining hymns, so vitality is unverified (marked niche, low confidence). This is a fieldwork gap, not proof of absence.
- **Gospel quartets.** Atlanta hosts a "Celebration of Quartet" awards show, but quartet anniversaries, the classic community form, barely appear online ([CQs](https://www.celebrationofquartet.com/)). Low confidence.

#### Blues as a community practice
Blind Willie McTell made Atlanta a Piedmont-blues capital. John and Ruby Lomax recorded him for the Library of Congress in an Atlanta hotel room in Nov 1940, Atlantic recorded him in 1949 (*Atlanta Twelve-String*), and he preached at Mt. Zion Baptist in Atlanta in 1957-59 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_Willie_McTell)). Today the community form is the **club jam**: **Lola's Jam, Mondays at 10 pm at Northside Tavern**, honoring singer-keyboardist Lola Gulley, is billed as "one of Atlanta's longest-running musical traditions" (listed Oct 26, 2026) ([CL](https://creativeloafing.com/event-639232-lola-s-jam-at-northside-tavern-10-26-2026-10-00)). The **Atlanta Blues Society** (Keeping the Blues Alive award 2019) runs the Atlanta Blues Challenge (2026 winners announced June 2026) and held a gathering and jam at Blind Willie's on Oct 18, 2026 ([ABS](https://www.atlantabluessociety.org/)). The acoustic 12-string Piedmont style is niche: it lives on as repertoire rather than as a local lineage.

#### Caribbean carnival and steelpan
- The **Atlanta Caribbean Carnival** has run since **1988** on Memorial Day weekend. Its mas parade assembles near Truist Plaza, goes up Peachtree and ends at Rodney Cook Sr. Park in Vine City ([Saporta Report](https://saportareport.com/atlanta-caribbean-carnival-parade-returns-celebrating-culture-and-unity/columnists/adrianne-murchison/adrianne-murchison/)). The **Atlanta Caribbean Carnival Bandleaders Association** (formed 2003; producer since 2005; president Patricia Henry) has already published its 2027 dates: Children's Carnival May 8, J'ouvert May 28, Parade May 29, plus a fete on May 30. The 2027 edition celebrates St. Kitts & Nevis ([ACCBA](https://www.atlantacarnival.org/)). Ramona Condell of *Mas with Panache* is described as "one of the last few designers curating designs locally", a warning sign for local costume-making.
- **Steelpan:** **Pan People Steelband** played the 2026 Pan African Festival. **Nugen Steelpan Academy** (teacher from Trinidad and Tobago) runs graded classes and a community steelband ([Nugen](https://nugensteelpan.com/classes/)).

**Vitality: thriving** (carnival), **active** (pan).

#### Haitian, Ghanaian and other African communities
- **Haitian Flag Day Fest** was held May 18, 2025 in Forest Park (Clayton) with the Haitian Consulate and the Haitian American Chamber ([Clayton](https://seeclaytoncountyga.com/event/haitian-flag-day-fest-2025/)). Haitian dance is taught mostly through the African dance network (Giwayen Mata's 2025 anniversary class; AADF workshops).
- **GhanaFest Atlanta** (Aug 9, 2025, Brookhaven) included a Parade of Chiefs and Queen Mothers ([listing](https://mypassportpay.com/atlanta-ghanafest-2025/)).
- No 2024-26 evidence was found for Igbo New Yam, Yoruba festivals or a public Ethiopian festival with music. Eritrean community presence is evidenced by the Eritrean community center in Stone Mountain that hosted the 2025 World Refugee Day ([285 South](https://285south.com/humidity-tough-times-or-the-world-cup-none-of-it-stopped-community-members-from-showing-up-for-world-refugee-day/)).

### 3. Native American traditions

The Atlanta MSA is on Muscogee (Creek) land, with Cherokee country to the north. Most Muscogee people were forced out in the 1820s-30s, so today's Native heritage scene is mostly **intertribal** and partly organized by non-Native institutions. That is exactly what First Voices set out to change.

- **Native American Festival & Pow Wow, Stone Mountain Park.** Runs **Oct 29-Nov 1, 2026** (happening as this was written). Thursday and Friday are school field-trip days; Saturday and Sunday hold intertribal dance and drum competitions with Grand Entry at 12:30. Billed as the "largest Native American gathering in Georgia" and a Southeast Tourism Society Top 20 Event. Festival-only tickets cost $15.99 ([SMP](https://stonemountainpark.com/activity/events/native-american-festival-and-pow-wow/)). It happens in a park long tied to the Confederate carving, a tension worth noting.
- **Indian Springs Native People Festival & Powwow** (Butts County). The **36th** edition was Sep 5-6, 2026 at the Indian Springs Hotel Museum in Flovilla; admission $5; host: Butts County Historical Society ([listing](https://stayhappening.com/e/36th-annual-indian-springs-native-people-festival-andamp-powwow-E118SB1ABDR9Q); [2025](https://www.festivalsinamerica.com/festival/indian-springs-native-people-festival-indian-springs/2025)). Per the New Georgia Encyclopedia, the McIntosh Inn at Indian Springs, built in 1823 by Muscogee leader William McIntosh, is where he signed the 1825 Treaty of Indian Springs, and he was killed three months later by Muscogee who saw it as betrayal ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/?s=Indian+Springs)).
- **First Voices Festival** (Little Five Points). Started in 2022 by Turtle Island Trading Company (Native-owned; Carmen Halagahu and Yellowbird, Oglala Lakota), 7 Stages and the L5P business association. Halagahu's motive: "most of all the cultural powwows that happened around Atlanta are not run by indigenous people." It is described as the first event to bring a powwow inside Atlanta's city limits. The 2025 program included Muscogee elder **William Harjo** (one of about 500 native Muscogee speakers) serving sofkey. 7 Stages adds a **$1 land-use fee** to every ticket to fund a Mvskoke language program and Native individuals ([ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/first-voices-festival-in-little-five-points-celebrates-4-years/)). The **5th edition runs Nov 20-27, 2026**, with a free powwow on Nov 21-22 at 1136 Austin Ave NE (emcee Buffalo Yellowbird; arena director Jeremy Jones, Eastern Band Cherokee) that includes Danza Azteca ([7 Stages](https://www.7stages.org/shows/first-voices-festival-2026/)).
- **Atlanta Afroindigenous Peoples Festival** (inaugural June 14-15, 2025, by Sequoia Ascension). Founders Umi IMAN and Khadijah Siferllah (the dance duo Al Taw'am) are of Black, Tsalagi, Jamaican and Muslim heritage; the festival included a powwow, workshops and film ([ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/connecting-culture-through-the-art-of-dance-at-the-atlanta-afroindigenous-peoples-festival/)). No 2026 edition was verified.
- **Fading:** Reinhardt University's **Unity Festival** at the Funk Heritage Center in Waleska (Cherokee County), with Appalachian and Cherokee demonstrations, was **canceled in 2025** and has no 2026 date ([powwows.com](https://calendar.powwows.com/events/reinhardt-university-unity-festival/)). Etowah Indian Mounds (Bartow) offers interpretive programs such as atlatl throwing (Oct and Nov 2026), not community festivals.

**Vitality: active.** There are three or four powwows a year in the MSA, but no weekly drum group or Native community center was found in public sources (a gap).

### 4. South Asian traditions

**Garba and raas (Gujarati).** The Gujarati community spread through north Fulton, Forsyth, Gwinnett and Clayton supports both professional "star nights" and temple garba.
- **ATL Garba Festival 2025** at Ambaji USA Shree Shakti Mandir (1450 Huie Rd, Morrow) ran **9 nights over 3 weekends, Aug 29-Sep 14, 2025**. Each night had one headliner: Kinjal Dave, Geeta Rabari, Jignesh Barot, Jigardan Gadhvi, Kirtidan Gadhvi, Aishwarya Majmudar, Umesh Barot, Aditya Gadhvi and Atul Purohit. A season pass was $200, and children under 12 got in free ([indian.community](https://indian.community/atl-garba-festival-2025/)).
- Navratri itself ran **Oct 11-19, 2026**. A ticketing-platform guide claims big nights draw 3,000-8,000 and that people drive in from Tennessee, the Carolinas, Alabama and Florida. Its venue details are partly wrong, so treat its numbers as low confidence ([Rameelo](https://www.rameelo.com/blog/garba-events-atlanta-georgia-guide)).

**Vitality: thriving.** It is participatory: thousands dance rather than watch.

**Carnatic music and Bharatanatyam.**
- **Atlanta Fine Arts**, founded in 2022 by young Carnatic musicians, runs the annual **Thyagaraja Aradhana**, where the community sings the saint-composer's Pancharatna kritis together (Feb 1, 2025, Chinmaya Gurukul, Cumming; free; 360+ reported attendees) ([AFA](https://atlfinearts.com/)).
- **Carnatic Vidyalaya** (Viji Murali; 23 years, 60+ large events) ([site](https://carnaticvidyalaya.com/)).
- **Kruti Dance Academy** (1995): founder Dina Sheth has 35+ years of teaching and 100+ arangetrams, teaching classical, folk and fusion; artistic director Shemoni Parekh ([Kruti](https://www.kruti.com/)).
- Other Bharatanatyam schools include Natya Dhaara, Deeksha, Pavithram and Atlanta Nritya Academy.

**Vitality: thriving.** Every arangetram is a public recital that sums up years of training.

**Not verified:** bhangra teams, Durga Puja music, Bengali or Punjabi folk ensembles (no time to check). The Bhutanese-Nepali community (Clarkston and Stone Mountain) reportedly celebrates Dashain, Tihar and Teej through the Nepalese Association of Georgia and the Bhutanese Community Association of Georgia, but only general descriptions were found (low confidence).

### 5. East and Southeast Asian traditions

- **Korean.**
  - The **Korean Cultural Center** (nonprofit, 2003) has a named samulnori team plus gayageum, haegeum, danso and minyo performers; it offers **samulnori lessons Fridays at 7 pm** (practices Wed and Fri) in Duluth and asks newcomers to call ahead so instruments are ready ([members](https://atlkcc.com/about/members/); [lessons](https://atlkcc.com/contact-us/learn-samulnori/)). The freshest dated item on its site is from 2024.
  - **Korean Festival:** 2025 at Sugarloaf Mills (Oct 4-5; free if you wear hanbok); 2026 at **Gwinnett Place Mall, Sep 19-20**, where organizers expected 100,000+. Gwinnett County cites "nearly 30,000 Korean American residents" and calls itself the "Seoul of the South" ([Gwinnett](https://www.gwinnettcounty.com/-/news-events/stories/story-details/celebrate-culture-community-and-flavor-at-the-2026-korean-festival)).
  - A separate Korean American Association festival page claims a 2009 founding and 35,000 visitors, and calls the Atlanta Korean Community Center the world's largest; the page is internally inconsistent ([KAAGA](https://www.atlkoreanfestival.com/about.html)).
  - At DeKalb Schools' Unity Fest (Mar 2026), a student Korean ensemble played gayageum ([285 South](https://285south.com/at-unity-fest-children-celebrate-their-cultures-both-old-and-new/)).
  - **Vitality: active.**
- **Chinese.** The **Atlanta Chinese Dance Company** was founded in 1991 by Singapore-born Hwee-Eng Y. Lee, who began teaching in 1986 with five students at the old Chinese Community Center in Chamblee. Her daughter Kerry Lee is co-artistic director. In Aug 2026 the company presented *Chinese American Journeys* (ribbon, sword and lantern dances; pieces on suffragist Mabel Ping-Hua Lee and the 2021 spa shootings) ([285 South](https://285south.com/atlanta-chinese-dance-companys-new-show-explores-the-chinese-american-experience-in-the-u-s-south/); [ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/chinatown-memories-a-must-see-dance-performance-for-womens-history-month/)). No Atlanta Chinese instrumental (erhu/guzheng) community orchestra was verified; the Chinese Music Ensemble ArtsATL listed is UGA's, in Athens (outside the MSA). **Vitality: active** (dance), unverified (music).
- **Okinawan and Japanese.** **Mirai Kanai** eisa taiko belongs to the Atlanta Okinawa Kenjin-Kai. It played the 100th and 101st National Cherry Blossom Festivals, the Atlanta Botanical Garden (Aug 2026) and the East by Southeast festival (Oct 2026) ([Mirai Kanai](https://metroatlanta.wixsite.com/miraikanai)). **JapanFest** (founded 1986) ran Sep 19-20, 2026 at the Gas South Convention Center ([JapanFest](https://www.japanfest.org/); [ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/arts-agenda-fall-festivals/)).
- **Vietnamese.** Tet festivals are run by the Vietnamese American Community of Georgia (Korean American Center, Norcross) and the First Senior Center of Georgia (Feb 2026, with lion dance). Attendance claims of 1,200-5,000 are secondhand (low confidence) ([GAT](https://gasiantimes.com/lunar-new-year/2025/01/24/lunar-new-year-2025-calendar-of-celebrations/)). **Vitality: active.**
- **Lao.** A Laotian American Society member played the ranat at 2026 World Refugee Day. **Niche.**
- **Filipino.** Kalayaan Atlanta held a family festival (June 7, 2025, Stone Mountain) and an annual June 12 gala; Makabayan Georgia (2008) estimates 40,000 Filipino Americans in Georgia. Catching Mangoes Dance (founded 2023 by Jacque Pritz) creates Filipino-diaspora dance. **No rondalla found.** **Niche.**
- **Hmong:** nothing found.
- **East by Southeast Festival of the Arts** (Clarkston Community Center, Oct 16-18, 2026) presents Asian-descended Southeastern artists and booked Mirai Kanai and the Atlanta Chinese Dance Company ([CCC](https://clarkstoncommunitycenter.org/east-by-southeast-festival-of-the-arts-brings-three-days-of-creativity-culture-and-community-to-clarkston/)).

### 6. Latin American and Hispanic Caribbean traditions

Metro Atlanta's Latino traditions are strong in **festival form** but thin in **teaching and participatory infrastructure**, at least as visible online.

- **Mexican.**
  - **Día de los Muertos at Oakland Cemetery** (4th year in 2025, free) is co-produced with the Mexican Consulate and Institute of Mexican Culture. It began with 70 children making piñatas. Families build ofrendas under a seven-point checklist, and folklórico and mariachi groups perform ([ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/oakland-cemeterys-annual-dia-de-los-muertos-festival-honors-life-death-and-cultural-connection/)).
  - **Plaza Fiesta** on Buford Highway (DeKalb) says it has been the consulate's official site for **El Grito** for "19 years" and claims crowds over 60,000 (the page is undated; low confidence) ([Plaza Fiesta](https://plazafiesta.net/event/el-grito-fiestas-patrias-mexican-independence-day/)).
  - **Danza Azteca:** Danza Calpulli Ehecatl at Stone Mountain's Latino Family Festival (YouTube, undated) and Danza Azteca at First Voices 2026.
  - The **Latino Family Festival** at Stone Mountain (2025) featured Aztec dancers, a Colombian ballet, mariachi and the **Panamanian Marching Band of Atlanta** ([ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/arts-agenda-world-music-spring-2025/)).
  - **Gaps:** no Atlanta-based youth mariachi program, folklórico academy or **son jarocho fandango** could be verified. One search answer offered a "metro Atlanta" folklórico school that turned out to be in Lemon Grove, California. **Vitality: active, but festival-based.**
- **Puerto Rican.** **SanSe Atlanta 2026**, a Ser Familia benefit at Ameris Bank Amphitheatre in Alpharetta ($125 all-inclusive), calls itself Atlanta's largest Puerto Rican festival and promises "reggaetón to salsa to **plena**" ([Ser Familia](https://serfamilia.org/events/sanse-atlanta-2026/)). **No bomba group or workshop found.** **Niche.**
- **Cuban.** Julian Mejia brought **casino** to Atlanta in 1999 and still teaches it through SALSAtlanta ([ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/julian-mejia-introduced-atlanta-to-cuban-salsa-in-the-90s-and-has-been-teaching-it-ever-since/)). Afro-Cuban/Orisha dance turns up in AADF workshops. **Active.**
- **Brazilian.** **Unidos Atlanta Escola de Samba** runs beginner and intermediate samba, a passista track toward Rio Carnaval and an Oct 31, 2026 "Samba Saturday", and has bateria classes "coming soon" ([Unidos](https://www.unidosatlanta.com/)). Fogo Brasil teaches samba and capoeira. The Brazilian community clusters around Marietta, which hosts a Brazilian festival (dates not verified). **Active.**
- **Colombian, Dominican, Panamanian.** These appear only as festival acts (Colombian ballet; Panamanian marching band). **Niche / unverified.**

### 8. Appalachian and mill-village heritage

Atlanta's best-documented folk-music heritage is the **Appalachian and Piedmont migrant fiddling** of the mill era. From 1913 to 1935 the **Georgia Old-Time Fiddlers' Conventions** filled the old Municipal Auditorium (Courtland and Gilmer streets): two warm-up nights, a Saturday contest and a square dance in Taft Hall. Audiences included "former rural dwellers who had recently migrated to Atlanta in search of employment in the city's textile mills". The conventions launched Fiddlin' John Carson, Gid Tanner, Riley Puckett and Clayton McMichen. Lowe Stokes' 1924 upset of Carson was probably the source for Stephen Vincent Benét's poem "The Mountain Whippoorwill". The conventions ended in 1935 as radio, film and records drew their audiences away ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/georgia-old-time-fiddlers-conventions/)).

Champions with MSA ties:
- **A. A. Gray** of Tallapoosa (Haralson County): 1918, 1921, 1922 and 1929.
- **Anita Sorrells Wheeler** of Atlanta: 1931 and 1934, the only woman champion.

As a *heritage institution* this is **historic_only**. The living old-time and bluegrass scene belongs to the Layer 1 file. Two heritage echoes:
- Cabbagetown's **Chomp & Stomp**, in the former Fulton Bag mill village, is still "bluegrass-focused" ([ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/arts-agenda-fall-festivals/)).
- **Woodstock Arts** (Cherokee County) presents bluegrass as a "gateway between North Georgia Appalachia and metro Atlanta" ([ArtsATL](https://www.artsatl.org/growing-woodstock-arts-offers-a-cultural-gateway-between-north-georgia-appalachia-and-metro-atlanta/)).

### 9. Clarkston and the refugee mosaic

Clarkston (DeKalb) has been one of the country's best-known refugee resettlement towns.
- **World Refugee Day** was held for the 11th time in 2024 (Clarkston Community Center) and the 12th in 2025 (at Stone Mountain's Eritrean community center). The June 2026 edition at Refuge Coffee featured Karen-language singer Dar Nai, Bangladeshi American fusion band Jaago and a young Lao ranat player ([CCC 2024](https://clarkstoncommunitycenter.org/world-refugee-day-celebration-2024/); [285 South 2026](https://285south.com/humidity-tough-times-or-the-world-cup-none-of-it-stopped-community-members-from-showing-up-for-world-refugee-day/)).
- At **DeKalb Schools' Unity Fest** (3rd year, Mar 28, 2026), Indian Creek Elementary (students from 40 countries) and Freedom Middle presented a **Karen dance** and a gayageum ensemble ([285 South](https://285south.com/at-unity-fest-children-celebrate-their-cultures-both-old-and-new/)).
- The Rohingya community revived a soccer tournament in 2026 (sport rather than music, but a sign of community life).

**Vitality: active but under pressure.** The US refugee ban means "pretty much the only new students we see are those who come from other states" (teacher Adam Nykamp). These traditions now depend on the second generation rather than new arrivals.

### 3. Timeline

#### Before 1900: homelands, plantations, shape notes

- **To 1782 and earlier.** The region was Muscogee (Creek) land on the Cherokee borderland. The village of
  Pakanahuili ("Standing Peach Tree") stood where Peachtree Creek meets the Chattahoochee, in today's Buckhead/Paces,
  and first appears in writing in Governor John Martin's letter of May 27, 1782
  ([Georgia Historical Society marker](https://www.georgiahistory.com/ghmi_marker_updated/standing-peach-tree/);
  [Atlanta History Center](https://www.atlantahistorycenter.com/exhibitions/atlanta-in-50-objects/standing-peachtree/)).
  Peachtree Street is named after it.
- **1799.** U.S. agent Benjamin Hawkins records the Creek name behind "Chattahoochee" (marked or "painted" stone).
  Creek land cessions followed in 1821-25 ([River Through Atlanta](https://riverthroughatlanta.com/chattahoochee-river-history/)),
  and the Cherokee were forced out in 1838 ([Trail of Tears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trail_of_Tears)).
  This slice found no documentation of Muscogee or Cherokee musical practice specific to the metro.
- **Antebellum.** Enslaved musicians provided much of the dance music on Georgia plantations. One period account
  describes enslaved people spending the holidays "playing on the violin and other instruments, for their young
  friends to dance by" ([JSTOR, "Good Times on the Old Plantation"](https://www.jstor.org/stable/43610521)). Low
  specificity: no named antebellum Atlanta-area fiddler was found.
- **1844.** *The Sacred Harp* is published by B. F. White of Hamilton (Harris County, outside the MSA) and E. J. King
  of Talbotton ([NGE: Sacred Harp](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/sacred-harp/)).
- **1851-52.** The Chattahoochee Musical Convention is planned at composer Oliver Bradfield's home north of Newnan
  and organized in 1852 at Macedonia Baptist Church in **Coweta County**. It is the oldest surviving Sacred Harp
  convention. B. F. White attended its early sessions
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chattahoochee_Musical_Convention)).
- **1859.** J. P. Reese (born in Jasper County, which is in the MSA) composes the Sacred Harp tune "Newnan" (no. 321)
  ([fasola.org](https://fasola.org/indexes/1991/?p=321)).
- **1865.** The Chattahoochee convention restarts after the war at Mount Zion, Carroll County, and begins keeping
  written minutes, which survive. It has met every year since except 1881
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chattahoochee_Musical_Convention)).
- **1881.** The Fulton Bag and Cotton Mill opens. Cabbagetown grows up around it as a mill village of mostly
  Scots-Irish Appalachian migrants; the mill employed up to 2,600 people and closed in 1977
  ([Wikipedia: Cabbagetown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbagetown,_Atlanta)). This is the audience the fiddlers'
  conventions drew on.

#### 1900-1935: the cradle years

- **1905.** The United Sacred Harp Musical Association ("United Convention") holds its first session in Atlanta
  ([Sacred Harp Publishing Co.](https://sacredharp.com/museum/united/)). It now meets in Bremen every September
  ([Atlanta Sacred Harp](http://atlantasacredharp.org/annual.html)).
- **1909.** The Atlanta City Auditorium (more than 5,000 seats) opens at Courtland and Gilmer
  ([GSU paper](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/bitstreams/d48ad6d6-7c6b-429c-8330-a94efef16d06/download)).
- **1911.** Joseph Stephen James of Douglasville (Douglas County) chairs the revision that leads to the modern
  "Denson" book ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/sacred-harp/)).
- **April 1913.** About 50 fiddlers form the Georgia Old-Time Fiddlers' Association. J. B. Singley is the first champion
  ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/georgia-old-time-fiddlers-conventions/)).
  The master of ceremonies at 14 or more of the conventions was a man billed as "Alec Smart" from The Cove near
  Woodbury in Meriwether County. He wore a silk hat, played melodeon and kept order with a cowbell and a megaphone,
  and his real name is still unknown ([GSU paper](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/bitstreams/d48ad6d6-7c6b-429c-8330-a94efef16d06/download)).
- **1915.** A newspaper estimated that the fiddlers reached 30,000 people that week, playing at the federal prison,
  schools and women's clubs as well as the Auditorium. Carson also sang his "Little Mary Phagan" (about the Leo Frank
  case) on the Capitol steps that year
  ([GSU](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/bitstreams/d48ad6d6-7c6b-429c-8330-a94efef16d06/download);
  [NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/fiddlin-john-carson-ca-1868-1949/)).
- **March 15 or 16, 1922.** WSB ("Welcome South, Brother") goes on air, owned by the *Atlanta Journal*. Carson plays
  about a week later and becomes a national radio personality on its signal
  ([NGE: WSB](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/business-economy/wsb/) gives March 15;
  [NGE: Carson](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/fiddlin-john-carson-ca-1868-1949/) gives March 16).
- **September 18, 1922.** The Home Town Boys (Clayton McMichen, Riley Puckett and others) make their debut on WSB
  ([NGE: McMichen](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/clayton-mcmichen-1900-1970/)).
- **June 1923.** Polk Brockman, who ran the phonograph department of his family's Atlanta furniture store, persuades
  OKeh's Ralph Peer to record Carson's "The Little Old Log Cabin in the Lane" / "The Old Hen Cackled and the Rooster's
  Going to Crow" ([UNC: Brockman](https://exhibits.lib.unc.edu/exhibits/show/hillbilly_music/biographies/polk-c--brockman)).
  Sources disagree on the date: June 14 ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/fiddlin-john-carson-ca-1868-1949/)),
  "on or around June 19" ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/atlanta-prepares-demolish-site-country-musics-first-recorded-hit-180972115/)),
  "June 13 or 14" (blog sources).
- **1924.** Young Lowe Stokes beats Carson for the title, and the *Literary Digest* covers it. Stephen Vincent Benét's
  1925 poem "The Mountain Whippoorwill" likely draws on the coverage
  ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/georgia-old-time-fiddlers-conventions/)).
  In March 1924 Gid Tanner and Riley Puckett record in New York as Columbia's first country artists, and Puckett's
  "Rock All Our Babies to Sleep" is probably the first yodel on a country record
  ([NGE: Puckett](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/riley-puckett-1894-1946/)).
- **1925-1932.** Columbia's A&R man Frank Walker holds regular Atlanta field sessions
  ([Bluegrass Messengers](http://www.bluegrassmessengers.com/frank-walker--a--r-man-for-columbia.aspx)). Atlanta was
  called "the Nashville of its day" ([Goodson, *Atlanta Studies*](https://atlantastudies.org/2017/08/09/steve-goodson-the-nashville-of-its-day-recalling-the-origins-of-recorded-country-music-in-1920s-atlanta/)).
- **April 17, 1926.** First Skillet Lickers session in Atlanta (Tanner, Puckett, Fate Norris, McMichen, Bert Layne)
  ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/gid-tanner-and-his-skillet-lickers/)).
- **November 8, 1926.** Columbia records Peg Leg Howell on Decatur Street's blues scene. "New Prison Blues" is the
  label's first country blues release. From April 8, 1927, "Peg Leg Howell and His Gang" adds the Black fiddler Eddie
  Anthony ([Wikipedia: Howell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peg_Leg_Howell)).
- **1927.** OKeh issues the Scottdale String Band's "Stone Mountain Wobble"; Scottdale is a DeKalb County mill village
  ([TTA snippet](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Stone_Mountain_Wobble)).
  **October 18, 1927:** Blind Willie McTell's first Victor session, in Atlanta. Victor made five trips to Atlanta
  between 1927 and 1929 ([Big Road Blues](https://sundayblues.org/2022/07/24/big-road-blues-show-7-24-22-georgia-stomp-victor-in-atlanta-1927-1929-great-recording-sessions-pt-9/)).
- **October 16, 1928.** The Black fiddle-and-guitar duo Andrew and Jim Baxter (Gordon County, outside the MSA) record
  "Georgia Stomp" for Victor in Atlanta ([recording notes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WtLkxLiKjiU);
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_and_Jim_Baxter)).
- **1930.** Lowe Stokes is shot and loses his right hand
  ([FRC](https://fieldrecorder.org/product/lowe-stokes-georgia-fiddler-frc723/); Wikipedia says "late 1920s").
- **1931 and 1934.** Anita Sorrells Wheeler is state champion.
- **1934.** The last Skillet Lickers session produces "Down Yonder", with Gordon Tanner on lead fiddle. It became the
  band's biggest seller (more than a million copies according to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gid_Tanner))
  ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/gid-tanner-and-his-skillet-lickers/)).
- **1935.** The last convention. Alec Smart blamed radio and phonographs: "The country folks ain't satisfied with the
  simple old fiddle tunes no more... They want this jazz band music"
  ([GSU](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/bitstreams/d48ad6d6-7c6b-429c-8330-a94efef16d06/download)).

#### 1936-1965: radio, field recorders, decline

- **1936.** *The Original Sacred Harp* (Denson revision) ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/sacred-harp/)).
- **1938.** The Chattahoochee convention moves into Wilson Chapel, built for it by Matthew and Ada Ward Wilson near
  Carrollton ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chattahoochee_Musical_Convention)).
- **1940.** John and Ruby Lomax record Blind Willie McTell for two hours in their Atlanta hotel room for the Library of
  Congress and pay him $10 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_Willie_McTell)).
  **November 16, 1940:** the *WSB Barn Dance* debuts from the Biltmore Hotel and runs until 1950. Atlanta "briefly laid
  claim to being the country music capital of the nation"
  ([Hillbilly-Music](https://www.hillbilly-music.com/artists/story/index.php?id=13040);
  [NGE image record](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/country-music-overview/wsb-barn-dance_001/);
  [UPI 1986](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1986/01/17/The-voice-of-the-South/2896506322000/)).
- **1949.** Ahmet Ertegun records McTell on the street for Atlantic (later released as *Atlanta Twelve-String*).
  Carson dies on December 11 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_Willie_McTell)).
- **1950.** The Georgia Mountain Fair begins in Hiawassee, outside the MSA, with 2,000 people
  ([GMF](https://georgiamountainfairgrounds.com/pageserver/history)).
- **1953.** Hugh McGraw is drawn into Sacred Harp. The NEA biography quotes him dating his first singing to 1952
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_McGraw); [NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/hugh-mcgraw)).
- **1956.** Record-store owner Edward Rhodes tapes McTell's *Last Session*.
- **1960.** Gid Tanner dies in Dacula.
- **1963.** George Mitchell (who grew up in Atlanta) and Roger Brown find Peg Leg Howell living in poverty, after
  asking at Shorter's Barber Shop on Decatur Street ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peg_Leg_Howell)).
- **May 24, 1965.** Earl Johnson (born in Gwinnett County, 1926 champion) plays his last show at the Stone Mountain
  Fiddlers' Convention and dies a week later ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_Johnson_(fiddler))).

#### 1965-1991: the folk revival and the institutions that outlived it

- **1965.** Methodist minister Bruce Donnelly starts the Twelfth Gate coffeehouse. It moved to a house on 10th Street
  in 1967 and lasted until 1974 ([Atlanta Folk #21](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-21-lenas-place-coffeehouse)).
  Church coffeehouses followed: the Hermitage at Oakhurst Baptist (1967), Come Together at St. Bede's (1971),
  Common Cup, run by 20 churches (1973), and the Hungry Ear at Northwest Unitarian (1980) (same source).
  The Bistro, at 1102 West Peachtree, hosted Jerry Jeff Walker around 1966-67. Nobody came to Jimmy Buffett's 1971
  New Year's Day gig there ([Atlanta Folk #21](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-21-lenas-place-coffeehouse);
  [Beaufort County Now](https://www.beaufortcountynow.com/post/41936)).
- **1966.** Georgia State University starts a folklore curriculum and Rabun County's Foxfire program begins; before
  then folklore was not an academic subject in Georgia ([NGE: Folklife](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/folklife-overview/)).
- **October 30, 1972.** The Great Southeast Music Hall opens in Broadview (Lindbergh) Plaza, with beer by the bucket.
  Bill Monroe played March 27-April 1, 1973, and the Sex Pistols played their first American show there on
  January 5, 1978 ([Seal Place](https://sealplace.blogspot.com/2014/12/the-great-southeast-music-hall.html)).
  Sources disagree on when it closed (see the data-quality notes).
- **July 30, 1973.** WRFG ("Radio Free Georgia") first broadcasts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WRFG)).
  **October 20, 1973:** first Stone Mountain Highland Games
  ([SMHG Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/StoneMountainHighlandGames/posts/at-900-am-on-october-20-1973-stone-mountain-highland-games-open-the-gates-to-wha/1474710569268536/)).
- **1975.** Scott Russell and Susan Davis answer a *Creative Loafing* ad for international folk dancing at Garden Hills
  Recreation Center and find about 20 people dancing to a turntable. This was the start of the Chattahoochee Country
  Dancers, now Atlanta Contra Dance ([AJC 2025](https://www.ajc.com/arts-entertainment/2025/11/atlanta-contra-dance-celebrates-50-years-of-swinging-your-partner/);

### 4. The Georgia Old-Time Fiddlers' Conventions (1913-1935)

These conventions are the key to Atlanta's fiddle story.

- **Venue.** The Atlanta City (Municipal) Auditorium at Courtland and Gilmer, opened in 1909 with more than 5,000
  seats. The contest night was usually followed by a square dance in Taft Hall (later Veterans Memorial Hall)
  ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/georgia-old-time-fiddlers-conventions/)). Georgia
  State University acquired the building around 1979 and demolished the auditorium itself for a parking deck. The
  front section (lobby, offices and the assembly hall) survives and houses the president's office (Daniel; NGE calls
  it Alumni Hall, Dahlberg Hall in 2013 publicity).
- **Format.** Thursday and Friday exhibition nights with string bands, comedians, dancers, "chanters" and child
  prodigies, then the Saturday contest. Fiddlers mostly came from north Georgia by train, with family and neighbors.
- **Champions** ([NGE](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/georgia-old-time-fiddlers-conventions/)):
  J. B. Singley (1913); Fiddlin' John Carson (1914, 1923, 1927); Shorty Harper (1915, 1916); John Silvey (1917);
  A. A. Gray (1918, 1921, 1922, 1929); F. B. Coupland (1919); R. M. Stanley (1920); Lowe Stokes (1924, 1925); Land
  Norris (banjo, 1925); Earl Johnson (1926); Gid Tanner (1928); Joe Collins (1930); Anita Sorrells Wheeler (1931,
  1934). Sources disagree on Carson's tally: NGE lists three titles, while
  [Play Bluegrass](https://playbluegrass.com/fiddle/players/fiddlin-john-carson/) says seven between 1914 and 1922.
- **The quiet champion.** Ahaz Augusta "A. A." Gray of Tallapoosa, Haralson County, is the most often documented
  winner. In 1934 he said he thought he had won nine times. His tune "Bucking Mule" ("you do a lot of fancy work
  behind the bridge that makes the fiddle bray like a mule") earned him the nickname "Mule" Gray
  ([GSU paper](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/bitstreams/d48ad6d6-7c6b-429c-8330-a94efef16d06/download)).
- **Fiddlers versus song collectors.** Tump Jackson of Towns County mocked a visiting "fancy violinist" who "put down
  little crooked notes in a black book" and then couldn't play a tune himself (same source). Cecil Sharp never
  reached north Georgia in his 1916-18 Appalachian trips, and Georgia had no comprehensive folk-song collection
  ([NGE: Folklife](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/folklife-overview/)).
- **Afterlife.** Fiddle contests moved to Stone Mountain (1960s) and the mountains: "Georgia's Official State
  Fiddlers' Convention" was held during the Georgia Mountain Fall Festival in Hiawassee
  ([2009 press release](https://ww1.prweb.com/prfiles/2009/09/14/2146224/PRESSRELEASEFALLFESTIVAL09.pdf)), and the new
  Georgia State Fiddlers Convention (2023-) is in Blairsville. In 2025 its "Fiddle King" was Jim Wood of
  Shelbyville, Tennessee ([Bluegrass Today](https://bluegrasstoday.com/2025-georgia-state-fiddlers-convention-results/)).
  **No contest has been held in Atlanta itself since 1935, apart from the 2013 GSU demonstration contest.**

### 5. The north Georgia fiddle sound and its repertoire

- **Style.** The Skillet Lickers, Carson and the Georgia Yellow Hammers made Atlanta and north Georgia "an early
  center of old-time string band music, especially the hard-driving fiddle-based style"
  ([Wikiwand: Skillet Lickers](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Skillet_Lickers)). The band's signature was two or three
  fiddles playing in unison or rough harmony (McMichen, Stokes, Bert Layne, Tanner), over Riley Puckett's guitar bass
  runs. McMichen's playing was "jazzy but polished", and he complained that his bandmates were "about thirty years"
  behind the times ([NGE: McMichen](https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/clayton-mcmichen-1900-1970/)).
  Tanner played the clown, with a falsetto-to-bass voice and a claimed repertoire of more than 2,000 songs.
- **Black fiddling was on record too.** Eddie Anthony's "raw alley fiddle" powered Peg Leg Howell's Gang on Decatur
  Street ([Oxford Reference](https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803095416218)), and
  Andrew Baxter recorded "Georgia Stomp" in Atlanta in 1928. Atlanta's 1920s field sessions recorded white hillbilly
  and Black blues and string-band musicians in the same rooms.
- **Tunes associated with Atlanta and its region** (see the JSON for the full list):
  - "Georgia Railroad" (also "Peter Went a-Fishing"), recorded by Gid Tanner on September 11, 1924 (Columbia 15019-D)
    ([Bluegrass Messengers](http://www.bluegrassmessengers.com/georgia-railroad--version-1-gid-tanner.aspx);
    [TTA snippet](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Georgia_Railroad)).
  - "Hell Broke Loose in Georgia", a four-part Skillet Lickers tune sharing three parts with "Streak o' Lean, Streak
    o' Fat" ([oldtimefiddletunes.net](https://www.oldtimefiddletunes.net/tunes/HellBrokeLoose.pdf)).
  - "Stone Mountain Wobble" (also "Stone Mountain Toddle"), Scottdale String Band, OKeh 1927; Scottdale is in DeKalb
    County ([TTA snippet](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Stone_Mountain_Wobble)).
  - "Georgia Wagoner", a version of "Wagoner" recorded by Carson (1926) and by Gid and Gordon Tanner
    ([TTA snippet](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Georgia_Wagoner)).
  - "Georgia Wildcat Breakdown" (first recorded as "McMichen's Breakdown"), McMichen's band theme
    ([TTA snippet](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Georgia_Wildcat_Breakdown)).
  - "Georgia Stomp", Andrew and Jim Baxter, Victor 1928, recorded in Atlanta.
  - "Down Yonder" (1934): not a place name, but the Atlanta Skillet Lickers' million-seller.
  - **Sacred Harp tunes named for MSA places:** "Newnan" (321, J. P. Reese 1859), "Holly Springs" (453, H. N. McGraw
    1935, named for the Bremen church), "Big Creek" (494, Richard DeLong 1986; probably named for Big Creek Primitive
    Baptist in Alpharetta, an inference), "Georgia" (197, T. B. McGraw 1935), "Mount Zion" (88b and 220, the name
    shared with the Carroll County singing site) and "McGraw" (353)
    ([fasola.org 1991 index](https://fasola.org/indexes/1991/?v=title)).
  - **Frank Maloy's county tunes:** "Fulton County Hornpipe" (B-flat), "Cobb County Reel" (D), "Dekalb County
    Breakdown" (D), "Gwinnette County Breakdown" (so spelled), "Clayton County Reel", "Cherokee County Breakdown",
    "Carroll County Hornpipe", "Haralson County Hornpipe", "Coweta County Breakdown" and the rest: 28 of the 29 MSA
    counties ([Slippery-Hill](https://www.slippery-hill.com/content/159-original-georgia-fiddle-tunes-0)).
  - **The Session** (from the local tune dump): only "The Atlanta Schottische" (a barndance), "The Atlanta" (a
    hornpipe), "Atlanta Hornpipe" (an alias of "Batchelder's"), "Georgia's Gift" and "Marching Through Georgia". The
    Irish and Scottish tune world has almost nothing named for Atlanta. "Marietta", "Decatur" and "Chattahoochee"
    returned no fiddle tunes (search results for "Marietta" were hymn tunes by Samuel Holyoke and others, not named
    for the Georgia city).
- **Recording sources for learning the style:** Lowe Stokes's 1981-83 home recordings from Chouteau, Oklahoma, on
  [Slippery-Hill](https://www.slippery-hill.com/collection/lowe-stokes); the County LP *Hell Broke Loose in Georgia:
  Georgia Fiddle Bands 1927-1934* (County 514, 1968); and *Old-Time Fiddle Tunes and Songs from North Georgia*
  (County CD-3509).

### 6. Sacred Harp: the metro's deepest living tradition

Sacred Harp isn't strictly a fiddle topic, but in this metro it is the strongest unbroken folk practice.
- **Weekly and monthly singings** (Atlanta Sacred Harp, compiled by John Plunkett, updated
  [Oct 30, 2026](http://atlantasacredharp.org/monthly.html)): First Thursday, 7-9 pm (moving in November 2026 to Holy
  Trinity Parish's Tisdale Hall in Decatur, singing from the 2025 *Sacred Harp* and *The Eclectic Harmony*); Third
  Monday, 7 pm at "The Barn" behind St. John the Wonderworker in Grant Park; and a monthly "'Teenth Tuesday" in Athens
  (outside the MSA).
- **Annual all-day singings inside the MSA** (2025-26 minutes book, [annual list](http://atlantasacredharp.org/annual.html)):
  Emory University (February), J. L. White "White Book" singing (Decatur, February), Rogers Memorial (Ephesus, Heard
  County, March), Atlanta Sacred Harp Singing (April), State Line Church (Bremen, April), Holly Springs PB Church
  (Bremen, June), Alpharetta June Singing (June), Hopewell PB Church (Ephesus, June), Union Musical Sacred Harp
  Convention (Decatur, June), Mt. Zion Memorial (Carroll County, July), the **Chattahoochee Musical Convention
  (Whitesburg/Wilson Chapel, August 1-2, 2026)**, B. M. Smith & Philip Denney Memorial (Carrollton, August), the
  **United Sacred Harp Musical Association Convention (Bremen, September)**, the B. F. White Memorial (Decatur,
  September, White Book), Poplar Springs PB (Bowdon, September), Albright, Cates, Brown & Sheppard Memorial (Bremen,
  October), Seed and Feed Sacred Harp Singing (Decatur, October), Denney Memorial (Carrollton, October 31), and Holly
  Springs Memorial (Bremen, November 1). That is roughly 19 annual singings in the MSA (high confidence on the list,
  medium on the exact count because venues change). The Georgia State Convention currently meets in Dahlonega,
  outside the MSA.
- **Two rare "White Book" singings in Decatur** (the J. L. White revision, an offshoot of B. F. White's book) are a
  specialty this survey did not expect.
- **The urban revival.** John Plunkett came in through Schola Nova, a choir from Kelly Morris's Seed & Feed theatre,
  and in the early 1990s through Friday-night singings at Big Creek Primitive Baptist in Alpharetta, which was still
  farmland before GA 400 was finished. The older singers there were World War II and Korea veterans who had farmed
  the land Alpharetta now sits on ([Atlanta Folk #20](https://atlantafolk.substack.com/p/atlanta-folk-20-sacred-harp-singing)).
  Plunkett now chairs the Sacred Harp Publishing Company board. McGraw's line: "I wouldn't cross the street to listen
  to this music but I'd cross the country to sing it."
- **Emory.** The annual Emory singing (from 2005) and the 2015 centennial celebration of James's 1911 book took
  place at Cannon Chapel ([SHPC](https://sacredharp.com/2015/05/28/old-strings-on-a-new-harp/)).
  Emory's Rose Library holds the [Union Musical Sacred Harp Singing Convention records](https://archives.libraries.emory.edu/repositories/2/resources/4494).

### 12. Character: what makes Atlanta distinct

- **Origin myth versus civic memory.** Atlanta was the country-recording capital of the 1920s, but it kept no
  convention, no museum and no recording-site landmark (the Georgia Music Hall of Fame was in Macon and closed in 2011).
  A neighborhood chili festival in Cabbagetown does more to remember Carson than the city does.
- **The in-town and suburban split.** The revival scene (contra, AAFFM, Fiddler's Green, Lena's Place, Frank Hamilton
  School, Eddie's Attic, Sacred Harp's weekday singings) sits in the in-town east side: Decatur, Little Five Points,
  Candler Park, Grant Park, Inman Park. The older traditions are rural and exurban: Sacred Harp conventions in
  Carroll, Haralson, Heard and Coweta counties, the Tanner family in Gwinnett, bluegrass in Cherokee County. Irish
  culture has moved north to Roswell, Alpharetta and Brookhaven.
- **The church coffeehouse.** From the Twelfth Gate (1965) to Lena's Place (since 1983) to Fiddler's Green at the First
  Existentialist Congregation, Atlanta's listening rooms have mostly been church fellowship halls.
- **Costs** (sources in the JSON): contra $15 ($7 students). Fiddler's Green cost $2 in 1988 and $3 in 1990-91. Chomp
  & Stomp and Southern Crossroads were free. Lena's Place proceeds go to a different nonprofit each month.

## Surprises

1. The building where the South's first recording session took place (OKeh, June 1923, 152 Nassau St., Fiddlin' John Carson) was demolished in 2019 for a Margaritaville resort, despite a temporary restraining order. Bristol built a museum; Atlanta built a resort.
2. Ralph Peer called Carson's first record 'pluperfect awful'; it sold out within days and eventually sold about half a million.
3. At the 1914 Fiddlers' Convention Carson brought his hound Old Trail to howl along to 'The Mockingbird', and won. The 1913 convention argued over whether straw-beating was allowed; the emcee 'Alec Smart' kept order with a cowbell and a megaphone, and his real name is still unknown.
4. The convention's most frequent champion, A. A. 'Mule' Gray of Tallapoosa (inside today's MSA), is the least remembered; the only woman champion, Anita Sorrells Wheeler (1931, 1934), called Carson 'a big bag o' wind' for opposing women entrants.
5. Lowe Stokes of the Skillet Lickers kept fiddling after losing his bowing hand in 1930, and the band reportedly recorded 'Hell Broke Loose in Georgia' in Atlanta on Black Tuesday, 1929.
6. The Sacred Harp: 2025 Edition, the first revision in 34 years, debuted at the United Convention in Atlanta (Sept 2025) with singers from 35 states and 6 countries; 10,000 copies went out in about six months and the Georgia House passed a resolution honoring the publisher.
7. B.F. White, compiler of The Sacred Harp (1844), is buried under a magnolia in Oakland Cemetery, and singers go there after conventions to sing to him. Decatur is one of the last places anywhere that sings from J.L. White's early-1900s 'White Book'.
8. Carroll County holds the Sacred Harp Publishing Company, the 1852 Chattahoochee Convention and the birthplace of Thomas A. Dorsey, 'father of gospel music'. A separate African American seven-shape 'note singing' tradition (United and Associated Note Singers) meets on a Sunday church circuit in the same western counties, with singers carrying suitcases of books.
9. Frank Hamilton, co-founder of Chicago's Old Town School of Folk Music (1957) and a 1960s Weaver, co-founded the Frank Hamilton School in Decatur in 2015 and was still teaching there in 2026 at about 92; it runs about 45 weekly classes per term, including one on the songs of Decatur's Indigo Girls.
10. Bluegrass jams swamp Irish sessions: thesession.org lists only 4 sessions within 80 km of downtown, against about 40 verified bluegrass jam meetings a month, held in churches, a family barn (Everett's Music Barn, a Norcross duplex rebuilt in a Suwanee cornfield around 1970), a beer garden and a meat-and-three cafeteria (Matthews, Tucker).
11. Modern western square dancing out-meets contra and English dancing roughly three to one; one of the busiest clubs is Hotlanta Squares, an LGBTQ+ club dancing two nights a week.
12. The Tuesday Night Contra runs about half its nights to recorded music and has staged an 'Electro Contra', rare for a mainstream urban series.
13. The 1970s contra boom happened at the 'Existentialist Church' in Candler Park; 50 years later the same congregation hosts English dancing, WaltzPlay and AAFFM's Fiddler's Green concerts. Atlanta contra dancers coached the wedding-contra scene in The Hunger Games: Mockingjay Part 2.
14. Roswell Music Jam's founder called Roswell 'the middle of the jam wasteland' in 2022; his first jam drew 8 people, it now draws about 70 and has spawned church-hosted sister jams in Cumming, Woodstock and Fayetteville.
15. Atlanta's oldest folk concert series run in churches: Lena's Place (since 1983-84) gives each month's door to a different charity, and Fiddler's Green (since 1986) has gone from $3 and a midnight jam to $10 and home by 10.
16. WRFG co-founder Harlon Joye has hosted Fox's Minstrel Show since 1973 and was still on air in 2026; Sagebrush Boogie has run every Thursday since 1983.
17. The Sex Pistols played their first US show (1978) at the Great Southeast Music Hall in a Winn-Dixie strip mall, where Bill Monroe had played in 1973 and Steve Martin played banjo before he was famous.
18. Eddie's Attic's twice-yearly Open Mic Shootout (58 editions) has crowned Jennifer Nettles, John Mayer and Tyler Childers.
19. The St. Patrick's parade (1858) is older than the 1864 burning of Atlanta and reached its 142nd edition in 2026; the Burns Club (1896) meets in an exact 1911 replica of Burns' birthplace cottage and was founded largely through the pharmacist at whose fountain the first Coca-Cola was served.
20. The first Native-led powwow inside city limits came only in 2022 (First Voices, Little Five Points); its theater partner adds a $1 'land use fee' to every ticket to fund Muscogee language work.
21. Atlanta's biggest garba run happens before Navratri: in 2025 a Clayton County temple ran nine nights of top Gujarati folk singers across three late-summer weekends.
22. Every Georgia county has its own composed fiddle tune (Frank Maloy's 159-tune project), including a 'Fulton County Hornpipe' in B-flat.
23. Cecil Sharp never collected in north Georgia, and folklore was not taught at a Georgia university until 1966.
24. No morris, sword or rapper team, Scottish/Cape Breton session, klezmer band, Scandinavian dance or verifiable son jarocho fandango could be found in a 6.5-million metro; the nearest community klezmer band is in Athens.
25. RSCDS Atlanta (50 years old in 2026) dances mostly in the exurbs (Marietta, Cartersville, Lawrenceville) and offers a no-steps 'Walk Scottish Together' class; its anniversary book adds Atlanta-named dances such as 'Sylvia, Priestess of Atlanta'.

## Curiosities (seeds for phase 2)

1. Why does a 6.5-million metro sustain only about 11 Irish session meetings a month (two weekly, both Tuesday)? Compare Irish sessions per million across the 20 metros, against Irish-American share and the presence of a Comhaltas hub.
2. Atlanta was the cradle of commercial old-time music, yet today has fewer dedicated old-time jams than Asheville or Chattanooga. When and why did the lineage break, and did the same happen in other 1920s recording cities (Bristol, Dallas, Charlotte)?
3. Is the church-hosted suburban bluegrass 'Music Jam' franchise (Roswell to Cumming, Woodstock, Fayetteville) a Sun Belt pattern also found around Dallas, Houston and Phoenix?
4. Listing decay: many Southeastern Bluegrass Association jam listings were last edited in 2023. How fast do bluegrass-association jam lists go stale in other cities, and what is the real count?
5. Modern western square-dance club nights outnumber contra nights here. Is that true in every Sun Belt metro, and are LGBTQ+ square-dance clubs aging out more slowly than traditional clubs?
6. Sacred Harp heartland paradox: about 20 annual all-day singings in the MSA but only 2-3 local practice singings a month. Do non-heartland cities (Chicago, Boston, Seattle, Philadelphia) have more weekly urban singings and fewer all-days?
7. Has the 2025 Sacred Harp edition measurably raised attendance? Compare registered singers and leaders in the 2024, 2025 and 2026 Minutes.
8. How many African American seven-shape note singings still meet in 2026, compared with about 52 in 2019, and who is documenting them?
9. Church-fellowship-hall coffeehouses (Lena's Place, Fiddler's Green) are Atlanta's folk backbone, as Quaker and Mennonite meetinghouses are Philadelphia's. Is faith-community infrastructure the common thread of surviving folk series nationally?
10. Recorded-music contra: how common is it elsewhere, and does it draw younger dancers than live-band nights?
11. Metro Atlanta's Hispanic population is large and fast-growing, yet no youth mariachi program, son jarocho fandango or Atlanta-based folklórico school could be verified. Is that gap real, and how does it compare with Houston, Dallas and Charlotte?
12. How many big metros had no Native-led powwow inside city limits until the 2020s?
13. Which other US cities held 1910s-30s municipal fiddle conventions, did they all die around 1935, and why did Georgia's state contest move to the mountains (Hiawassee, Blairsville)?
14. With refugee admissions halted in 2025-26, will Clarkston's Karen, Bhutanese-Nepali and Congolese performance traditions thin out within a decade?
15. Are WRFG's continuous roots shows (Fox's Minstrel Show since 1973) the longest-running folk radio hosts in any top-20 metro?

- **Stone Mountain Wobble (aka Stone Mountain Toddle)** (old-time country rag): Scottdale String Band (Scottdale, DeKalb County), OKeh 1927; named for Stone Mountain, DeKalb County. [link](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Stone_Mountain_Wobble)

- **Georgia Wildcat Breakdown (McMichen's Breakdown)** (old-time / swing): Clayton McMichen's band signature; Skillet Lickers recorded it in Atlanta on October 24, 1931. [link](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Georgia_Wildcat_Breakdown)

- **Bucking Mule** (old-time): A. A. Gray of Tallapoosa's contest-winning tune ("makes the fiddle bray like a mule"). [link](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/bitstreams/d48ad6d6-7c6b-429c-8330-a94efef16d06/download)
