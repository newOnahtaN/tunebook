# Philadelphia-Camden-Wilmington (PA-NJ-DE-MD): the folk scene, October 2026

*Phase-1 dossier for the "Folk scenes of the top 20 metros" project. MSA population 6,329,118 (2025). Counties: Bucks, Chester, Delaware, Montgomery, Philadelphia (PA); Burlington, Camden, Gloucester, Salem (NJ); New Castle (DE); Cecil (MD). Items just outside (Lehigh Valley, Lancaster, Princeton/Trenton, Reading) are flagged `outside_msa`. Structured data: `philadelphia.json`.*

## Scene in a nutshell

Philadelphia's folk scene is old, layered and neighborhood-bound. Its trad heart is Mt. Airy, where an Irish-American social club, the Irish Center, hosts the weekly Thursday contra, set dancing, international folk dance, sessions and a singers' night, with the Main Line, Swarthmore and the 1900 utopian village of Arden, Delaware, as satellites. About 117 verified series meet roughly 200 times a month inside the MSA: about 32 Irish session meetings, 31 céilí, set and Scottish country dance nights, 22 international and Israeli dances, 16 bluegrass jams, 14 English dances and 11 contras, but only 6 old-time jams. Around them sit the Philadelphia Folksong Society (1957) and its Folk Festival (1962), which nearly died in 2023; WXPN's Folk Show (since 1962); a Sacred Harp revival in the city that printed the first Sacred Harp (1844); the Mummers' banjo-and-saxophone string bands; and living bomba, son jarocho, Cambodian, Ukrainian and Black gospel traditions. The New Jersey suburbs are almost blank.

## The 10 most interesting things

1. **An Irish social club is the hub of everything.** The Irish Center (Commodore John Barry Arts & Cultural Center) in Mt. Airy, a 1905 auto-club building that was the first home of the Germantown Jewish Centre and was bought by Irish societies in 1958, hosts the weekly Thursday contra, weekly international folk dancing, weekly set dancing, a monthly session, a children's session, a free monthly céilí and a singers' session. The community raised $82,000 to save it in 2014. ZIP code 19119 carries about 24 recurring trad events a month. ([Irish Center history](https://theirishcenter.org/irish-center-history/), [Thursday contra](https://www.thursdaycontra.com/))
2. **The city that printed the first Sacred Harp sings from it again.** John Connelly designed the four shape notes in Philadelphia in 1798, and T. K. & P. G. Collins printed the first Sacred Harp (1844) near where the Liberty Bell now hangs. The practice returned only in the late 1980s; today there are two West Philly singings a month at the DuBois-Robeson People's Center, a Germantown Mennonite singing, a Brandywine Valley singing, and local singers co-edit their own book, *The Shenandoah Harmony*. ([Philly Sacred Harp](https://phillysacredharp.org/events/))
3. **The Mummers are a living, contested folk orchestra.** Fourteen all-amateur string bands play banjos and saxophones (no brass) on Broad Street every New Year's Day. Their anthem "Oh, Dem Golden Slippers", now a bluegrass fiddle standard, was written by Black songwriter James Bland; Black clubs marched from 1901 until discrimination drove them out by 1929, and blackface was banned only in 1963. Even a morris side (Kingsessing, since 1977) marches. ([Mummers Museum](https://www.mummersmuseum.org/about/))
4. **Delaware built bluegrass and old-time infrastructure for the whole Northeast.** The all-volunteer Brandywine Friends of Old Time Music (1971) started the Delaware Valley Bluegrass Festival (1972) at the request of Bill Monroe and Ralph Stanley, then founded the Brandywine Mountain Music Convention (1974-93) when old-time bands were rebuffed. Today it fiscally sponsors Slippery-Hill, the fiddle world's source-recording archive. ([BFOTM](https://brandywinefriends.org/), [DVBF history](https://delawarevalleybluegrass.org/history/))
5. **An Appalachian music belt sat inside the MSA.** NEA Heritage Fellow Ola Belle Reed ran New River Ranch (1951-58) in Cecil County, Maryland; Sunset Park in Chester County hosted Hank Williams, Patsy Cline and Dolly Parton from 1940 to 1995; autoharp virtuoso Kilby Snow lived in Landenberg.
6. **Irish-American composing royalty were working men.** Ed Reavy, perhaps the greatest Irish-American tune composer, was a Philadelphia plumber; his Victor 78s were cut across the river in Camden on 25 April 1928. Mick Moloney (NEA Fellow 1999), who came to Penn in 1973, became the revival's central organizer, and the Philadelphia Céilí Group's festival (1975) claims to be the first non-competitive presentation of Irish music, song and dance in the US.
7. **The Folk Festival nearly died, and the radio show lost its host.** The Philadelphia Folk Festival (1962) missed 2023 for the first time with the society down to about $30,000 and the board discussing bankruptcy; it returned in 2024 under an all-volunteer working board. WXPN's Folk Show (since 1962) has had no permanent host since 13 September 2026.
8. **Quaker and Mennonite meetinghouses are the folk venues.** Wrightstown and Abington Friends, Perkasie and Doylestown Mennonite, and Germantown Mennonite host the song society, coffeehouses and Sacred Harp; Arden, Delaware, a single-tax utopian village (1900), runs contra, Israeli, international and Scottish-English dancing nearly every week in its Gild Hall.
9. **Scottish country dance is huge; Scottish fiddling is absent.** Four weekly RSCDS classes, a monthly social, an 847-member Meetup and a 2026 Pride Ball, but no Scottish or Cape Breton fiddle session anywhere.
10. **The heritage layer is deep and surprising.** Wilmington's Big August Quarterly (1814) is described as the oldest Black festival still running; Lucia Fest at Old Swedes' Church turned 88 in 2025; a queer- and trans-led son jarocho circle meets weekly in South Philly; and only three trained Cambodian "monkey role" dancers live in the US, one of them in Philadelphia.

## Character: what makes Philadelphia different

- **Old, institutional and neighborhood-bound.** Philadelphia's folk life is anchored in long-lived institutions (Philadelphia Folksong Society 1957, Folk Festival 1962, Germantown Country Dancers, Kingsessing Morris 1977, Philadelphia Céilí Group 1958 (festival 1975), Lansdowne Folk Club 1993) and in a few neighborhoods: Mt. Airy/Germantown/Chestnut Hill, West Philadelphia's Baltimore Avenue, the Main Line and Swarthmore, and Arden and Newark in Delaware. Old City and Center City pubs (the Plough & the Stars, Fergie's) carry the Irish sessions.
- **Irish and dance first, old-time second.** The metro's signature is Irish: about 32 session meetings a month, plus céilí and set dancing. Contra and English country dancing are strong and long-running; Scottish country dancing is unusually big. Old-time is small (six jam meetings a month, in a restaurant, a taproom, breweries and a community center), and bluegrass is suburban (Havertown, Jenkintown, Hilltown, Doylestown).
- **A New Jersey gap.** The four New Jersey counties (about 1.3 million people) have no regular contra and only a monthly session in Gloucester City, a monthly céilí in Palmyra and a members-only jam in Medford, though Salem County hosts the region's biggest bluegrass festival.
- **Who shows up.** Contra and dance crowds skew educated, progressive and mixed-age, with active youth programs (teen and family barn dances, under-30 pricing, Larks/Robins role terms adopted by vote). International and Israeli dancing has shifted to weekday daytimes, a sign of a retiree base. Irish sessions span generations, with a dedicated children's "Next Generation" learning session at the Irish Center.
- **Faith-community buildings and volunteer societies hold it together**, while staffed institutions (PFS with an executive director and building, the Philadelphia Folklore Project, the 2018-2023 Philadelphia Folk School) proved fragile in 2015-2025.

## A visiting fiddler's week

- **Sunday:** Plough & the Stars session 4-8 pm (Old City); or the Next Generation session and free Céilí Óg at the Irish Center (2nd Sundays); John & Peter's in New Hope (2nd/4th); Fergie's ballad session at 7.
- **Monday:** Sligo Pub session in Media; Kingsessing or Renegade morris practice; Philly Pub Sing (1st Monday) at Fergie's.
- **Tuesday:** Plough & the Stars 7-10 pm; bluegrass at the Kelly Center, Havertown (jam 1st/3rd, slow jam 2nd/4th); Park Avenue Contra in Swarthmore (2nd/4th; bring a fiddle to the 2nd-Tuesday open band); set dancing and international folk dance at the Irish Center.
- **Wednesday:** Fainting Goat session in Glenolden; West Philly old-time jam at Gojjo (2nd/4th) or Mt. Airy Tap Room (3rd); English country dance in Merion.
- **Thursday:** the Thursday Night Contra at the Irish Center; Holmesburg bluegrass jam in Jenkintown; 2nd Thursdays add sessions at Lost Time Brewing and the Kelly Center and the Irish Center singers' session; 2nd and 4th Thursdays bring Sacred Harp in West Philly.
- **Saturday:** Fergie's session 4-7 pm, then a contra (3rd Saturday in Mt. Airy, 4th in Center City), the West Philly Square Dance (2nd) or the Scottish social (2nd); Germantown Sacred Harp (2nd Saturday).
- **Annual must-dos:** the Philadelphia Folk Festival (August, Schwenksville), the Delaware Valley Bluegrass Festival (Labor Day, Salem County NJ), the Brandywine Revival of Old Time Music (June), the Old Fiddlers' Picnic (August, Chester County, since 1930), the Céilí Group festival (autumn) and the Mummers Parade (January 1).

### Geography: Mt. Airy, the Main Line, and a New Jersey gap

- **Mt. Airy (19119)** is the capital. The Irish Center at 6815 Emlen St, Summit Presbyterian Church a few blocks away (Third Saturday contra, Germantown Country Dancers' First Saturdays, the family barn dance, the teen dance) and the Mt. Airy Tap Room old-time jam together carry about 24 events a month. The Carpenter SEPTA regional rail station is a short walk from the Irish Center ([FDC](http://folkdancecouncil.org/lloyd-hall-commodore-barry.html)).
- **Main Line / inner Montgomery County**: Merion Friends Meeting (GCD's Wednesday ECD, Kingsessing Morris practice), the Kaiserman JCC in Wynnewood (klezmer, Israeli), Bryn Mawr (Renegade Morris, a college Scottish club), Fort Washington (Mostly Waltz, Vintage Israeli), Jenkintown (Holmesburg bluegrass jam), Elkins Park (Israeli dancing at Beth Sholom).
- **Delaware County**: Swarthmore's Park Avenue Community Center (contra twice a month, Gentle English, old-time jam), Havertown's Kelly Center (bluegrass every Tuesday, Irish session), Media and Glenolden pubs (weekly Irish sessions), Clifton Heights (Scottish class).
- **Wilmington / New Castle County**: Arden's Gild Hall (contra, Israeli, international, Scottish-English almost weekly), the New Castle County Irish Society hall (weekly céilí and set classes), Kelly's Logan House (new 2026 session), and Newark (Scottish class; bluegrass jam listings that could not be re-confirmed).
- **New Jersey side** (Burlington, Camden, Gloucester, Salem): a 4th-Friday session at Holy City Publick House in Gloucester City, a 1st-Friday céilí at the Palmyra Knights of Columbus, and the members-only South Jersey Acoustic Roots Music Society in Medford. A targeted search found no regular contra series in Haddonfield, Moorestown, Medford or Mullica Hill. Yet Salem County hosts the region's biggest bluegrass event, the Delaware Valley Bluegrass Festival.
- **Cecil County, MD**: nothing current found in Layer 1, though historically the Rising Sun area was home to Ola Belle Reed, a fixture of the Brandywine Mountain Music Convention line-ups ([BFOTM](https://delawarevalleybluegrass.org/brandywine-mountain-music-convention/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ola_Belle_Reed)).

Inside the MSA that is only 6 old-time jam slots a month, against 16 for bluegrass and 32 for Irish. The old-time scene leans on its neighbors: Lancaster (weekly Tuesday jam at West Art, 3rd Sundays at Tellus360), Bethlehem (Godfrey Daniels 1st Tuesday), Old Zionsville (3rd Tuesday) and Milford NJ (4th Wednesday). Dead or gone: the Mermaid Inn jam, the Main Line jam at the Gryphon in Wayne (2017-2018).

The region's old-time history runs deeper than its jam count. The Brandywine Friends of Old Time Music (1971) ran the **Brandywine Mountain Music Convention** from 1974 to 1993, with themed years ("Music of Galax" 1974, "Music of West Virginia" 1975, "Music of French America" 1977 with the Balfa Brothers and Louis Boudreault, "Women of Old Time Music" 1980 with Elizabeth Cotten and Rose Maddox) and performers like Tommy Jarrell, Fred Cockerham, Kilby Snow and the New Lost City Ramblers ([performer list](https://delawarevalleybluegrass.org/brandywine-mountain-music-convention/)). Its spirit continues in the **Brandywine Revival of Old Time Music**, a camping and jamming festival near West Chester (June 19-21, 2026, $50 a weekend; [site](https://brandywinerevival.com/)).

### Contra and square dancing

**PATMAD Thursday Night Contra** is the anchor: every Thursday (except Thanksgiving and Christmas Eve) in the Irish Center ballroom, beginner lesson 7:30, dance 8-10:30, suggested $10/$15/$20+ ([thursdaycontra.com](https://www.thursdaycontra.com/)). The October-November 2026 schedule shows the range: touring bands (Countercurrent with Brian Lindsay; Torrent with Will Mentor calling), Mid-Atlantic regulars (the Lava Lamps with Dave Wiesler), a big local "Spootiskerry Skeletons" band for Halloween, and the open band **SPUDS** about seven times a year.

History, pieced together from a SPUDS anthology (*Tuneadelphia*, 2008) and Wayback snapshots:
- In the early 1970s, English country dance leader **Hanny Budnick**, short of recordings, recruited musicians from her dancers; they became **The Fine Companions**, the house band. Squares came in via Lane Neubauer and International House callers such as John Krumm. By the mid-1970s the contra dancers had split from the English dancers and moved from Germantown Friends School to the Water Tower, St. Martin-in-the-Fields and then **Summit Presbyterian Church**. In the late 1970s the Thursday dancers formed a pick-up band: **SPUDS = Summit Pick Up Dance Society** ([SPUDS PDF](https://spuds.thursdaycontra.com/Excerpt_SPUDS_Tuneadelphia.pdf)). The PDF's "7" digits are missing ("early 0's", "late 190's"); the 1970s reading is my inference, consistent with a reference to the Fox Hollow festival, which ended in 1980.
- The dance then spent "nearly 20 years" at **Glenside Memorial Hall** in Cheltenham Township (2014 admission: $8, $5 students), and after a run of "location alerts" moved to the **Commodore Barry Club** in early 2015 ([Wayback 2014](http://web.archive.org/web/20140629081152/http://www.thursdaycontra.com/), [Wayback 2015](http://web.archive.org/web/20150506065751/http://www.thursdaycontra.com:80/)). Its Facebook group is still called "glensidecontra".
- **PATMAD** (Philadelphia Area Traditional Music and Dance) incorporated on Jan 1, 2007, approved at a community meeting at Glenside Memorial Hall on Dec 6, 2006; before that the Thursday Night Dance Committee ran things ([PATMAD info](https://www.thursdaycontra.com/patmad/)).
- COVID: online Saturday dances from 2020, a first outdoor in-person dance on Nov 6, 2021 ("over a hundred dancers"), biweekly indoor dances, then weekly again with mask-optional nights from September 2023. Members voted (101 votes) for **Larks and Robins** in July 2023. In December 2024 the board said attendance was "gradually growing back to pre-COVID numbers" ([news](https://www.thursdaycontra.com/news/)).

### 1. Folk clubs and societies

#### Philadelphia Folksong Society (PFS), founded 1957

- **Origins.** PFS grew out of the Sunday sings at **The Gilded Cage**, Esther and Ed Halpern's coffeehouse on S. 21st St off Rittenhouse Square (1956-1969) ([pfs.org](https://pfs.org/)). Founders included George Britton, Mike Marmel and Joe Aronson ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Folksong_Society)). In the 1960s, festival proceeds helped fund Penn's new folklore and folklife department.
- **Programs over the years:**
  - Philadelphia Folk Festival (1962-)
  - Spring Thing camping weekend (since May 1976)
  - Fall Fling (since Sept 1977)
  - Cabin Fever (February)
  - Heartwood one-day festival (2015)
  - Odyssey of American Music school program (1974)

- **Homes.** Mt. Airy for decades, then an old church at 6156 Ridge Ave in Roxborough from 2018, which was its first dedicated performance space. It gave that lease up in the 2023 crisis ([PhillyVoice](https://www.phillyvoice.com/philadelphia-folk-festival-2023-canceled-folksong-society/)).
- **Membership** (confidence medium; sources differ):
  - "3,000+" in 2014 (Wikipedia citing SonicBids).
  - "over a thousand" on the membership page in 2026, which also promises a monthly newsletter (*Tune Up!*), festival discounts and "a free monthly member concert at the Keswick Theatre in Glenside" ([pfs.org/membership](https://pfs.org/membership/)). No dated Keswick member concert was found.

### 5. Traditional singing

#### Sacred Harp and shape-note

**Philadelphia's historic role.** The Collins firm printed four editions of *The Sacred Harp* (1844, 1850, 1859, 1870), *The Southern Harmony* (1838 onward), and the seven-shape *Christian Minstrel*. E. W. Miller's press at 1102-1104 Sansom St printed *Southern Harmony* revisions and Walker's *Christian Harmony* (1867); 24 of Miller's 40 workers were women ([Rachel Hall](https://sacredharp.com/2017/09/01/philadelphia-birthplace-of-the-shapes-and-center-of-shape-note-publishing/)).

**The revival.** Shape-note singing arrived in southeastern Pennsylvania in the late 1980s. For about nine years the Thursday singing met at the **A-Space** anarchist community center on Baltimore Ave: "loud, raw... better fitting a frontier camp meeting... or a punk show." Writing in 2018, Rachel Hall counted about a dozen regular singings a month within two hours of Philadelphia, five annual all-day singings and a two-day convention. Her line on who comes: "pierced and tattooed city folk rubbing shoulders with plain-dressed Anabaptists" ([Smithsonian Folklife](https://web.archive.org/web/20241221035836/https://folklife.si.edu/magazine/new-generation-of-shape-note-singers-in-philadelphia)).

The 2027 convention goes to Lancaster County ([Keystone](https://phillysacredharp.org/keystone-convention/)).
- **Brandywine Cooper Book All-Day Singing**, Oct 10, 2026.

**Cost:** free, always. "There should never be a charge to attend."

#### Pub sings, shanties and carols

**Philly Pub Sing.** Ben Fink, a classically trained baritone and organizer, moved to Philadelphia in 2021 and couldn't find a pub sing. He started one at Doobies Bar in 2023; it settled upstairs at **Fergie's Pub** (1214 Sansom) in 2024.

- **When:** first Mondays and third Sundays, 5:30-8:00 pm ([Bandcamp bio](https://phillypubsing.bandcamp.com/)). The May 2025 Inquirer piece still described it as first Mondays only.
- **What:** no lyric sheets, no mics, no conductor. Shanties, labor songs, Civil War songs, "Hal-An-Tow", "Rank Strangers", Chumbawamba, "9 to 5". Fergus Carey's verdict: "Singing will save us!" ([Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/entertainment/music/philly-pub-sing-fergies-20250503.html)).
- **Recordings:** sessions are released on Bandcamp. The October 5, 2026 set ran from "Agamemnon" to "Johnny Come Down to Hilo".

**Shanties.** No standalone recurring chantey sing was found in the MSA. The Independence Seaport Museum's site has no shanty or chantey events. The Pub Sing absorbs that energy. The DC-based Ship's Company Chanteymen (pub sings since 1996) appeared at the Seaport Museum's first **Drink the Delaware** festival on March 28, 2026, and at the 2nd Lewes Shanty Fest in Delaware (outside the MSA) ([Ship's Company](https://www.shipscompanyhistory.org/chanteymen)).

**Christmas.** The Philly Pub Carol Sing (since 2023) draws on the Sheffield village-carol, West Gallery, shape-note and gospel traditions. The Royal Heritage Society's holiday wassail at Ormiston Mansion ends in parlor carol singing ([Global Philadelphia](https://globalphiladelphia.org/events/ormiston-mansions-annual-holiday-wassail)), but no dated 2024-26 listing was found. No Revels-style Christmas production was found.

**Mummers (song aspect only).** The parade's theme song, "Oh, Dem Golden Slippers" (James A. Bland, 1879), is also a bluegrass instrumental standard. Bland died in Philadelphia in 1911; a Pennsylvania historical marker (1961) stands at Merion Memorial Park ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Bland)). String bands and clubs are left to the heritage agent.

#### Gospel quartet singing

The **Dixie Hummingbirds** formed in 1928 in Greenville SC and moved to Philadelphia in 1942. They influenced James Brown, the Temptations and Stevie Wonder, won a Grammy for their own "Love Me Like a Rock", and received a 2000 NEA National Heritage Fellowship ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/dixie-hummingbirds)). In the 1940s they performed simultaneously as the Dixie Hummingbirds (in churches), the Swanee Quintet (in Philadelphia) and the Jericho Quintet (at Café Society) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dixie_Hummingbirds)). They have a Pennsylvania historical marker in Philadelphia and remain active with a younger lineup.

The living layer of community quartet "anniversary" programs in Black churches is real but was not verifiable online for 2024-26 (see data-quality notes).

#### Ballad and unaccompanied groups

No dedicated ballad-swap or unaccompanied traditional-song club was found in the MSA, apart from the Pub Sing and the shape-note groups. The 1960s Pennywhistlers (Ethel Raim's Eastern European women's harmony group) are remembered singing at the Gilded Cage, but they were a New York group ([Mudcat](https://mudcat.org/thread.cfm?threadid=55668)).

### 8. Character and a visiting fiddler's week

The scene is **geographically split**:

- **Wednesday:** the PFS/PASA Zoom open mic (3rd Wednesday).

### 1. The big picture

Philadelphia's living heritage scene has three unusual features:

1. **A city-sanctioned, competitive, amateur folk parade with its own instrumental tradition.** The Mummers Parade (official since 1 Jan 1901) is called "the longest-running continuous folk parade in the United States" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade); [Mummers Museum](https://www.mummersmuseum.org/about/)). Its String Band division is a genuinely distinctive local music: unamplified banjos, saxophones (alto to bass), accordions, double basses, glockenspiels and drums, no brass ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)). About 10,000 people take part each year ([Mummers Museum](https://www.mummersmuseum.org/about/)).
2. **An independent public-folklore nonprofit with four decades of fieldwork.** The [Philadelphia Folklore Project](https://folkloreproject.org/) (founded 1987 by Debora Kodish) has documented and funded community artists (African American, Cambodian, Hmong, Liberian, Mexican, Indonesian...). Its 75,000-item archive moved to Temple University's Special Collections Research Center in 2025, opening to the public in 2026 ([AFS](https://americanfolkloresociety.org/philadelphia-folklore-project-transfers-its-archive-to-temple-university/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Folklore_Project)).
3. **Layers of very old colonial-era ethnic institutions still holding annual rituals**, alongside young post-1980 diaspora groups: the Welsh Society (1729), Gloria Dei (Old Swedes') Church's Lucia Fest (88th year in 2025), Wilmington's Big August Quarterly (since 1814), next to a son jarocho collective (~2014), a bomba/plena cultural center (2024/25) and an Indonesian festival (2023).

The metro's heritage geography: South Philadelphia (Mummers clubhouses on "Two Street", Italian Market, Mexican 9th Street, Cambodian/Vietnamese/Indonesian communities, Lebanese St. Maron's, Swedish museum); North Philadelphia/Kensington (Puerto Rican "Centro de Oro": Taller Puertorriqueño, Feria del Barrio, bomba center); West and Southwest Philadelphia (African drum and dance, Liberian community); Chinatown (lion dance, Chinese opera); the northern suburbs (Ukrainian Jenkintown/Horsham, Armenian Cheltenham/Roxborough, Greek Upper Darby, Polish Doylestown); Chester County (Kennett Square Mexican community; the vanished Appalachian music parks); Wilmington (August Quarterly, Italian and Greek festivals).

### 2. The Mummers (thriving, contested)

**What it is.** Clubs ("New Year's Associations/Brigades") compete in five divisions: Comics, Wench Brigades, Fancies, String Bands and Fancy Brigades ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade); [Mummers Museum](https://www.mummersmuseum.org/about/)). Clubhouses cluster on and around South 2nd Street ("Two Street") in Pennsport and Whitman; the museum notes strong Irish, German, Italian, Polish and Roman Catholic ties, with longshoremen and firefighters prominent ([Mummers Museum](https://www.mummersmuseum.org/about/)).

**Club counts (2026).** From the [official 2026 results](https://www.philadelphiamummersparade.com/2026-results/): 14 string bands (Aqua, Avalon, Duffy, Durning, Ferko, Fralinger, Greater Kensington, Hegeman, Jersey, Polish American, Quaker City, South Philadelphia, Uptown, Woodland); 10 wench brigades placed (Americans, Oregon, Bryson, MGK, Cara Liom, Riverfront, Pirates, O'Malley, Saints, Froggy Carr); one Fancy club (Golden Sunrise); comic brigades organized under three mother clubs (Rich Porco's Murray, Goodtimers, Landi), 20 brigades placed; Wikipedia lists 10 Fancy Brigades (Avenuers, Clevemore, Downtowners, Golden Crown, Jokers, Satin Slipper, Saturnalian, Spartans, Shooting Stars, South Philly Vikings) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)). Confidence: high for string bands, medium for the brigade totals.

**2026 parade.** Severe weather on 1 Jan 2026 postponed the judged string band competition; it was held on 31 Jan 2026 at Lincoln Financial Field, where only a small part of the stadium was filled and admission was charged ([Delaware Valley News](https://delawarevalleynews.com/2026/01/31/official-string-band-results/)). Quaker City won with "The Joker's Funhouse"; South Philadelphia 2nd, Fralinger 3rd; Avalon and Ferko withdrew ([official results](https://www.philadelphiamummersparade.com/2026-results/)). The Hegeman String Band's captain was Kelliann Gallagher, notable given women were not officially allowed in the parade until the 1970s ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)).

**Earlier tie.** The string band association reported the first tie for first prize in "modern String Band history" (South Philadelphia and Quaker City) and disqualifications of Duffy and Durning over theme rules; the statement refers to the "124th Mummers parade" though the page is titled 2025 results ([PMSBA](https://www.pmsba.org/2025-string-band-divison-results/)). Year attribution is ambiguous (see data-quality notes).

**Deep roots.** Swedish and Finnish colonists brought "Second Day Christmas" neighbor-visiting to Tinicum (Delaware County); English mummers' plays, gunfire on New Year's and the Pennsylvania German belsnickel custom merged into it. The "speech director" rhyme "Here we stand before your door, / As we stood the year before; / Give us whiskey; give us gin, / Open the door and let us in" survives ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)). The earliest documented club, the Chain Gang, dates to 1840; the first string band, Trilby, organized in 1898, first paraded in 1902 and last paraded in 2016 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)). The theme song is James Bland's "Oh, Dem Golden Slippers" (1879; introduced to the parade in 1903) and the strut descends from the cakewalk ([Mummers Museum](https://www.mummersmuseum.org/about/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)). The parade was cancelled only in 1919 (flu), 1934 (Depression) and 2021 (COVID) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)).

**Race, gender and controversy.** The all-Black Golden Eagle Club (formed 1866) had 300 members in the 1906 parade; judges discriminated against Black clubs and the last, the Octavius Catto Club, withdrew after a last-place finish in 1929 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)). Blackface was banned by city policy in 1964 after a reversed 1963 ban; incidents continued (2013 Ferko "Bringing Back the Minstrel Days"; 2015-16 blackface/brownface; 2016 anti-trans skit; two Mummers banned in 2020 and Mayor Kenney threatened to end the parade) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)). In 1996 half of string bands had female members, which captains attributed to declining membership ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)). Since 2010 there has been no prize money; members pay dues and fund costumes through "beef and beers", gigs and loans ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)).

**Mummers Museum.** Opened 1976 for the Bicentennial at 1100 S. 2nd St ("New Year's Shooters and Mummers Museum"), with costumes, oral histories and a strut exhibit ([Mummers Museum](https://www.mummersmuseum.org/about/)); currently open Wed-Sat 10-3, donation-based ([Mummers Museum visitor page via search snippet](https://www.mummersmuseum.org/visitors/)). The Library of Congress preservation blog featured a 2025 volunteer collections project there ([LOC blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/preservation/2025/03/mummers/)). A 2026 "Winners Circle" display is on its site.

**Vitality: thriving** as a community institution (14 bands, ~10,000 participants, year-round clubhouse life), with signs of strain (two string bands withdrawing in 2026, Trilby's demise, membership worries since the 1990s). For a fiddler: violins are permitted but only "occasionally" used; the soul of the sound is the 4-string banjo and sax section.

### 4. Mexican: son jarocho, mariachi, danza (active)

- **Son Revoltura** (son jarocho collective), co-founded by Ximena Violante and Yared Portillo about a decade before 2025. A 2014 Leeway Foundation grant bought jaranas and brought veteran jaraneros to teach instrument-building. **Free weekly meetups every Tuesday** at the Washington Avenue office of the immigrant-rights group Juntos; leadership is largely queer and trans; they write new verses for protests, including a "La Bamba" against the proposed Chinatown arena in 2024. They played West Philly Porchfest (31 May 2025) and led a fandango at the Mann Center (4 June 2025) ([Broad Street Review, May 2025](https://www.broadstreetreview.com/features/music-collective-son-revoltura-brings-mexican-folk-traditions-to-the-people-of-philly); [Facebook group "Philly son jarocho"](https://www.facebook.com/groups/phillysonjarocho/)). This is the metro's best-documented weekly participatory Latin folk session.
- **Día de los Muertos on South 9th Street** (Association of Mexican Business Owners of Philadelphia, AEM): 2 Nov 2025, with Aztec dancers, a bike parade with the Mexican Cultural Center, mariachi by **Mariachi Villaseñor**, and a migrant-memorial altar by Claudia Peregrina ([Wooder Ice, Oct 2025](https://wooderice.com/day-of-the-dead-returns-to-9th-street-with-parade-live-music-art-and-cultural-celebration/)). - **Kennett Square (Chester County)**, home of the mushroom industry's Mexican workforce: Casa Guanajuato (a hometown association) runs a Hispanic Heritage Festival in September and Día de los Muertos events; local group Danza Tenochtli held a 2025 "Raíces y Ritmos" fundraiser ([Kennett Collaborative](https://kennettcollaborative.org/event/hispanic-heritage-festival/); [Casa Guanajuato](https://www.casaguanajuato.org/en/events/); [Zeffy](https://www.zeffy.com/en-US/ticketing/2025-function-danza-tenochtli-raices-y-ritmos)). ### 5. African American and African diaspora traditions (thriving)

**Drum and dance lineage.** Ghanaian artist Saka Acquaye arrived in 1953 and taught West African drumming and dance; students included Arthur Hall (Arthur Hall Afro-American Dance Ensemble, 1958; later the Ile Ife center, now the Village of Arts and Humanities) and Robert "Baba" Crowder (Kulu Mele African Dance & Drum Ensemble, 1969). Ione Nash ("Philly's great-grandmother of African dance") ran an ensemble that was an Odunde fixture; Nana Korantema Ayeboafo founded Philadelphia's first all-female percussion ensemble in 1975. John and Dorothy Wilkie directed Kulu Mele after study in Guinea, Senegal, Ghana and Cuba; Fasina Wilkie and Ama Schley now lead it. Assane (Anssumane) Silla and Cachet Ivey "have led open community classes for decades"; Ira Bond's Malidelphia bridges African immigrant and African American communities ([Temple PJC / Love Now Media, June 2025](https://templepjc.org/2025/06/24/phillys-african-drum-tradition-as-resistance-and-ritual/); [PFP: Kulu Mele](https://folkloreproject.org/artists/kulu-mele/)). The same article places drum circles at **Malcolm X Park** (51st and Pine) as a current West Philadelphia practice.

**Odunde Festival**, founded 1975 by Lois Fernandez and Ruth Arthur (first held in April 1975 as the "Oshun Festival" on $100 of neighborhood donations), is held in June at South Street and Grays Ferry Avenue. It begins with a procession to the Schuylkill River and claims to be the "Largest African American Street Festival in North America", with an attendance figure of 500,000 (self-reported, low confidence). It ran in 2026 ([odundefestival.org](https://www.odundefestival.org/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odunde_Festival)).

**Big August Quarterly, Wilmington.** Begun in 1814 by Peter Spencer for the African Union Church; once a "kind of Independence Day" for free and enslaved Black people of Delmarva (Harriet Tubman and Thomas Garrett were often in town) and called the oldest such celebration in the country ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_August_Quarterly)). It is still running: 24-31 Aug 2025, and in 2027 a wreath-laying, a Youth Gospel Explosion and "The Big Quarterly" with "local and regional and nationally known gospel groups all day long" at Tubman-Garrett Riverfront Park ([augustquarterly.org](https://augustquarterly.org/)). Host: Mother African Union Church, incorporated 1813.

**Gospel quartet.** The Dixie Hummingbirds (formed 1928 in Greenville SC, Philadelphia-based) were NEA Heritage Fellows in 2000, listed as Philadelphia ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/dixie-hummingbirds); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixie_Hummingbirds)). The community quartet circuit (anniversaries, church programs) is **not verified** for 2024-26 in my searches; the August Quarterly is the best-documented living gospel gathering.

**Tap and vernacular.** LaVaughn Robinson (NEA 1989, South Philadelphia street tap) and Horace "Spoons" Williams (NEA 1985, spoons/bones and poetry) are honored as Philadelphia folk artists ([NEA Robinson](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/lavaughn-e-robinson); [NEA Williams](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/horace-spoons-williams)). Germaine Ingram (tap) is on PFP's roster ([PFP sitemap](https://folkloreproject.org/artists-sitemap.xml)).

**Capoeira Angola.** FICA Philly (International Capoeira Angola Foundation chapter), co-led by Mestre Kamau Blakney (made Mestre in Brazil in 2019) and Contramestre Eva, appears every year at Odunde ([PFP](https://folkloreproject.org/artists/mestre-kamau-blakney/)).

**Liberian.** The Liberian Women's Chorus for Change (Fatu Gayflor, "the golden voice of Liberia"; Zaye Tete and Marie Nyenabo, both former National Cultural Troupe members; the late Tokay Tomah) uses traditional song, dance and drama on war trauma and gender violence; it was the subject of PFP's 2016 film *Because of the War* ([PFP artists feed](https://folkloreproject.org/artists/feed/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Folklore_Project)). Evidence is from 2022 PFP pages; I found **no dated 2024-26 public event**, so: niche.

### 6. Asian diasporas

- **Cambodian (active).** CAGE (Cambodian American Girls Empowering, started 2015, studio in the Bok Building) runs **weekly classical and folk dance classes September-May with the Cambodian Association of Greater Philadelphia (CAGP)**, ending in a recital ([cagempowering.org](https://www.cagempowering.org/)); a CAGE open practice was listed for Dec 2025 ([do215](https://do215.com/events/2025/12/10/cage-s-open-practice-tickets)). CAGP's 46th Cambodian New Year gala was held 5 April 2025 ([Global Philadelphia](https://globalphiladelphia.org/events/46th-annual-cambodian-new-year-celebration-cambodian-association)). PFP artists include Thavro Phim, a Royal University of Fine Arts dancer who specializes in the monkey role ("one of only three professionally-trained Cambodian dancers specializing in the monkey role living in this country") and moved to Philadelphia in 2001 ([PFP](https://folkloreproject.org/artists/thavro-phim/)), plus Chamroeun Yin, Leendavy Koung and Eang Mao ([PFP sitemap](https://folkloreproject.org/artists-sitemap.xml)). I could not verify a living **pin peat** ensemble.
- **Chinese (thriving lion dance, niche opera).** The **Philadelphia Suns** (founded 1972 per search summaries) run Lunar New Year **Midnight Eve and New Year's Day lion dance parades** through Chinatown with firecrackers. Practice is **open to anyone, Sundays 1:00-2:30 pm at 227 N. 10th St, from the weekend after Labor Day until Lunar New Year**, taught by Harry Leong and Kung Fu Master Peter Louie ([phillysuns.org](https://www.phillysuns.org/lion-dance); [2025 parade post](https://www.phillysuns.org/post/2025-lunar-new-year-parade)). The **Philadelphia Chinese Opera Society** (Feb 1999; artistic director Shuyuan Li; "the only local organization" for Beijing opera; 2023 Bartol award) teaches in Chinatown ([philapcos.org](https://philapcos.org/); [PFP](https://folkloreproject.org/artists/philadelphia-chinese-opera-society/)). PFP also lists Sifu Shu Pui Cheung (lion dance/kung fu) and Hua Hua Zhang.
- **Indonesian (active, growing; a surprise).** Gapura, "the first Indonesian Community Center in the Philadelphia region", and Fleisher Art Memorial host an annual Indonesian Festival at Fleisher (719 Catharine St) with Sundanese, Balinese and Javanese dance, gamelan, angklung and the troupe Modero & Company. The 3rd edition was in 2025 and it continued in 2026 ([Fleisher](https://fleisher.org/community-programs/indonesian-festival-2026_/); [Fleisher 3rd annual](https://fleisher.org/3rd-annual-indonesian-festival/)). Sundanese dancer Detty Kastubi Aziz: "having the festival in the Philly area is like building a home". ### 10. Appalachian migrant music in the MSA's southwestern corner (historic_only, but key for a fiddler)

- **Ola Belle Reed** (1916-2002), a clawhammer banjo player and singer born in Ashe County NC, moved with her family to southern Pennsylvania as a teenager. With her brother Alex Campbell she ran the **New River Ranch** music park in Rising Sun, Cecil County MD (inside the MSA) until it was destroyed in 1958, then was house band at **Sunset Park**, West Grove (Chester County). She was a 1986 NEA National Heritage Fellow listed at Rising Sun MD ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ola_Belle_Reed); [NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/ola-belle-reed)). Her "High on a Mountain" and "I've Endured" are bluegrass standards.
- **Sunset Park** (Penn Township near West Grove) was run by the Waltman family from 1940 to 1995. Its PHMC marker (2018) calls it "one of the premier music venues outside of Nashville" (Hank Williams, Loretta Lynn, Dolly Parton, Bill Monroe) and notes that live unreleased recordings survive ([HMDB](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=135755)). Sunday shows ran May-September.
- The same Chester/Cecil borderland is now home to Kennett Square's Mexican mushroom-worker community (section 4): one rural corner, two migrant music worlds sixty years apart.

## Part 4. History, people, festivals, instruction, shops, archives and the fiddle angle

Slice: history / people / festivals / instruction / shops & makers / archives / fiddle angle / character.
Metro: Philadelphia-Camden-Wilmington, PA-NJ-DE-MD MSA (Bucks, Chester, Delaware, Montgomery, Philadelphia PA; Burlington,
Camden, Gloucester, Salem NJ; New Castle DE; Cecil MD). As of 2026-10-30. Research by web search and fetch only.

### 1. History

#### Colonial and early national (1638–1840s)

- **New Sweden (1638–1655)** put Swedish, Finnish and later Dutch settlers on the lower Delaware, around Wilmington's Fort
  Christina ([New Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Sweden)). The Swedish Colonial Society and Gloria Dei (Old
  Swedes') have republished the *Rudman Hymnals* of 1700, with 17th-century melodies
  ([Swedish Colonial Society](https://colonialswedes.net/music/)). The American Swedish Historical Museum in South
  Philadelphia, founded in 1926, calls itself the oldest Swedish museum in the US
  ([ASHM](https://mnet.swedishamericanmuseum.org/index.php/american-swedish-historical-museum)).
- **Mumming** came with English, Swedish and German immigrants before the Revolution. "Mummers, shooters, belsnickles,
  fantasticals and callithumpians" roamed the streets between Christmas Eve and New Year's Day
  ([Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia, "Mummers"](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/mummers/)).
- **The Dancing Assembly of Philadelphia (1748–49)** was a subscriber-only elite assembly on the model of Bath
  ([EGP](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/dancing-assembly/),
  [Digitens](https://www.digitens.org/en/notices/philadelphia-dancing-assembly-1749-1849.html)).
- **Pennsylvania German culture** took root with Germantown (1683) and spread up the Perkiomen. Goschenhoppen, in
  upper Montgomery County inside the MSA, is "one of the oldest continuously existing Pennsylvania German communities in
  America" ([Goschenhoppen Historians](https://goschenhoppen.org/the-vault/)).
- **Philadelphia was the early republic's tune-publishing capital.** "The President's March" (the basis of "Hail,
  Columbia") was issued by Moller & Capron in 1793. Benjamin Carr's *Evening Amusement* (1796) printed "College
  Hornpipe" and "White Cockade" ([TTA: President's March](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:President's_March_(2)_(The)),
  [TTA: College Hornpipe](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:College_Hornpipe_(The))). George Willig published
  "Caledonian March" in 1837 ([TTA](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Caledonian_March_(1))). A 1859 manuscript by
  M. E. Eames, "probably from Philadelphia", holds "Reefer's Hornpipe"
  ([TTA](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Reefer's_Hornpipe)).
- **Francis Johnson (1792–1844)** was a Black bandleader, keyed-bugle virtuoso, fiddler and composer, and the first
  Black American to publish sheet music. His cotillions "gave Philadelphia its rhythm"
  ([Museum of the American Revolution](https://www.amrevmuseum.org/the-music-of-francis-johnson-a-soundtrack-to-antebellum-black-philadelphia),
  [WRTI 2026](https://www.wrti.org/wrti-spotlight/2026-05-20/francis-johnson-a-black-music-trailblazer-of-the-antebellum-era)).
  Sources disagree on his birthplace: most say Philadelphia, but [BlackPast](https://www.blackpast.org/african-american-history/johnson-francis-1792-1844/) says Martinique.
- **Minstrelsy.** Philadelphia "was an important center in the early development of minstrelsy"
  ([EGP, "Oh, Dem Golden Slippers"](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/oh-dem-golden-slippers/)). *The Crow
  Quadrilles* (1837) and "Brack Eyed Susianna" (1846) were printed here
  ([TTA](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:My_Long_Tail_Blue),
  [TTA](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Been_to_the_East_Been_to_the_West)).

#### Banjos, pipes and parades (1860s–1915)

- **H. A. Weymann & Son (1864)** of 923 Market Street made the "Keystone State" mandolins, banjos and guitars, an early
  "jumbo" guitar and the Mandolute ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weymann_guitars),
  [Vintage Banjo Maker](https://www.vintagebanjomaker.com/weymann/4594323581)).
- **S. S. Stewart (1855–1898)** ran "one of the largest banjo manufacturers in the 1890s", making tens of thousands a year.
  He published *S. S. Stewart's Banjo and Guitar Journal* from 1882 to 1902
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Swaim_Stewart)). In January 1898 he merged with George Bauer as
  Stewart & Bauer, at 1410–1414 N. 6th Street, and died that April ([Vintage Banjo Maker](https://www.vintagebanjomaker.com/stewart/4594323549)).
  Keenophone, a Philadelphia phonograph maker, took over the Bauer company in 1911. Buegeleisen & Jacobson bought the
  Stewart name in 1915 ([Mugwumps](http://www.mugwumps.com/sss_date.html)). The Smithsonian holds an 1889 Stewart
  five-string ([NMAH](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/search/object/nmah_605689)). An *Antiques Roadshow*
  appraiser speaks of a whole "Philadelphia school" of banjo making
  ([PBS](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/roadshow/appraisals/s-s-stewart-philadelphia-banjo-ca-1885/)).
- **William and Charles Taylor**, uilleann-pipe makers from Drogheda, appear in Philadelphia city directories from
  1877 to 1892. They redesigned the pipes into a louder, concert-pitch "American" instrument
  ([Lives of the Pipers](https://livesofthepipers.com/1taylorwilliam.html), [bagpipedia](https://www.bagpipedia.nl/lemma_person.php?id=3060)).
  James E. Brennan (born Philadelphia, 1879) made pipes "in the style of the Taylor brothers"
  ([Lives of the Pipers](https://livesofthepipers.com/pipemakers.html)). "Taylor's Hornpipe" may be named for Billy Taylor,
  one of Patsy Touhey's teachers ([TTA](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Taylor%27s_Hornpipe_(2))).
- **James Bland's "Oh, Dem Golden Slippers" (1879)** was a minstrel parody of a spiritual written by a Black songwriter.
  It became the Mummers' unofficial anthem, said to have been brought to them around 1905 by the minstrel man Charles
  Dumont, and it is also a bluegrass staple ([EGP](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/oh-dem-golden-slippers/)).
- **The Mummers Parade (1901–)** is a city-sponsored New Year's Day parade up Broad Street, and "the longest-running
  continuous folk parade" in the US ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummers_Parade)). The first string band
  marched in 1901 "as a novelty", with violins, banjos and guitars. A separate division was formed in 1915, and
  saxophones, accordions and glockenspiels came later ([PMSBA](https://www.pmsba.org/about-us/),
  [Fralinger](https://www.fralinger.org/mummers-history)). Blackface was banned in 1963 after NAACP protests led by
  Cecil B. Moore. In 1992 Cambodian American artists staged a Khmer dance drama with a fancy brigade
  ([EGP](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/mummers/)).

#### Irish and Jewish 78s; Appalachian migrants (1907–1960)

- **John McGettigan**, a Donegal man, settled in Philadelphia in 1907
  ([The Session](https://thesession.org/discussions/50812)). He recorded 85 sides between 1922 and 1939 as leader of
  McGettigan's Orchestra, the Killarney Trio, the All-Irish Orchestra and the Irish Minstrels
  ([DAHR](https://adp.library.ucsb.edu/index.php/mastertalent/detail/111896/McGettigan_John)).
- **Ed Reavy** came over in 1912 and settled in the "Corktown" enclave, now Powelton Village
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Reavy)). He made a Victor trial on 26 September 1924. On 25 April
  1928 he recorded "Boys of the Lough / Tom Clark's Fancy" in Camden, with Michael Crowley on piano, and played fiddle
  with the Killarney Trio ([DAHR](https://adp.library.ucsb.edu/index.php/mastertalent/detail/116340/Reavy_Ed)).
  *Wikipedia gives 1927; DAHR's ledger says 1928.*
- **Klezmer.** Harry Kandel came to Philadelphia in 1913, played with Sousa, started his orchestra in 1916, and recorded
  for Victor from 1917 to 1927 ([FAU](https://rsa.fau.edu/blog/collections/judaic-collection/kandel-klezmer-orchestra/)).
  Jacob Hoffman (xylophone) plays on Kandel's 1924 Victor "Doina and hora", issued from Camden
  ([UW-Madison](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/ATKYLGKR3GKKMP9E/A52AI3F7RKGJLR8M)). He was the father of
  NEA Fellow Elaine Hoffman Watts ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/elaine-hoffman-watts)). Hankus Netsky's
  *Klezmer: Music and Community in Twentieth-Century Jewish Philadelphia* (Temple UP, 2015) is the standard study
  ([Temple UP](https://tupress.temple.edu/book/20000000009593)).
- **The Route 1 "hillbilly" belt.** In the Depression the Campbell family moved from the New River valley in North
  Carolina to northeastern Maryland. "By the mid-1930s, scores of music parks and picnic grounds had been established" for
  Appalachian migrants across Maryland, Delaware and Pennsylvania ([NEA: Ola Belle Reed](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/ola-belle-reed)).
  - **Sunset Park** in Jennersville (West Grove), Chester County, ran from 1940 to 1995. Hank Williams and Johnny Cash
    played there ([Chester County History Center](https://www.facebook.com/ChesterCountyHistory/posts/who-remembers-sunset-park-sunset-park-was-a-music-venue-in-jennersville-pa-open-/1544622707667950/),
    [Daily Local](https://www.dailylocal.com/2018/09/03/anita-carr-to-talk-about-history-of-sunset-park/)). The PHMC
    approved a state historical marker for it ([Bluegrass Today](https://bluegrasstoday.com/sunset-park-designated-a-pennsylvania-landmark/)).
  - **The other parks:** New River Ranch in Rising Sun, the Gloryland Campground in Bear DE, and the Santa Fe Ranch,
    Ravine Park and others in Pennsylvania ([BlueGrass West](https://bluegrasswest.com/ideas/sunsetpark.htm)).
- **The Chester and Delaware Counties' Old Fiddlers' Picnic** began at Crystal Springs Park near Parkesburg, moved to
  Lenape Park, and went to Hibernia County Park after Lenape closed in 1980. The 96th was in 2025 and the 97th on
  8 August 2026, which implies about 1930 for the first ([Coatesville Times](https://coatesvilletimes.com/?p=37080),
  [PhillyVoice](https://www.phillyvoice.com/old-fiddlers-picnic-hibernia-park-2026/)).
- **The Irish Musicians' Association of America** was founded in the mid-1950s by Louis Quinn (NY), Frank Thornton
  (Chicago) and Ed Reavy (Philadelphia) ([Mid-Atlantic CCÉ](https://ccemidatlantic.com/louis-quinn)).

#### The revival (1956–1981)

- **The Gilded Cage (1956–1969)** was Esther and Ed Halpern's coffeehouse on South 21st Street near Rittenhouse Square.
  PFS says its "seeds … were planted" there ([PFS](https://pfs.org/), [EGP](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/coffeehouses/)).
- **Philadelphia Folksong Society (1957)** was started by George Britton, Mike Marmel, Joe Aronson and others to hold
  small concerts "nine times each year". It was incorporated in 1963
  ([Penn finding aid](https://findingaids.library.upenn.edu/records/SMREP_PFS.01)).
- **The Irish Center and the Céilí Group (1958).** The Commodore John Barry Society bought the old Pelham Auto Club at
  6815 Emlen St, Mt. Airy, and the Céilí Group was founded there the same year
  ([Irish Center history](https://theirishcenter.org/irish-center-history/)).
- **Penn folklore.** MacEdward Leach built the second US folklore PhD program (1959) and a department (1962)
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacEdward_Leach), [Penn Museum](https://www.penn.museum/collections/archives/findingaid/879858)).
  Kenneth S. Goldstein, a folk-record producer, earned Penn's first folklore PhD in 1963
  ([Penn Museum](https://collections.penn.museum/collections/archives/findingaid/879859)).
- **Philadelphia Folk Festival (1962).** Goldstein and DJ Gene Shay founded it with Britton, Bob Siegel, David Baskin,
  Esther Halpern and Tossi Aaron, during a hiatus of Newport ([EGP](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/philadelphia-folk-festival/)).
  - **First years:** Wilson Farm, Paoli. Pete Seeger, Rev. Gary Davis, the Greenbriar Boys and the Obray Ramsey played
    the first one, which drew about 2,500 people. Attendance reached about 10,000 in 1963 and 15,000 in 1964. Profits
    funded a folklore library and Penn folklore fellowships.
  - **Moves:** Tredyffrin Township revoked its zoning variance in January 1965 after a show ran to 2 a.m.
    ([EGP](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/essays/philadelphia-folk-festival/)). *Sources disagree on what
    followed.* EGP says Old Pool Farm "by 1965". Tredyffrin Easttown Historical Society says Spring Mountain in 1966 and
    Old Pool in 1967 ([TEHS](https://www.facebook.com/TEHistoricalSociety/posts/the-philadelphia-folk-festivalthe-first-annual-philadelphia-folk-festival-was-he/6139733699388369/)).
    Upper Salford Township says Spring Mountain in 1966, a township park in 1967, and Old Pool Farm from 1971
    ([Upper Salford](https://uppersalfordtownship.com/philadelphia-folk-fest/)).
- **The 2nd Fret** at 1902 Sansom Street was Manny Rubin's club and "Philly's premier folk club". Joni Mitchell
  recorded live there in 1967 ([All That Philly Jazz](https://phillyjazz.us/2015/05/04/the-2nd-fret/)). "Philadelphia"
  Jerry Ricks booked it from 1960 to 1966. He hosted and studied with Son House, Mississippi John Hurt, Skip James and
  Libba Cotten, and recorded with Hurt in 1964 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Ricks)).
- **The Main Point (1964–1981)** at 874 Lancaster Avenue, Bryn Mawr, was founded by four couples, including the
  Campbells and PFS co-founder George Britton and his wife. Bruce Springsteen first played "Thunder Road" (as "Wings
  for Wheels") there on 5 February 1975 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Main_Point),
  [Temple SCRC](https://findingaids.library.upenn.edu/records/TUSCRC_SCRC249)). Its successor, The Point, ran from
  1998 to 2005.
- **Gene Shay** produced weekly folk radio from 1962 (WHAT, then WHYY, then WXPN) until his last WXPN show on
  1 February 2015 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_Shay)). He emceed the festival for more than 50 years.
- **The Brandywine strand.**
  - **BFOTM:** Founded in Delaware by Carl Goldstein and Shel Sandler, two young lawyers, with the autoharpist Mike
    Hudak, a protégé of Kilby Snow. Snow had moved to Landenberg, Chester County ([FRC](https://fieldrecorder.org/brandywine-friends-of-old-time-music/)).
    *The founding year is 1971 on the DVBF site (first concert: Ralph Stanley, January 1971) and 1972 on BFOTM's own
    About page.* Snow's Folkways LP was recorded "on the stage of an auditorium near Kennett Square"
    ([Folkways](https://folkways.si.edu/kilby-snow/country-songs-and-tunes-with-autoharp/old-time/music/album/smithsonian)).
  - **Delaware Valley Bluegrass Festival:** first held Labor Day 1972 in Bear, DE.
  - **Brandywine Mountain Music Convention:** 1974–1993 ([BFOTM](https://brandywinefriends.org/about-us/)).
- **Goschenhoppen Folk Festival (1967–)**, by the Goschenhoppen Historians (founded 1963), is a non-commercial "living
  history" Pennsylvania German festival. It began in East Greenville and moved to the Henry Antes Plantation in 2007
  ([UPV News](https://upvnews.com/news/article.ashx?article=25823),
  [Boyertown Area Expression](https://boyertownareaexpression.town.news/g/boyertown-pa/n/383073/goschenhoppen-folk-festival-finds-national-significance)).
- **Bucks County Folk Music Shop (1966)** was opened by Karl and Jackie, who played in the folk and bluegrass group The
  Odes ([about](https://www.buckscountyfolkmusic.com/pages/about-us)). WHYY calls it the oldest family-run acoustic
  stringed-instrument store in the US ([WHYY](https://whyy.org/articles/bucks-county-folk-music-shop-bluegrass-tradition/)).
- **Mick Moloney** came in 1973 for graduate study at Penn ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/mick-moloney)).
  He catalysed the Céilí Group festival in 1975 and formed Green Fields of America in Philadelphia in 1977
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Fields_of_America)).
- **Philadelphia Céilí Group festival (1975)** was organised by 13 volunteers, including the McGills, the Hauns, Tim
  Britton and Carl Zuckerman. It was billed as "the first non-competitive presentation of Irish Music, Song and Dance in
  the US" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_C%C3%A9il%C3%AD_Group)). The 14th festival was held on
  10 September 1988 at Villanova University ([Villanova](https://digital.library.villanova.edu/Record/vudl:1152209)).
- **Other PFS events:** the Odyssey of American Music school program (1974), Spring Thing (May 1976) and Fall Fling
  (September 1977) ([Wikipedia: PFS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Folksong_Society)).
- **The Thursday Night Contra** started in the 1970s at Summit Presbyterian in Mt. Airy. It moved to Glenside Memorial
  Hall in 1996 and back to Mt. Airy, at the Commodore Barry Club, in 2014 ([Thursday Contra](https://thursdaycontra.com/welcome.html)).

#### Consolidation and decline of the old institutions (1981–2019)

- **1987:** Debora Kodish founded the **Philadelphia Folklore Project** and led it until 2014. It supported more than
  350 folk artists, framed as "folk arts and social change" ([PFP staff](https://folkloreproject.org/about/staff-board/),
  [Leeway](https://www.leeway.org/grantees/debora-kodish)).
- **1990:** The Delaware Valley Bluegrass Festival moved to the Salem County Fairgrounds near Woodstown, NJ.
- **1993:** Brandywine was suspended after its 20th anniversary "due to a shortage of funds"
  ([FRC](https://fieldrecorder.org/brandywine-friends-of-old-time-music/)).
- **1995:** Sunset Park closed. Eugene O'Donnell went home to Derry after living in Philadelphia from 1957 to 1995
  ([TTA via search](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Eugene_O'Donnell)). The AFC accessioned the Philadelphia Céilí
  Group collection as AFC 1995/003 ([LoC](https://www.loc.gov/folklife/guides/ceili.html)).
- **1996:** Joseph M. Reavy published *The Collected Compositions of Ed Reavy* (Green Grass Music)
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Reavy)).
- **1998:** The Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann Philadelphia/Delaware Valley branch was formed ([CCÉ-DV](https://cce-dv.com/who-we-are/)).
- **1999:** Penn closed the Department of Folklore and Folklife and kept a graduate group and a Center for Folklore and
  Ethnography ([Penn Almanac](https://almanac.upenn.edu/archive/v46/n01/folkclose.html),
  [Almanac on Abrahams](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/roger-abrahams-folklore-and-folklife)). Its archive went to the
  Penn Museum Archives in 2011 ([Penn finding aid](https://findingaids.library.upenn.edu/records/UPENN_MUSEUM_PU-MU.4010)).
- **2011:** The Milliner–Koken collection was published ([Heritage Tunes](https://heritagetunes.com/books/the-milliner-koken-collection-of-american-fiddle-tunes/)).
- **2014:**
  - Koken and Milliner started the **Brandywine Revival** old-time camping and jamming weekend on the Milliner farm
    ([Banjo Hangout](https://www.banjohangout.org/archive/286404)).
  - "Friends of the Irish Center" raised $82,000 to save the building ([Irish Center](https://theirishcenter.org/irish-center-history/),
    [Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/IrishCenterPHL/posts/another-amazing-story-about-our-irish-center-and-our-amazing-volunteers-httpswww/680394754116047/)).
- **2015–2018:** PFS announced a move to a former church at 6156 Ridge Ave, Roxborough
  ([WHYY](https://whyy.org/articles/philly-folksong-society-alters-long-range-plan-with-move-to-former-roxborough-church/)).
  It opened the **Philadelphia Folk School** there in September 2018
  ([Patch](https://patch.com/pennsylvania/roxborough/philadelphia-folksong-society-opens-folk-school)).
- **2018:** Kevin McGillian died. He was a Tyrone-born accordionist and ceili player whom the CCÉ branch and the Céilí
  Group both honour ([CCÉ-DV](https://cce-dv.com/our-history-kevin-mcgillian), [obit](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/188501528/b_kevin-mcgillian)).

#### COVID, crisis and rebound (2020–2026)

- **2020:** Gene Shay died of COVID-19 on 17 April 2020, aged 85 ([Penn Almanac](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/gene-shay-wxpn)).
  The festival ran virtually in 2020 and 2021 and raised more than $200,000 for out-of-work artists. It came back as a
  hybrid event in 2022, its 60th anniversary ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Folk_Festival)).
- **2022:** Mick Moloney died on 27 July 2022 in New York ([Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/obituaries/2022/08/06/mick-moloney-obituary-banjo-player-and-scholar-with-a-driving-passion-for-traditional-and-folk-music/)).
- **February 2023:** PFS cancelled the festival for the first time since 1962. It was "deeply in debt from the previous
  festival and the general operations", with debt reported at over $200k
  ([Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/entertainment/music/philadelphia-folk-festival-canceled-folksong-society-debt-20230209.html),
  [PFS](https://pfs.org/3421-2/)).
- **May 13–14, 2023:** PFS moved out of the Ridge Avenue building ([YouTube via search](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VORAFeEPBPc)),
  and the Folk School ended with it. *Note: the brief's "2024 financial crisis" is actually 2023. In 2024 the festival
  returned.*
- **2024:** The festival returned on August 16–18 as the "61st", with Gangstagrass, John Oates and Tony Trischka's
  EarlJam. One donor gave $25,000 from a family trust. Board meetings were opened to the public ([PFS](https://pfs.org/3421-2/)).
  Also in 2024, the Céilí Group festival turned 50 ([Chestnut Hill Local via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_C%C3%A9il%C3%AD_Group)).
  The Philadelphia Irish Festival at Penn's Landing was cancelled because the I-95 cap work demolished its plaza
  ([Irish Star](https://www.irishstar.com/news/pennsylvania-news/philadelphia-irish-festival-cancelled-construction-32917956)).
  Just outside the MSA, the Kutztown Folk Festival (1950–2024) was suspended in November 2024 after $347k of losses
  since 2022 ([experiencepa](https://www.experiencepa.com/kutztown-folk-festival/)).
- **2025:**
  - February: the PFP archive of more than 75,000 items went to Temple's Special Collections Research Center
    ([PFP](https://folkloreproject.org/pfp-archive-finds-new-home-at-temple-university-library/),
    [AFS](https://americanfolkloresociety.org/philadelphia-folklore-project-transfers-its-archive-to-temple-university/)).
  - August 15–17: the "62nd" Philadelphia Folk Festival ([Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/p/DAYgG7tNUCc/),
    [NJ Arts Maven](https://njartsmaven.substack.com/p/philadelphia-folksong-society-and)).
- **2026:**
  - March 10: the third Gene Shay Founders' Award concert, honouring Tom Paxton, at the Colonial Theatre, Phoenixville
    ([PFS](https://pfs.org/3rd-annual-gene-shay-founders-award-benefit-concert/)).
  - May 22–24: Spring Thing at a new site, College Settlement Camp in Horsham ([PFS posts](https://pfs.org/posts/)).
  - August 14–16: the "63rd" Folk Festival, with Leftover Salmon, Sam Bush, Tom Rush and Terrance Simien. It used seven
    stages and 1,500–2,000 volunteers ([Philadelphia Weekly](https://philadelphiaweekly.com/news/2026/08/15/philadelphia-folk-festival-opens-its-64th-edition-at-old/),
    [folkfest.org](https://folkfest.org/)).
  - July: a $15,000 "Friend-a-thon" fundraiser ([PFS posts](https://pfs.org/posts/)).
  - **2027 is announced for August 13–15**, billed as the 64th year ([folkfest.org/about](https://folkfest.org/about/)).
  - The Mummers Museum turns 50 and has a new 50-year contract with the city ([WFMZ](https://www.wfmz.com/news/area/southeastern-pa/mummers-museum-preserves-125-years-of-colorful-history/article_e2c34346-e12d-45a5-a04e-8147a716152a.html)).
  - Elsewhere in 2026: the 54th Delaware Valley Bluegrass Festival (September 4–6), the 97th Old Fiddlers' Picnic
    (August 8), the Brandywine Revival (June 19–21) and the 52nd Céilí Group festival (October 9–11).

#### Fiddlers and other instrumentalists

**Irish:**

- **Ed Reavy (1897/98–1988)**, Cavan-born fiddler and composer.
  - **Output:** more than 100 published tunes, and his sons estimated over 500 in all
    ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Reavy)).
  - **Best known:** "The Hunter's House", "Never Was Piping So Gay", "Fisherman's Island", "Maudabawn Chapel", "The
    Man from Barnagrove" and "In Memory of Coleman" (all on [The Session](https://thesession.org/tunes/search?q=reavy)).
  - **Honours:** president of the Irish Musicians' Association; named "Composer of the Century" in 2000
    ([Irish Echo via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Reavy)).
- **Eugene O'Donnell** was a Derry fiddler and step dancer, and a master of slow airs and set dances. He lived in
  Philadelphia from 1957 to 1995 and joined Green Fields of America
  ([Compass](https://www.compassrecords.com/artist/eugene-odonnell/),
  [Ward Irish Music Archives](https://soundcloud.com/ward-irish-music-archives/the-hurling-boys)). The slip jig
  "Eugene O'Donnell's" was composed for him by James MacCafferty ([TTA](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Eugene_O'Donnell)).
- **John McGettigan** (Donegal; arrived 1907) was a singer, fiddler and bandleader on 78s from 1922 to 1939
  ([DAHR](https://adp.library.ucsb.edu/index.php/mastertalent/detail/111896/McGettigan_John)).
- **B. Kevin McGillian (1927–2018)** was a Tyrone-born accordionist from Upper Darby, a ceili player and a composer of
  jigs ([Blackwater](https://blackwatermusic.com/music/blackwater/track/tonroes-ciaran-tourishs-kevin-mcgillians/)).
- **Séamus Egan** (born Hatboro, 1969; raised in Mayo and Lansdowne) won All-Ireland titles on four instruments, played
  in Green Fields of America and co-founded Solas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamus_Egan),
  [Boston College](https://findingaids.bc.edu/agents/people/3563)).
- **Tim Britton**, a piper, was among the Céilí festival's founders and played at the Céilí Group's 1979 "Cultural
  Series" with Moloney and O'Donnell ([Villanova](https://digital.library.villanova.edu/Collection/vudl:358225)).

**Old-time and bluegrass:**

- **Ola Belle Reed (1916–2002)**, her brother **Alex Campbell**, and her husband **Bud** and son **David Reed**. Their
  bands were the New River Boys, the Boys and Girls, and the Gang. They broadcast weekly from their store in Oxford, PA
  ([UNC](https://finding-aids.lib.unc.edu/catalog/20010)).
- **Kilby Snow (1905–1980)**, a left-handed autoharp virtuoso, lived in Landenberg, Chester County

  Heritage Award in 2016 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Koken)). **Clare Milliner** is a fiddler and
  co-author of the collection, and hosts the Brandywine Revival on her family farm. They run Mudthumper Music in Kennett
  Square ([Mudthumper](http://mudthumper.com/)).
- **Carl Goldstein, Sheldon (Shel) Sandler and Mike Hudak** founded BFOTM ([FRC](https://fieldrecorder.org/brandywine-friends-of-old-time-music/)).
- **Bill and Fritzi Wisdom** ran the "Friday Night Old Time" jams, now an archive of recordings linked from
  [phillyoldtime.org/resources](https://www.phillyoldtime.org/index.php/resources/).
- **Current teachers** listed by phillyoldtime.org include Elizabeth LaPrelle (clawhammer banjo and mountain-style
  singing, West Philadelphia), Madeleine Smith (fiddle, Mt. Airy), Palmer Loux (fiddle) and Ted Klein (banjo, northern
  Delaware) ([teachers](https://www.phillyoldtime.org/index.php/teachers/)).

### 7. Fiddle angle

#### Local styles and lineages

1. **Philadelphia Irish.** The defining figures are Ulster-born: Reavy (Cavan), McGettigan (Donegal), O'Donnell (Derry
   slow airs and set dances) and McGillian (Tyrone). The other Irish metros are associated with Sligo and Clare. *This
   is an inference from the people found here (low-medium confidence)* and worth testing. Reavy's compositions are the
   region's biggest contribution to the world repertoire.
2. **The Route 1 Appalachian-migrant belt** runs through southern Chester County, Cecil County and New Castle County.
   - **Ola Belle Reed's** banjo-and-song country music came from Ashe and Alleghany Counties, NC, and was played in
     music parks.
   - **Kilby Snow's** autoharp, and the Delaware Valley Bluegrass Festival.
   - **The Brandywine convention** turned the area into old-time music's mid-Atlantic hub for 20 years. Its legacy:
     BFOTM concerts, which still run in Newark DE (Wood Flower on Nov 6, 2026; Midnight Traynhams on Dec 4, 2026),
     the Brandywine Revival, the Milliner–Koken book, and Slippery-Hill.
3. **Klezmer** has a three-generation professional family (the Hoffmans), and Kandel's 1917–27 Victor sides are
   foundational to the 1970s klezmer revival.
4. **Mummers string bands.** Mass-ensemble banjos (now tenor and plectrum), saxophones and glockenspiels play a
   repertoire anchored by "Oh, Dem Golden Slippers". It is a living, Philadelphia-only folk-band form.
5. **Colonial and federal dance-tune printing** (Carr, Willig, Moller & Capron). Philadelphia printed many of the
   country-dance tunes that New England contra fiddlers still play.

### 8. Character (from this slice)

- **Old institutions, recent fragility.** Many of the scene's anchors are 50–125 years old: the Mummers (1901), PFS
  (1957), the Céilí Group and the Irish Center (1958), the Folk Festival (1962), the Delaware Valley Bluegrass Festival
  (1972) and the Céilí festival (1975). Between 2014 and 2025, however:
  - the Irish Center nearly lost its building (2014);
  - Penn's department had closed (1999);
  - PFS cancelled its festival (2023), lost its headquarters and Folk School, and became all-volunteer;
  - PFP's founder retired (2014) and its archive went to Temple (2025).

  The pattern is retrenchment to volunteer models, and those are working: three festivals since the crisis and a 2027
  date set. Delaware's BFOTM has been all-volunteer from the start and has never stopped presenting.
- **Geography: a NW Philadelphia core with country festivals.** The weekly Irish and dance life is concentrated in
  Mt. Airy and Germantown. The Irish Center hosts the Céilí Group, the Thursday contra and the festival; Germantown
  Country Dancers meets at Summit Church; and PFS's PO box is on Germantown Avenue
  ([PFS contact](https://pfs.org/contact-pfs/), [GCD nearby dances](https://germantowncountrydancers.org/nearby.php)).
  Old-time players cluster in West Philadelphia (West Philly old-time jam and teachers). The big festivals are out in
  the countryside: Schwenksville (Montgomery), Woodstown (Salem NJ), Pocopson (Chester), Hibernia (Chester) and Fair
  Hill (Cecil).
- **A tri-state edge as a second centre.** For a fiddler, the Wilmington, Newark, Kennett Square and West Chester corner
  is arguably as important as the city. That is where the old-time and bluegrass infrastructure is.
- **Signature traditions from this slice:** Irish (composition, ceili and set dance), the Mummers string bands, revival
  folk song (Folk Fest, the coffeehouse lineage) and Appalachian-migrant old-time and bluegrass.

## Surprises

1. One Irish-American social club in Mt. Airy (the Irish Center, a 1905 auto-club building bought by Irish societies in 1958 and saved by an $82,000 community campaign in 2014) hosts the weekly contra, weekly international folk dance, weekly set dancing, a monthly session, a children's session, a free monthly céilí and a singers' session. ZIP 19119 alone carries about 24 recurring trad events a month.
2. Philadelphia invented the shapes: John Connelly designed the four shape notes there in 1798, and the first Sacred Harp (1844) was printed by T. K. & P. G. Collins, whose shop stood about where the Liberty Bell now hangs. The practice returned only in the late 1980s; local singers now co-edit their own tunebook, The Shenandoah Harmony.
3. Scottish country dancing is bigger than contra by some measures (847-member Meetup vs 559 for Mt. Airy contra; four weekly RSCDS classes and a 2026 Pride Ball), yet there is no Scottish or Cape Breton fiddle session anywhere in the metro.
4. The Mummers String Bands are a 14-band, all-amateur banjo-and-saxophone folk orchestra tradition with no brass and few violins. In 2026 weather pushed the judging into an NFL stadium a month late. Their theme, 'Oh, Dem Golden Slippers' (1879), now a bluegrass fiddle standard, was written by Black songwriter James Bland, who lay in an unmarked Bala Cynwyd grave until 1939; Black clubs marched 1901-1929 and blackface was banned only in 1963.
5. Kingsessing Morris Men (since 1977) march in the Mummers Parade on Broad Street (2025 theme: 'Brats on Broad', in hot-dog masks).
6. The West Philly old-time jam meets at an Ethiopian restaurant (Gojjo) and the West Philly bluegrass jam upstairs at another (Abyssinia), a block apart.
7. The Delaware Valley Bluegrass Festival (1972) began because Bill Monroe and Ralph Stanley asked Delaware's Brandywine Friends of Old Time Music to host the Northeast's first bluegrass festival; when they rebuffed old-time bands, BFOTM founded the Brandywine Mountain Music Convention (1974-93).
8. Slippery-Hill, the old-time fiddle world's source-recording archive, is fiscally sponsored by the Brandywine Friends and grew out of the Chester County-compiled Milliner-Koken Collection (1,404 tunes).
9. NEA Heritage Fellow Ola Belle Reed lived and ran a hillbilly music park (New River Ranch, 1951-58) inside the MSA in Cecil County, Maryland; nearby Sunset Park in Chester County hosted Hank Williams, Patsy Cline and Dolly Parton from 1940 to 1995.
10. Ed Reavy, arguably the greatest Irish-American tune composer, was a Philadelphia plumber; his Victor 78s were cut across the river in Camden on 25 April 1928 (not 1927 as usually stated).
11. The most-played Philly-titled session tune, 'Wissahickon Drive' (258 Session tunebooks), was written by Chicago's Liz Carroll, and the tune circulating as 'Philadelphia Reel' was written by a DC-area Frenchman about his red fiddle.
12. The Philadelphia Folk Festival, billed as North America's longest continuously running outdoor music festival, missed 2023: the society was down to about $30,000, the board discussed bankruptcy, and threats had led Homeland Security to recommend fencing and armed guards. It came back in 2024 with an all-volunteer working board.
13. WXPN's Folk Show, on Sunday nights since Gene Shay began it in 1962, has had no permanent host since Ian Zolitor's last show on Sept 13, 2026; World Cafe Live, named after the show Shay named, filed Chapter 11 in March 2026 and now calls itself World Stage.
14. Quaker and Mennonite meetinghouses are the region's folk infrastructure: Wrightstown Friends, Abington Friends, Perkasie Mennonite (a coffeehouse running 30 years), Doylestown Mennonite and Germantown Mennonite all host song circles, coffeehouses or Sacred Harp.
15. Arden, Delaware, a single-tax utopian village founded in 1900, runs contra, Israeli, international and Scottish-English dancing out of its Gild Hall almost every week, and once hosted Lead Belly, Pete Seeger and Burl Ives.
16. Wilmington's Big August Quarterly (1814) is described as the oldest Black festival in the US still running, now an all-day gospel festival; Lucia Fest at Old Swedes' Church reached its 88th year in 2025.
17. Mummery itself traces to Swedish and Finnish colonists' 'Second Day Christmas' visiting in Tinicum, Delaware County.
18. The four New Jersey counties of the MSA (about 1.3M people) have no regular contra and only a handful of sessions, yet host the region's biggest bluegrass festival (Salem County).
19. Rizzo's police raided the Humoresque coffeehouse on Sansom Street in 1959 for welcoming interracial and gay couples; that Sansom/Rittenhouse coffeehouse strip incubated the Folksong Society.
20. Philadelphia's Hmong community (about 3,000 around 1980) was largely driven out by racist violence in the mid-1980s, the reverse of the Twin Cities story.
21. The city's weekly son jarocho circle is queer- and trans-led and writes protest verses, including an anti-arena 'La Bamba' for Chinatown.
22. Several weekly dances happen on weekday daytimes (Fridays 12:30, Wednesdays 11:30, Thursdays 1:30), a sign of a retiree-driven international and Israeli dance scene.
23. Park Avenue Contra lets under-30s in for as little as $1; the Thursday contra cost $8 in 2014 and is now pay-what-you-wish ($10/$15/$20+), with role terms (Larks/Robins) set by a 101-member vote in 2023.
24. Delaware has zero NEA National Heritage Fellows; of the MSA's nine, only Mick Moloney and Ola Belle Reed come from fiddle-band traditions, and none has been named since 2014.
25. Kutztown, billed as America's oldest folklife festival (1950), died in 2024 just outside the MSA, the same year the Philadelphia Folk Festival came back.

## Curiosities (seeds for phase 2)

1. Irish sessions per 100k residents: Philadelphia has about 32 session meetings a month for 6.3M people. How does this compare with Boston, New York and Chicago, and does it track Irish-American population share or the presence of a cultural-center hub like the Irish Center?
2. Why does Scottish country dance thrive here (and in Atlanta) with no Scottish or Cape Breton fiddle session at all, while Boston and DC have both?
3. Session churn: thesession.org shows about 19 dead Philadelphia-area listings (2002-2023) against 14 live in-MSA ones. Do sessions in cultural centers outlast pub sessions in every city?
4. Why is the trad scene concentrated in Mt. Airy and the Main Line while South Philly, the Northeast and South Jersey have almost nothing? Is a single 'folk neighborhood' typical (Cambridge/JP, Glen Echo, Phinney Ridge)?
5. Is brewery- and restaurant-hosted old-time jamming a national post-2015 pattern?
6. Why did staffed Philadelphia folk institutions (PFS with an executive director and a building; the Philadelphia Folklore Project; the 2018-2023 Philadelphia Folk School) become fragile while Delaware's all-volunteer Brandywine Friends has run continuously since 1971? Compare staffed vs volunteer societies across metros.
7. How often do flagship folk radio shows survive the loss of a long-time host (WXPN 2026 vs WUMB, WFMT, WAMU)?
8. Shape-note singings per million residents: compare Philadelphia (city that printed the first Sacred Harp; ~4 local singings a month) with Atlanta (heartland; ~2-3 local practice singings but ~20 annual all-days), Boston, Chicago and the Twin Cities.
9. Do other metros have a competitive amateur folk-music parade with instrument rules (Mummers string bands vs Mardi Gras Indians, West Indian Day), and how has membership trended?
10. Is Philadelphia's Irish trad lineage measurably more Ulster than New York's (Sligo/Clare) or Boston's (Galway)?
11. Why does a 6.3M metro with a 97-year-old Old Fiddlers' Picnic have no fiddle contest?
12. Is the role of Quaker and Mennonite meetinghouses as folk venues unique to southeastern Pennsylvania, or do church fellowship halls play the same role elsewhere (as in Atlanta's church coffeehouses)?
13. Why did the Hmong leave Philadelphia but thrive in St. Paul and Fresno? Are there other 'lost' diaspora communities in big metros?
14. NEA Heritage Fellows per million residents by metro: Philadelphia has 9 for 6.3M and none since 2014. Does an active state folklife program explain the differences?
15. Where are the region's at-risk recordings (Bill and Fritzi Wisdom's jam tapes, Sunset Park broadcasts, PFS festival tapes after the 2023 move)?

## Appendix B. Tunes named for or tied to the metro

- **Wissahickon Drive** (Irish (reel)): Composed by Liz Carroll (Chicago); named for a street in NW Philadelphia; in 258 Session tunebooks [link](https://thesession.org/tunes/1769)
- **Crossing the Delaware** (Irish (reel)): Liz Carroll and Sean McGlynn; on Carroll's 1988 first solo CD; title referent not verified [link](https://thesession.org/tunes/5507)
- **Big Red (alias 'Philadelphia', 'Philadelphia Reel')** (contra/Irish (reel)): Composed by Philippe Varlet (DC area) for his red fiddle; renamed 'Philadelphia Reel' in circulation; not actually from Philadelphia [link](https://thesession.org/tunes/1983)
- **The Hunter's House (and other Ed Reavy tunes: Never Was Piping So Gay, Fisherman's Island, Maudabawn Chapel, The Man from Barnagrove, In Memory of Coleman, Castle Bray)** (Irish): Composed in Philadelphia by Ed Reavy [link](https://thesession.org/tunes/472)
- **Eugene O'Donnell's** (Irish (slip jig)): By James MacCafferty for the Derry/Philadelphia fiddler [link](https://thesession.org/tunes/7550)
- **Kevin McGillian's** (Irish (jig)): Composed by Philadelphia-area accordionist Kevin McGillian [link](https://blackwatermusic.com/music/blackwater/track/tonroes-ciaran-tourishs-kevin-mcgillians/)
- **Taylor's Hornpipe** (Irish (hornpipe)): Possibly named for Philadelphia piper/pipe-maker Billy Taylor (Touhey's teacher) [link](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Taylor%27s_Hornpipe_(2))
