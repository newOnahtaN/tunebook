# Detroit: the folk scene of metro Detroit

*Detroit-Warren-Dearborn, MI MSA (CBSA 19820; Wayne, Oakland, Macomb, Livingston, St. Clair and Lapeer counties; 4.39 million people in 2025). Ann Arbor/Washtenaw County, Lansing and Windsor, Ontario, are outside the MSA but part of the scene; they are flagged `outside_msa` throughout. Research as of October 2026. Companion data: `detroit.json`.*

## The scene in a nutshell

Metro Detroit is a heritage-music giant with a thin Anglo-Celtic trad scene. Inside the six-county MSA the verified participatory calendar is only about **50 events a month**: one weekly Irish pub session (in Plymouth), one monthly contra (in Troy), a few bluegrass jams, and, surprisingly, twice-weekly international folk dance as the busiest trad activity. Ann Arbor, a separate 370,000-person MSA 40 miles west, runs about as many trad events as all of metro Detroit and hosts The Ark, the region's real folk club. Yet Detroit's folk history is among America's richest: French habitant fiddling, Henry Ford's 1920s dance crusade, Lomax's 1938 recordings of Serbian gusle and Delta blues, a Cape Breton fiddling hub, the Hillbilly Highway, Joni Mitchell's coffeehouse years. Its living traditions are Arab and Chaldean music in Dearborn, about 550 Polish folk-dance students, tamburica and bandura orchestras, Black gospel quartets, blues jams, and a new Detroit Folk School reviving square dance.

## The 10 most interesting things

1. **The college town out-dances the metro.** Washtenaw County (Ann Arbor, ~370,000 people) runs about as many trad events a month as the entire Detroit MSA. Detroit's own Comhaltas branch, the Detroit Irish Music Association, has held its weekly flagship session in Ann Arbor since 2010.
2. **Henry Ford's dance crusade, and its myth.** Ford put dance musicians on the Ford Motor Company payroll for 23 years (1924–47), brought dancing master Benjamin Lovett to Dearborn, published *Good Morning* (1926) and built Lovett Hall (1937) for quadrilles, partly as an Americanization program against dances "from the African Kongo." But he **never sponsored a fiddle contest**: the famous 1926 "Ford contests" were run by dealers, and the myth stuck. His "American" orchestra's star was a Romani cimbalom player, Billy Hallup.
3. **A lost contra hall.** Lovett Hall hosted the "longest-running dance series in Michigan" from 1981 until The Henry Ford ended it on four days' notice in February 2005. It now hosts weddings.
4. **A Detroit tune went south.** Detroit dancing master Adam Couse's 1854 "Detroit Schottische" reportedly sold ~100,000 copies, and fiddle historian Paul Gifford thinks the Southern old-time staple "Flop-Eared Mule" descends from it (others argue a common ancestor).
5. **Lomax's Detroit is not what you'd expect.** Of 116 Detroit-tagged items from the 1938 Library of Congress expedition, the music is Serbian gusle epics, tamburitza, Hungarian soldiers' songs and Delta blues by Calvin Frazier and Sampson Pittman, a decade before John Lee Hooker (a Ford worker) cut "Boogie Chillen'."
6. **Detroit was a Cape Breton fiddling capital.** The Five MacDonald Fiddlers, including composer Dan R. MacDonald, formed in Detroit; their elder, Johnny Archie MacDonald, was a Detroit police officer whose Saturday "down east" dances featured Buddy MacMaster and Winston Fitzgerald. There is a Cape Breton jig called "The Detroit." No Cape Breton session survives.
7. **The contra revival began in a museum** (an impromptu contra at the Detroit Historical Museum's "Galactic Steam Dulcimer Circus," Nov 13, 1976), and the newest dance is the **Detroit Folk School's monthly Queer Square Dance** in Southwest Detroit, described locally as "dancing on Henry Ford's grave."
8. **Gospel is the biggest singing tradition, and it never called itself folk.** The Gospel Music Workshop of America was founded in Detroit in 1967; Mattie Moss Clark made Detroit the center of the mass-choir sound; quartets still hold anniversary programs on 8 Mile and, since 2024, sing outside the Motown Museum. Meanwhile there is **no Sacred Harp singing in the MSA**, even though *The Sacred Harp* has a tune called "Detroit."
9. **The heritage honor roll.** Seven of about 24 metro music/dance Michigan Heritage Award winners are blues artists; the Ukrainian Bandurist Chorus, which immigrated as an entire choir of 17 families in 1949, became Detroit's first ensemble NEA Heritage Fellow in 2025; Howard "Louie Bluie" Armstrong (NEA 1990) is the metro's fiddler fellow.
10. **Heritage outlives its neighborhoods.** About 550 students attend 14 Polish folk-dance schools (2025 census) even as Hamtramck, now majority Arab and South Asian, cheers a parody polka band called "The Polish Muslims." A Dearborn public elementary school has had an Arabic music ensemble since 2002, and the National Arab Orchestra (2009) runs a takht ensemble and a community choir.

## Character: what makes Detroit different

**A heritage metro, not a revival metro.** The auto industry pulled in dozens of peoples at once, and many never left the region, only the old neighborhoods. The living folk culture is overwhelmingly community heritage, run out of parishes, fraternal lodges and suburban cultural centers: the Croatian lodge in Warren, the Ukrainian Cultural Center, the Arab American National Museum in Dearborn, Polish PRCUA dance schools, the St. Andrew's Society (1849) in Troy. Much of it happens in closed weekly rehearsals and at annual festivals rather than in open weekly events, which is why the event count is low while the tradition count is high.

**The revival scene lives in church halls and the suburbs, and partly in Ann Arbor.** The listening rooms are a 70-seat former church theatre in Livonia (Trinity House), a Unitarian church coffeehouse in Bloomfield Hills (MAMA's, cash or check only since 1993), a Plymouth arts council (BaseLine) and a struggling Hamtramck bar (Ghost Light). The trad dance and song scene runs along the Oakland County I-75/Woodward corridor (Troy, Royal Oak, Southfield, Rochester Hills) and western Wayne County (Plymouth, Livonia, Westland, Redford). Detroit proper hosts the Gaelic League cluster in Corktown, a Southwest Detroit square dance, a house jam and a DIY folk festival. When people say "the folk club" they often mean The Ark (1965), 40 miles west.

**Black traditions carry the largest participatory song culture.** Gospel choirs, quartet anniversaries, lined-out hymnody and blues jams are the metro's biggest "traditional singing" practice, mostly church- and bar-based and very hard to count from the web.

**Costs.** Contra $12 (OCTDS); Ann Arbor contras pay-what-you-can ($10–15 suggested); céilí, set and Scottish classes $10; sessions and jams free or $5; open stages $0–7; listening rooms $20–25; MAMA's $20 cash; house concerts by donation (~$20, low confidence); gospel anniversaries free with an offering.

**Who shows up.** OCTDS says about a third of dancers at a typical contra are beginners; the international folk dance club is led by dancers active since the 1970s; Polish dance schools enroll children whose parents come from Honduras, the Philippines and China as well as Poland; the Detroit Folk School draws a young queer crowd. No demographic surveys were found.

## A visiting fiddler's week

- **Sunday:** the Gaelic League's radio session (musicians welcome on air at 2:30, session 3–5 on select Sundays), then Conor O'Neill's in Ann Arbor at 6 (outside the MSA; the state's longest-running session, started by Mick Gavin). Or Empty Chair Night at Trinity House; Sacred Harp at The Ark on 2nd Sundays; or a gospel quartet anniversary program.
- **Monday:** international folk dance teaching night in Royal Oak (7:30), or the Cadieux Cafe blues jam with feather bowling.
- **Tuesday:** Trinity House Open Stage in Livonia (1st/3rd; $5; arrive early), Ghost Light songwriter night in Hamtramck (free), Thowra's dabke class in Dearborn, the Westland bluegrass jam (1st/3rd), or Ann Arbor's English country dance.
- **Wednesday:** the best Irish night: Seán O'Callaghan's in Plymouth (5–7:30), then on 1st Wednesdays the DIMA session at the Gaelic League (6:30–9).
- **Thursday:** DIMA's session in Ann Arbor, Irish set dancing in Corktown, RSCDS in Troy, or the Detroit Old Time Jam (2nd Thursday, if running).
- **Friday:** international folk dance in Southfield or Troy, or a first-Friday ceilidh at the Windsor Scottish Club.
- **Saturday:** Paint Creek Folklore Society's song swap and jam (1st Saturdays, Rochester Hills), BaseLine in Plymouth (3rd), the OCTDS contra in Troy (usually 4th), the Queer Square Dance in Southwest Detroit, or Ann Arbor's Grange contras (1st/3rd).
- **Plan a trip around:** Detroit Highland Games and ceilidh (early August), Concert of Colors (July), Motor City Irish Fest (summer), Armenia Fest (August), Ann Arbor Folk Festival (late January; the 50th is Jan 29–30, 2027), Paczki Day in Hamtramck (polka from 10:30 am).
- **Pilgrimages:** Lovett Hall and the Benson Ford Research Center in Dearborn; the Chess Mate site on Livernois; Mariners' Church (Lightfoot's "Maritime Sailors' Cathedral").
- **Learn before you go:** "Detroit Schottische," "Flop-Eared Mule," "The Detroit" (Cape Breton jig), "Little Judique Jig," "March of St. Timothy" (named for the church hall of the first Detroit contra series), and a waltz and schottische for any Michigan jam.

## Part 1. Trad instrumental and dance scene

### Irish sessions: a Corktown clubhouse, a Plymouth pub, and an Ann Arbor church

**The Gaelic League / Irish American Club** (2068 Michigan Ave, Corktown; [est. 1920](https://www.gaelicleagueofdetroit.org/)) is the symbolic home of Irish music in Detroit proper. Its events feed (a WordPress events API, read directly) shows a busy calendar:

- **1st-Wednesday DIMA session**, 6:30–9 pm: an hour of slow session, then a round-robin open session. Free for members, $5 suggested otherwise ([DIMA](https://www.detroitirishmusic.org/play)).
- **Sunday sessions, 3–5 pm**, on roughly two Sundays a month (Jun 28, Jul 26, Aug 16, Aug 30, Sep 13, Sep 27, Oct 11, Nov 1 2026). They follow Kathleen O'Neill's *Detroit Irish Radio Show*, broadcast live from the club on CRB Radio from 1 to 3 pm. Musicians are invited to arrive at 2:30 and play on air ([Gaelic League events](https://www.gaelicleagueofdetroit.org/events/)). A trad session that doubles as a live radio feed is a nice local quirk.
- **Irish set dancing with Anne McCallum**, Thursdays 7–9 pm, $10.
- **Kitty's Céilí** (Kitty Heinzman, Ardan Academy), now on a Wednesday at 8:30 pm, about monthly, $10.
- **Beginner step class**, Wednesdays at 5:30.
- **Irish Music Institute "Play in the Parade" kids' class**, Fridays at 5 pm. Every child gets a free tin whistle and marches in the St. Patrick's Parade.
- **Concerts**: Andy Irvine (Sep 17 2026, $30) and fiddler Gerry O'Connor with Don Penzien and Detroit uilleann piper Seán Gavin (Oct 29 2026).
- Note: buying alcohol at the bar may require Gaelic League membership.

**The Detroit Irish Music Association (DIMA)** is the metro's Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann branch, with roots in the 1970s. It says it has been "promoting and teaching traditional Irish music in the Detroit area for over 40 years," and was based at Ferndale's White Heather Club and then the Gaelic League ([DIMA about](https://www.detroitirishmusic.org/about)). The surprise: since **2010 its weekly flagship has been in Ann Arbor**. It ran at Green Wood UMC until 2025 and is now at Grace Ann Arbor North (3150 Glazier Way), Thursdays 7–10 pm from September to May. The first hour alternates between a member-led teach-a-tune and a slow session; tea is free and lessons run in side rooms. Adult membership starts at $30 a year. A CCÉ branch named for Detroit holds its main weekly session in another MSA.

**The Irish Music Institute (IMI)** is the newcomer, founded in September 2022 ([about](https://irishmusicinstitute.org/about/)). Its stated goals read like a scene-building plan for a city that lacks one:

- "establish a regular traditional music session in metro Detroit" (done: **Wednesdays 5–7:30 pm at Seán O'Callaghan's**, 821 Penniman Ave, Plymouth)
- an instrument-rental program
- a concert series for touring artists
- a festival

IMI also teaches on Tuesdays at the AOH hall in Redford and at the Knights of Columbus Monaghan Council in Livonia, and lists the long-running **Sunday 6 pm session at Conor O'Neill's** in Ann Arbor ([IMI](https://irishmusicinstitute.org/)). Conor's has been on thesession.org since February 2003; a September 2025 commenter called it "the only one on this site."

The Plymouth Wednesday is the **only weekly Irish pub session inside the MSA** that I could verify. thesession.org's "nearby" search (70 km around 42.40, −83.15) returns just three active sessions: Plymouth, Conor O'Neill's, and an East Lansing dance studio that is actually outside the radius. By comparison, the statewide miIrish.com Google Calendar shows weekly sessions in Grand Rapids/Conklin, Bay City and Traverse City. Detroit is no busier than much smaller Michigan towns.

**Graveyard of sessions.** thesession.org records a long string of Detroit sessions that ended:

- Baile Corcaigh in Corktown (c.2005–07). The Perkins family session (Asher on box and concertina, Alison on fiddle) moved to the Plymouth Crossing in the old Daisy air-rifle factory.
- Gus O'Connor's (c.2007–09).
- Dick O'Dow's in Birmingham (until 2013: "The bar decided to try some different music").
- The every-other-Friday "SessioNite" at the AOH in Redford (2008–c.2013), which ran "very late, sometimes 2a."
- A weekly Gaelic League session, May 2013 to its cancellation in June 2015.
- Windsor's Kildare House session, which mixed old-time with Irish and Scottish ("the pub is gone," 2013).
- The Ypsi Alehouse session (2016–21), which "didn't survive Covid."

([thesession](https://thesession.org/sessions/955))

### Contra: one series in Troy, a lost ballroom in Dearborn, a thriving scene in Ann Arbor

Inside the MSA there is essentially **one regular contra dance**: the **Oakland County Traditional Dance Society** (founded 1991, a CDSS affiliate). It dances monthly, usually on the 4th Saturday, at **First United Methodist Church of Troy**, 6363 Livernois, "air conditioned and has a wood floor" ([OCTDS about](https://www.octds.org/about)).

- **Admission:** $12. Members of OCTDS or another dance group and students get $2 off, bringing a snack earns another $1 off, and kids 12 and under are free.
- **Crowd:** "typically a third of the people at a dance are beginning contra dancers" ([FAQ](https://www.octds.org/frequently-asked-questions)).
- **Times:** the FAQ still says a 7:00 lesson and 7:30 dance, but recent dates start earlier (Oct 24 2026, 5:00–7:30 pm; next dance "November 29 at 5:30pm").
- **Website:** some OCTDS subpages now serve Indonesian casino spam, a small sign of how thinly the scene is staffed.

The same church hosts the RSCDS Thursday class and **A Starry Night for a Ramble**, a benefit contra and square dance. It is called by "Glen & Ed" to the Paint Creek Country Dance Orchestra, costs $10, and holds a silent auction ([Patch, May 16 2026](https://patch.com/michigan/troy/calendar/event/20260516/a6b9e7ec-0058-4d06-9887-cdb43159f8e2/starry-night-for-a-ramble-2026-benefit-contra-and-square-dance)). The 2025 edition was cancelled.

**Henry Ford's dance legacy.** Ford brought New England dancing master **Benjamin Lovett** and his wife Charlotte to Dearborn in 1924. A 1924 wire story said Ford wanted to "take the shimmy out of dance" ([Cow Hampshire](https://www.cowhampshireblog.com/2008/01/31/he-taught-henry-ford-how-to-dance-swanzey-new-hampshires-benjamin-b-lovett-1876-1952/)). Ford and Lovett published the 1926 manual *Good Morning*. In 1937 Ford opened **Lovett Hall**, a "recreation building" with a lavish ballroom where Lovett called monthly dances with Henry and Clara on the floor ([The Henry Ford](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/happy-anniversary-lovett-hall)).

That legacy carried directly into the modern revival. **Glen Morningstar and the Olde Michigan Ruffwater Stringband** ran the "Lovett Hall American Contradancing" series from **October 4, 1981**. In December 2004 Morningstar announced that the hall "will no longer be available for regularly scheduled weekend dances." He said the change had "all happened in the last 4 days." The last dance was **February 6, 2005**, ending "the longest-running dance series in Michigan" ([Mudcat](https://mudcat.org/thread.cfm?threadid=76289)). Today Lovett Hall hosts weddings on its "much rumored 'spring-loaded' dance floor," and I found **no public old-time dancing** continuing there.

The Morningstar line lives on elsewhere:

- Glen is historian of the Paint Creek Folklore Society and probably the "Glen" calling Starry Night.
- *The Ruffwater Fakebook* is a core source for Ann Arbor's Pittsfield Open Band.
- Judi Morningstar plays for Ann Arbor's Grange contras.

**Ann Arbor (outside the MSA)** is where a Detroit contra dancer actually goes. **AACTMAD** has 200+ members and runs 100+ events a year; it is a CDSS affiliate and a founding member of what became Folk Alliance International ([AACTMAD](https://aactmad.org/who-we-are)). Its Google Calendar doubles as Michigan's de facto trad calendar. A typical month:

- First Saturday and third Saturday contras at the Pittsfield Union Grange, pay what you can, $10–15 suggested
- a second-Saturday Contra Dance Party at Concourse Hall
- weekly Tuesday English country dance, plus a monthly advanced ECD
- a monthly free French-Canadian session ("Knowledge of French is not required")
- Monday Scottish country dance (Tartan & Thistle, which offers carpools from Dearborn)
- first-Sunday international folk dance
- the Pittsfield Open Band, an open-band practice before the First Saturday dance
- dance weekends: Melt into Spring and Apple Blossom Bliss
- a new-caller collective (Lisa Greenleaf led a weekend workshop in April 2026)

Ann Arbor Morris celebrated **50 years** with a Grand Tour on Sep 27 2026. **No morris, sword or rapper team, no regular ECD series, and no dedicated Scandinavian group turned up inside the Detroit MSA.**

### International folk dance: the quiet powerhouse

The **International Folk Dance Club of Detroit** dances twice every week:

- **Mondays, 7:30 pm**, at the Mahany/Meininger Senior Center in Royal Oak.
- **Fridays, 7 pm**, alternating between the **Villa Pointe condo clubhouse** in Southfield and the **Balkan American Community Center** in Troy.

Its Google Group posts every playlist; the most recent is from Oct 29 2026 ([group](https://groups.google.com/g/international-folk-dance-club-detroit/c/UkAQ8DsN_QY)). The Halloween set ran Hora de Curcan, Kritikos Syrtos, Eleno Mome, Biserka, Sardinia's Passu Turrau, Kyrgyz Kara Jorgo and Kenya's Nyandolo, plus an "Addams Family Line Dance" and "Reggae Cowboy, to the tune of 'Ghostbusters'."

Leader **Rick King** first danced at the Detroit Folkdance Club in 1973 and later in Alex Cushnier's Wayne State group. He began teaching in 1990, organized workshops with local Chaldean and Lebanese ethnic groups, and merged the Wayne State and Ferndale groups into a weekly Friday night ([DCFF bio](https://www.dcff.net/staff/bios/king-rick-bio.html)). He is also a serious birder with a life list of 2,727. The club's newsletter promotes the Ann Arbor Balkan band **Ajde Aman**, which sells out the Blue Llama, and the Flint-area Macedonian "picnic" with live music.

### Scottish and the lost Cape Breton Detroit

**RSCDS Detroit** (founded 1980) dances Thursdays 7:30–9:30 in Troy for $10, with the first night free and anniversary balls ([RSCDS Detroit](https://www.rscdsdetroit.org/about-us/)). Across the river, **RSCDS Windsor** has been dancing "for over 50 years." It meets Wednesdays at Mackenzie Hall and holds a **first-Friday ceilidh at the Windsor Scottish Club** ([RSCDS Windsor](http://rscdswindsor.org/events/)).

The **St. Andrew's Society of Detroit** (founded 1849; "oldest benevolent organization in the State of Michigan") claims the **longest continuous Highland Games in North America**. It runs the Kilgour Scottish Centre in Troy, which holds occasional ceilidhs ([highlandgames.com](https://highlandgames.com/)).

The deeper story is Cape Breton. A 1973 *Scotia Sun* profile describes **Johnny Archie MacDonald**, who was born in 1893, grew up in Little Judique Ponds and moved to Detroit in 1924. He became a Detroit police officer and "nursed Scottish music through a long succession of music crazes." His "down east dances, which featured such Cape Bretoners as Angus Chisholm, Cameron Chisholm … Winston Fitzgerald, Donald MacLellan and Buddy MacMaster, have thrilled Detroiters on many a Saturday night" ([Electric Scotland](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/canada/macdonald_johnny.htm)). Today I found **no Cape Breton or Scottish fiddle session** on either side of the river.

### Old-time and bluegrass: thin on the ground

**The Detroit Old Time Jam** ("Westside Detroit • Est. 2024") is a potluck at a wheelchair-accessible private home in Northwest Detroit, on the 2nd Thursday from 6 to 9 pm. Tunes are "mostly in G, A, C, or D, so bring a capo." Its small GitHub-hosted site posts the last session's tunes with Slippery-Hill links: Waynesboro, Elk River Blues, Big Scioty, Scott Number Two, Katy Hill, Soldier's Joy ([detroitoldtime.com](https://www.detroitoldtime.com/)). It has a sister "Ypsi Jam" on first Fridays. The posted schedule runs only through December 2025, so its 2026 status is unconfirmed. For a city built partly by Appalachian migration along the "hillbilly highway," that is strikingly little old-time music.

**Bluegrass.** The Southeast Michigan Bluegrass Music Association's jam list (updated Oct 8 2026) shows only two jams inside the MSA ([SMBMA](https://smbluegrass.org/jam-list/)):

- **Cirrus Guitars**, Sterling Heights: 2nd Thursday, 6–8 pm.
- **Dyer Senior Center**, Westland: 1st and 3rd Tuesday, 7–9 pm.

Ann Arbor's Senior Center adds 2nd and 4th Wednesdays, and the list has more West Michigan, Lansing and Ohio entries than Detroit ones. The Pontiac Trail Blazers have been the house band at Ann Arbor's Detroit Street Filling Station's "Bluegrass Friday" since 2017 ($10 cover). The **Golden Griffon Stringtet** notes that "the melodic combination of hammered dulcimer and fiddle has long been a staple of dance bands in Michigan."

### The folklore-society tradition

The **Paint Creek Folklore Society** (founded 1974, about 70 members) began when **John Carter**, a Detroit Folklore Society member since 1968, met **Vincent Sadovsky** at the Rochester Folk Workshop. Sadovsky opened the guitar shop in October 1973, and its weekend jams "proved that the folk spirit was alive and well in Oakland County" ([PCFS history](https://paintcreekfolkloresociety.org/about-us/)).

Gatherings run 2–9 pm on most first Saturdays in the Fellowship Hall of University Presbyterian Church, Rochester Hills. The same campus includes Lawnridge Hall, a 1928 mansion once meant for Dodge heir Danny Dodge, who died on his 1938 honeymoon. Each gathering has three parts:

1. a one-hour presentation
2. a themed, unamplified song swap (November 2026: "Rivers, the Seas, Sailors")
3. breakout "Circles & Jam Sessions"

Visitors pay $10, and the first visit is free. PCFS stages the 53rd Tin Whistle Concert on Nov 14 2026 ($20, with the RFD Boys).

### Squares and other dance

Modern western square dance survives in eight SEMA member listings, including the Clawsonaires (Ferndale), Tom Katz (Royal Oak), Sashay Strutters and Dudes and Dolls (Detroit), and Shindigger and Star Twirlers (Westland) ([SEMA](https://www.squaredancesema.com/member-clubs.html)). Old-time squares appear only inside OCTDS contras and the annual Starry Night.

Other searches came up empty or near-empty:

- **Klezmer:** the KlezMitten festival has run since 2024 at the International Institute in Midtown, with 7 bands in 2025 including a new Klezmer Academy, but I found no recurring klezmer jam.
- **Hungarian, Polish and other diaspora dance nights:** no recurring participatory event was verified. Those belong to the heritage thread.

## Part 2. Folk song, listening rooms, traditional singing, radio, instruction and shops

### Listening rooms and coffeehouses (inside the MSA)

**Trinity House Theatre (Livonia)** is the workhorse. It was founded in 1981 by Paul Patton and members of Trinity Baptist Church as a live-theatre venue, became its own nonprofit in 1988, and "switched to live music in 1999." It now calls itself "one of the state's best listening rooms" ([Trinity House](https://www.trinityhousetheatre.org/)). It is tiny: a volunteer told Schoolcraft College's paper it "seats 70 people" ([Schoolcraft Connection](https://schoolcraftconnection.com/33526/arts-and-entertainment/music-arts-community/)), while a booking directory says about 87. The October 2026 calendar still showed **35 events**. Touring acts (Anne Hills, Jon Shain, Calum Graham) cost $20–25, and Detroit songwriters such as Jo Serrapere and Kevin Meisel also headline ([events](https://www.trinityhousetheatre.org/events/)). Three participatory series make Trinity House the closest thing Detroit has to a folk-club clubhouse:

- **Open Stage**, 1st and 3rd Tuesdays (and any 5th Tuesday), 6pm, $5, hosted by songwriter Bob Knox. "Due to the recent increase in the number of participants, there is a 2 SONG GUARANTEE for the first 20 sign ups" ([Open Stage](https://www.trinityhousetheatre.org/event/open-stage-48/)). In 2026 that is a remarkable line for a suburban open mic.
- **Empty Chair Night**, monthly on Sunday at 7pm, $12: "Not just another open mic show… members of the audience [can] join the host and featured act on stage to share a song 'in-the-round' style." Dan Cafferty hosts; the format is "the brain-child of Joel Palmer" ([event](https://www.trinityhousetheatre.org/event/empty-chair-night-featuring-dan-cafferty-and-special-guest-october-4-2026/)). Some nights are themed, such as the songs of Paul Simon on Jan 4 2026.
- **Songwriters Anonymous**, 3rd Wednesdays at 7pm: a topic discussion, then each songwriter plays one song. The group also stages a $20 showcase ([Song Anon](https://songwritersanonymous.org/events-happenings/meetings/)).

**MAMA's Coffeehouse (Bloomfield Hills)** is the classic church coffeehouse. MAMA stands for *Michigan Acoustic Musicians' Alliance*, an idea of local songwriter David Walker. The first show was in May 1993 at Birmingham Unitarian Church on Woodward, nine shows a year from September to May. Jim Bizer has run it since 2001, and net proceeds benefit the church ([about](https://bucmi.org/music-choir/mamas-coffeehouse/about-mamas-coffeehouse/)). The house style is delightfully unchanged: "$20 regular, $18 student/senior. **No advance sales; cash or check at door only**." The fall 2026 bills were Reggie Harris, then Joe Jencks with Deidre McCalla on Nov 21 ([MAMA's](https://bucmi.org/music-choir/mamas-coffeehouse/)). That booking is the national folk-circuit A-list, the same artists who play Club Passim or the Ark.

**BaseLine Folk Society (Plymouth)** meets on third Saturdays, September to May, at the Plymouth Community Arts Council (774 N Sheldon). For $7 you get eight to ten open-mic spots of ten minutes plus a featured act. There is a lottery if it's oversubscribed, and "first-time performers at BaseLine always have a spot in the lineup" ([PCAC](https://www.plymoutharts.com/baseline-folk-society.html)). The October 2026 letter, signed "Warmly, Baseline Folk Society," introduces host Rick Pitts, whose love of music "began with singing cowboys and an electric-guitar version of 'Ghost Riders in the Sky.'" The 2026-27 season is billed as the 21st, but a 2016 Hometown Life piece called that year the 12th ([2016](https://www.hometownlife.com/story/life/2016/09/15/just-folks-baseline-begins-12th-season-live-music/90443720/)), which implies a 2005 start and probably a COVID gap.

**Ghost Light (Hamtramck)** is the city-side counterpart: a bar linked to Planet Ant theatre that hosts a **free weekly Tuesday Songwriter Night** run by Dave Toennies (sign-up 7, music 8, 18+). It also books bills like "Folk Noir" (Oct 14 2026: Emily Rose, Kate Hinote Trio, Carmel Liburdi). Its website pleads: "Ghost Light has been struggling. We could use your support" ([Ghost Light](https://ghostlighthamtramck.com/)). The Detroit News photographed the open mic in July 2026, when Toennies announced the August closure ([Detroit News](https://www.detroitnews.com/picture-gallery/media/photo/2026/07/21/songwriter-open-mic-night-at-ghost-light-in-hamtramck/90924858007/)).

Further out, Livingston County has a winter **Acoustic Cafe Concert Series** at the Howell Opera House ($17).

### House concerts

- **Inner Ring House Concerts (Ferndale).** Michelle and Ben Wilbert's 1920s bungalow "comfortably seat[s] 25 and up to 35 if we get cozy" for "Kitchen Party" concerts ([Wilbert](https://www.facebook.com/michelle.e.wilbert/music/)). After a four-year pandemic pause they were back by Sept 2024, and Rochelle Clark played Sept 27 2026 ([Bandsintown](https://www.bandsintown.com/e/108691472-rochelle-clark-at-inner-ring-house-concerts-ferndale-mi)).
- **Garfield House Concerts (Northville)** has run since 2003, about two shows a year. The 38th (Rachael Davis) was in June 2023 and the 39th (Aberdeenshire ballad singer Iona Fyfe) in August 2023. Sam Lewis played on Oct 25 2026 ([listing](https://undiscovered.music/venues/garfield_house_concerts)).
- **A Detroit House of Music (DHOM)** on 14th Street near Woodbridge is songwriter Audra Kubat's home. She "lives, hosts concerts and songwriting workshops, and tends to a community garden" there, and pairs Detroit songwriters with neighborhood youth ([Kubat](https://audrakubat.com/a-detroit-home-of-music)).

### The Detroit Folk Festival: a DIY institution reborn

Kubat founded the **Detroit Folk Festival** around 2012 at the Trumbullplex collective in Woodbridge. By 2018 it was a noon-to-11pm day at the Old Miami on Cass with **25 artists**, a Dave Toennies open mic and two "Songwriters in the Round" sets. Tickets were $7 or $10, and half the proceeds went to InsideOut Literary Arts. Kubat told Metro Times: "It is less important to me that the music sounds 'folky.' What I am interested in is the story and giving voice to those who may not be able to tell it" ([Metro Times 2018](https://www.metrotimes.com/music-2/audra-kubat-returns-with-the-detroit-folk-festival-16121574/)).

After a lapse, it was **relaunched on Sept 26 2026 "as a yearly gathering"** in DHOM's backyard garden, unveiling a new outdoor stage: "26+ songwriters in rounds," BYOB, croquet and horseshoes, a 50/50 raffle, a vegan food truck, and a "dance party and bonfire under the Harvest Moon." The lineup is a census of the city's songwriting community. It opened with a welcome by Jaike Spotted Wolf and ran through Lac La Belle (Jennie Knaggs and Nick Schillace's Appalachian-tinged duo), Jo Serrapere with John Devine, Chris Bathgate, Joe Reilly and a Dolly Parton tribute. Late at night came King Moore with poet jessica Care moore, rapper-poet Mahogany Jones, and DJ Roger Th@t at midnight ([schedule](https://happeningnext.com/event/the-detroit-folk-festival-eid3a0e45nscd)). It is folk as "an intersection of genre," and quintessentially Detroit.

Two weeks later the **6th Woodbridge Porch Concerts** (Oct 10 2026, free, noon to 6) turned that neighborhood's front steps into stages. The series was created during the pandemic ([WDET](https://wdet.org/2026/10/06/the-metro-created-during-the-pandemic-woodbridge-porch-concerts-is-back-for-the-6th-year/)).

### Community singing

Detroit's version of the Pete Seeger community-sing ethic is mostly one man. **Matt Watroba** was born in Detroit, raised in Plymouth, taught school for 12 years, and now lives in Farmington Hills. He leads **Community Sings** at the Farmington Community Library, roughly monthly on Tuesday evenings (Jun 30, Jul 28 and Oct 27 2026), free, in person and on Zoom ([Watroba calendar](https://mattwatroba.net/event/6700332/776327169/community-sings-with-matt-watroba)). He leads another on second Wednesdays in Ann Arbor ([flyer](https://interfaithspirit.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Community-Sing-Matt-Watroba-April-June-2026.pdf)). His communitysings.com project set out "to train at least 100 song leaders": "Community singing is not performing" ([Watroba](https://www.mattwatroba.net/community-sings)). "If you've ever attended a community sing in Michigan, there's a good chance that Matt Watroba was leading the program," WKAR wrote ([WKAR](https://www.wkar.org/podcast/inside-the-arts/2026-06-25/matt-watroba-marks-50-years-on-stage-40-years-on-the-radio)).

His story could be its own folk song. A ninth-grade English teacher played Tom Paxton's "Buy a Gun for Your Son," and the teenage prog-rock fan "sheepishly admits to stealing the record from the classroom." He later confessed to the teacher, and Paxton signed the record. His first paid gig in 1976 earned ten bucks and dinner at an American Legion fish fry.

In Ann Arbor there is also a monthly **Folk Song Jam Along** ([AADL](https://aadl.org/node/647044)), and the Gaelic League in Corktown hosts occasional song-session "Hootenannies."

### Folk radio: the station that let it go

Detroit's folk radio history is a cautionary tale. In summer 1986 Watroba, with "absolute zero experience in radio," talked his way into an interview with WDET program director Judy Adams. That November he started **Folks Like Us** ([WKAR](https://www.wkar.org/podcast/inside-the-arts/2026-06-25/matt-watroba-marks-50-years-on-stage-40-years-on-the-radio)). A 2004 format change cancelled it; WEMU in Ypsilanti carried it until it returned to WDET. Then in December 2009 WDET cancelled three weekend roots shows at once:

- Folks Like Us (23 years);
- Larry McDaniel's bluegrass show *Arkansas Traveler* ("30 or so" years, per [Mudcat](https://mudcat.org/thread.cfm?threadid=125953));
- Robert Jones' *Deep River*.

In the same schedule WDET added two shows from Ann Arbor's Rob Reinhart, including the syndicated **Acoustic Café** ([AnnArbor.com 2009](https://www.annarbor.com/entertainment/wdet-cancellations-could-land-at-wemu-while-acoustic-cafe-gets-new-home/)).

Seventeen years on, Acoustic Café (since 1995, "heard on 125 stations") is still WDET's folk-adjacent show, Sundays 1-3pm ([WDET](https://wdet.org/shows/acoustic-cafe/)). The 2024 local-music revamp added **MI Local** with Jeff Milo (Tue 9-10pm), which regularly features Detroit folk acts such as Laurel Premo and The Whiskey Charmers. It brought no folk show ([Metro Times](https://www.metrotimes.com/music-2/detroits-wdet-moves-toward-more-local-programming-35260196/)). WDET's world-music show *This Island Earth* lost its host when Ismael Ahmed, founder of the Concert of Colors, died on Jan 31 2026 at 78 ([MAB](https://www.michiganmedia.com/2026/02/06/in-memoriam-ismael-ahmed/)).

So **Detroit's best-known folk DJ broadcasts from East Lansing**. *Folk with Matt Watroba* has aired on WKAR, Sundays 6-8pm, since October 2017. He moved because there "was the Ten Pound Fiddle, there was Elderly Instruments, and there was constant folk programming on WKAR." He entered the Folk DJ Hall of Fame in 2019, and in June 2026 he marked "50 years on stage, 40 on the radio" with a concert at The Ark.

Other signals reaching Detroit:

- **WEMU 89.1's The Roots Music Project**, Sat 4-7 plus the "Roots Break," Fri noon-2. Bob Nolan took over in Sept 2026, "an expert in all styles of music (especially truck drivin' songs)" ([WEMU](https://www.wemu.org/wemu-news/2026-08-24/positive-changes-are-afoot-at-wemu)).
- **WCBN's Down Home Show**, Saturdays noon-3.
- **WCHB 1340 AM**, the gospel station. It signed on Nov 7 1956 from Inkster as the first Black-owned and operated radio station built from the ground up ([Detroit PBS](https://www.detroitpbs.org/news-media/one-detroit/the-history-of-wchb-the-first-black-owned-and-operated-radio-station-from-the-ground-up/)) and still calls itself "The Voice of the Church in Detroit."
- Across the river, **CJAM 99.1's Border City Roots** ("roots and folk music from both sides of the river") had Windsor singer Allison Brown as host. CJAM called her its "former host" in 2023, and I could not confirm the show is still on.

### Traditional singing

#### Sacred Harp: the hole in the map

No shape-note group meets inside the Detroit MSA. Fasola Michigan lists Lansing (1st and 3rd Mondays), two Kalamazoo singings, Grand Rapids, Ann Arbor and Ypsilanti ([fasolamichigan.org](https://fasolamichigan.org/)); none of them are in the six counties. I found no current "Michigan State Sacred Harp Convention." The region's annual all-day singings are Mid-Michigan (Lansing, May), Kalamazoo, and out-of-state conventions ([annual list](https://fasolamichigan.org/?page_id=266)).

The nearest singings are lovely, though. **Ann Arbor's group**, "the longest tradition of Shape Note Singing of any active group in the state," sings 1-4pm on second Sundays **inside The Ark** ([Ann Arbor](https://fasolamichigan.org/?page_id=183)). Its offshoot, the **Ypsi Shape Note Group**, sings 2nd and 4th Tuesdays at Riverside Arts Center from the 2025 *Sacred Harp* and the *Shenandoah Harmony*, with a newcomers' workshop each 2nd Tuesday. Its flyer calls the music "the Metal of Early-American Music" ([Riverside](https://www.riversidearts.org/shape-note-singing-ypsilanti/)). On **Oct 3 2026** the two groups held the **1st annual Ypsi-Arbor All-Day Sing**, 10 to 3 with a potluck ([event](https://www.riversidearts.org/event/1st-annual-ypsi-arbor-all-day-sing-2026/)).

The irony: *The Sacred Harp* contains a tune called **"Detroit"** (39t, "Bradshaw, 1820," words by Doddridge), sung at conventions as far away as Atlanta ([fasola index](https://fasola.org/indexes/1991/?p=39t)).

#### Shanties and Great Lakes song

The 2021 shanty boom never became a recurring Detroit pub sing. What I found were one-offs: a "Mead Tasting & Sea Shanty Night" at Otaku Detroit in Madison Heights in June 2025 ([SimpleTix](https://www.simpletix.com/e/friday-night-circus-mead-tasting-sea-shant-tickets-220817)), and library singalongs led by Grand Rapids-based shantyman Ben Traverse, including one in Commerce Township. This is despite a rich source tradition: Michigan Public reported on "the forgotten sea shanties of Black Great Lakes sailors" ([Michigan Public](https://www.michiganpublic.org/arts-culture/2021-02-05/the-forgotten-sea-shanties-of-black-great-lakes-sailors)).

The metro's most powerful maritime song ritual is in a church. **Mariners' Church of Detroit** (1842) is Gordon Lightfoot's "Maritime Sailors' Cathedral," where "the church bell chimed 'til it rang twenty-nine times." The church holds an annual Great Lakes Memorial Service each November. For the 50th anniversary in 2025 it added a Nov 7 song-and-story evening with Mike Fornes singing "The Wreck of the Edmund Fitzgerald" ([Mariners'](https://marinerschurchofdetroit.org/edmund-fitzgerald/)). Lightfoot's original lyric called it "a musty old hall in Detroit." Parishioners objected, and he sang "rustic" thereafter ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wreck_of_the_Edmund_Fitzgerald)).

#### Gospel quartets and choirs: the biggest song community

Detroit's quartet line runs from the **Flying Clouds of Detroit**. They began in 1929 as the teenage "Russell Street Usher Board Four" of Russell Street Baptist ([Gospel History group](https://www.facebook.com/groups/GospelHistory/posts/372184510740081/)) and became "the strongest singing jubilee-based quartet in the Wolverine State" in the 1940s ([Opal Nations notes](https://opalnations.com/files/Flying_Clouds_of_Detroit_Interstate_HT_CD_46_2002__Liner_Notes.pdf)). Their 1947 "Something Within Me" for John W. Gilbert's Detroit label Religious Recordings is, by Tony Heilbut's account, one of the great quartet records ([Cross Rhythms](https://www.crossrhythms.co.uk/articles/music/The_Flying_Clouds_Of_Detroit_Vintage_acappella_harmonizers_of_40s_and_50s/44507/p1/)).

The tradition is alive as community practice:

- The **Gospel Soul Touchers** invited people to their "53rd anniversary" on Aug 9 2026 at 661 W. 8 Mile, "enjoy some of Detroit's finest quartet groups! … FREE ADMISSION." A 2024 video calls that year's program the 49th, so take the numbering loosely.
- **Detroit United Quartet Ministries** co-presents the Motown Museum's **Divinity United: Gospel Quartet Showcase** on Rocket Plaza with "10+ gospel quartets from across the Metro Detroit area… reviving the rich history and ties between quartets and Motown artists" (Jul 27 2024; Jul 26 2025) ([Motown Museum](https://www.motownmuseum.org/event/calendar-of-events/)).
- A quartet radio personality, "The Electrifying Chris Moorer," was feted by "Detroit's Quartet Gospel Community" on Oct 18 2026.
- The **GMWA Quartet Division** is chaired by Bishop Kenneth Robinson Sr.

Choir gospel is bigger still. The **Gospel Music Workshop of America** was founded by Rev. James Cleveland in Detroit in 1967. Its first board meeting was in Detroit in March 1968, and its first national convention met that summer at King Solomon Church ([GMWA Detroit](https://www.gmwadetroit.org/about)). The Detroit chapter's **GMWA Detroit Mass Choir**, led by Iris A. Taylor, Ph.D., is "open to anyone." It recorded *Detroit Remembers* Vol. 1 (2001) and Vol. 2 (2006), and since 2011 it has traded visits every two years with its Chicago sister choir. **Dr. Mattie Moss Clark** (1925–1994) directed at Bailey Cathedral COGIC from 1958. She "made Detroit a center for the mass choir sound" and founded a conservatory ([Michigan Heritage Award citation](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-1E)); the Clark Sisters are the pop face of that church base. DetroitGospel.com's fall 2026 calendar of free church concerts, tributes and anniversaries shows the scale ([DetroitGospel.com](http://detroitgospel.com/)).

(Arab, Chaldean and other sung heritage traditions are left to the heritage thread.)

### A Black–white folk partnership that is Detroit's own

**Rev. Robert B. Jones Sr.** was born in Detroit and learned blues from his grandmother, who bought his first guitar. He hosted WDET's *Blues from the Lowlands* for 19 years and has pastored Sweet Kingdom Missionary Baptist Church since 2002. His 1990s "Blues for Schools" program became "American Roots Music in Education." He received the **2025 Michigan Heritage Award** ([MTAP](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=fa14de19-5465-4907-b4eb-2e823c438028)), presented at intermission of his show at The Ark, where "my children… surprised me with an a cappella song" ([Jones](https://www.revrobertjones.com/post/2025-michigan-heritage-award)). With Watroba, his partner of 30+ years, he founded **Common Chords** in 2019, "dedicated to using music and the arts to tear down the walls that divide us" ([Common Chords](https://www.commonchords.net/)). In November 2026 they tour Club Passim and the Clearwater coffeehouse in White Plains, so Detroit is exporting its own folk duo.

### Instruction

There is no "Old Town School" in metro Detroit, and DIME (the Detroit Institute of Music Education, 2014–2020) closed when its Denver partnership collapsed ([Metro Times](https://www.metrotimes.com/music-2/detroits-dime-music-school-abruptly-shutters-after-partnership-with-denver-school-dissolves-24661363/)). Song-relevant teaching is scattered:

- **DIMA (Comhaltas)** teaches *traditional singing* alongside fiddle, flute and pipes on Thursday evenings ([DIMA](https://www.detroitirishmusic.org/learn)).
- The **Irish Music Institute** (founded Sept 2022) offers virtual lessons with teachers in Ireland and North America, plus a Wednesday session at Sean O'Callaghan's in Plymouth ([IMI](https://irishmusicinstitute.org/about/)).
- The **Detroit Music Teachers Collective** (2012) was founded by Suzuki violinist Clara Hardie and Lac La Belle's Jennie Knaggs.
- Kubat's **DHOM** runs songwriting workshops.
- **Common Chords** works in schools.
- The **GMWA Detroit Chapter** is literally a workshop.

Fiddlers wanting folk-specific lessons drive to Ann Arbor (Oz's Music since 1990; Michigan Banjo in Ypsilanti) or Lansing (Elderly's teacher roster: $30 a half hour for fiddle, banjo or concertina). Don't confuse the "Michigan Academy of Folk Music" with a Detroit school: it is in Holland, on Lake Michigan.

### Shops and makers

Detroit's instrument trade leans classical:

- **Psarianos Violins** (Troy, 1976): Peter Psarianos began "in a Murphy-Bed closet in his Detroit apartment" ([CSC](https://thecscagency.com/psarianos-story-video/)).
- **Wilson Fine Violins** (Birmingham, 1992): Rob Wilson trained under Dennis Lake of Lake's Fine Violins.
- **Pursell Lutherie** (Ferndale): Andrew Pursell spent ten years as Wilson's repair manager, then co-founded **Bayberry Music**. Its 2013 storefront on Woodward stocked "guitars to ukuleles, banjos to violins" and went online-only in 2022 ([Pursell](https://www.purselllutherie.com/about)).
- **Gordy's Music** (Ferndale): vintage banjos, dobros and mandolins; runs the Michigan Guitar Show.
- **Huber Breese** (Fraser, 1972): a Martin dealer.
- **Zimnicki Guitars** (Allen Park): Gary Zimnicki has built archtops, mandolins and even ouds for 40+ years "in close proximity to the birthplace of the assembly line" ([Zimnicki](https://zimnicki.com/studio/)).

The folk-instrument heavyweights the scene relies on are all outside the MSA: **Elderly Instruments** (Lansing, 1972), **Companion Custom Banjos** (north Ann Arbor, 2015, tone rings and tuners for builders nationwide), and violin maker **Joseph Curtin**, west of Ann Arbor. Curtin is a 2005 MacArthur Fellow, and his "ex-Ricci" Curtin & Alf violin set a $132,000 auction record for a living maker ([Curtin](https://josephcurtinstudios.com/)).

### Losses

The most recent loss is **Otus Supply's Parliament Room** in Ferndale (Dec 2016 to Feb 27 2023), which closed abruptly ([Eater](https://detroit.eater.com/2023/2/28/23618615/otus-supply-ferndale-closes)). Behind it are the 1960s coffeehouses: the **Chess Mate** on Livernois, where Joni and Chuck Mitchell played; the Living End; the Retort; and the Raven Gallery in Southfield ([Freep](https://www.freep.com/story/news/local/michigan/detroit/2021/07/11/detroits-chess-mate-forgotten-incubator-joni-mitchell/7895998002/)).

## Part 3. Heritage and diaspora traditions

### How we know: the honor rolls

**NEA National Heritage Fellows.** On arts.gov, a [state filter for Michigan](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/list?state=MI) lists eight fellows. Four are in the Detroit MSA:

- **Yang Fang Nhu** (1988, Detroit), a Hmong weaver and embroiderer who grew, spun and backstrap-wove hemp in northern Laos before fleeing in 1978 ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/yang-fang-nhu)).
- **Howard "Louie Bluie" Armstrong** (1990, Detroit), the African American string-band fiddler. He worked in Detroit auto plants and body shops during WWII, went quiet for 20 years, then toured again from 1972 as "the last of the black string bands" with Martin and Bogan. He picked up some eight languages playing for immigrant audiences, and of the violin he said "It's just like a third arm to me" ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/howard-armstrong)). He is the fiddle angle on this whole thread.
- **Nadim Dlaikan** (2002, Southgate), a Lebanese nay player and flute maker who came over in 1969 backing the singer Samira Tawfik. The scholar Sally Howell called him "the artistic glue" of Detroit's patchwork Arab ensembles: "a self-taught Palestinian American, a recently arrived Lebanese... an Iraqi Christian who picked up his love of music in an Iraqi garage band, and a Turk who is still struggling to learn enough Arabic" ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/nadim-dlaikan)).
- **Ukrainian Bandurist Chorus of North America** (2025, Detroit), the first ensemble from Detroit to be named ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/ukrainian-bandurist-chorus-north-america)).

Just outside the MSA is **Wade Mainer** (1987, Flint). The banjo player from the Mainer's Mountaineers recordings moved north in 1953 to work for GM, which makes his biography the "Hillbilly Highway" in a single life ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/wade-mainer)). The other Michigan fellows (Kelly Church, Yvonne Walker Keshick and Art Moilanen) are from out-state Michigan.

**Michigan Heritage Awards.** The state awards were established in 1985 and are run by the MSU Museum's [Michigan Traditional Arts Program](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/programs/michigan-heritage-awards/); each comes with a plaque, a legislative tribute and $1,000. Of the 147 awardees listed through 2026, I count **43 in the six MSA counties**, about 29%; all are in `heritage.json`. About 24 of them are for music or dance, and the pattern is striking:

- **Blues dominates.** Sippie Wallace (1985), Howard Armstrong (1989), Little Sonny Willis (1992), Eddie Burns (1994), Alberta Adams (2010), Johnnie Bassett (2012) and Rev. Robert Jones (2025): seven blues artists.
- **Black sacred song** is next: Mattie Moss Clark (1990), Deacon Albert Likely (2000), Calvin Cooke (sacred steel, 2011), Thomas Kelly (a cappella gospel, 2016) and gospel broadcaster Deborah Smith Pollard (2021). Likely learned lined-out metered hymns from formerly enslaved relatives near Evergreen, Alabama, and found that "in virtually every church at least one person could perform metered hymns" when he reached Detroit ([MHA](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-3A)).
- **Diaspora musicians:** Dlaikan (1994), oud master Abdul Karim Bader (2006, a former music director of Iraqi Radio and Television; [MHA](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-4F)), Polish accordionist Stas Wisniach (2004), Slovenian polka king Gaylord Klancnik (2011), Armenian kanun player Ara Topouzian (2022), the Ukrainian Bandurist Chorus (2022) and conjunto bajo-sexto player Martin Solis Jr. (2019).
- **The fiddlers' connection:** Mick Gavin (2018, Redford), born in Meelick, Co. Clare, in 1945, came to Detroit in 1975. He started what MSU calls "the longest running session in the state" at Conor O'Neill's in Ann Arbor, and his students include Jeremy Kittel and Tyler Duncan ([MHA](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-77)).

There are odd delights too: the **Cadieux Cafe Feather Bowling Club** (1999), Hmong textile artist Ia Moua Yang of Warren (2005), Palestinian embroiderer Amneh Baraka of Dearborn (1989), and a Great Lakes mail-boat crew (J.W. Westcott, 2005).

### Arab and Chaldean metro Detroit: the strongest living tradition

The numbers are big: about 300,000 people of Middle Eastern descent in Southeast Michigan by 2007 ([Wikipedia, citing several studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Middle_Eastern_people_in_Metro_Detroit)). The music has institutions to match.

- **National Arab Orchestra.** Michael Ibrahim founded it in 2009; its first concert was April 4, 2009, at U-M's organ recital hall in Ann Arbor ([history](https://araborchestra.org/history/)). It now runs a full orchestra, the **NAO Takht Ensemble** (oud, qanun, nay, violin, riqq, tabla; founded 2010, [DIA](https://dia.org/events/arab-american-heritage-month-national-arab-orchestra-takht-ensemble)) and a community choir. "Building Bridges Through Music" teaches Arab classical music free after school to Detroit public school students ([U-M LSA](https://lsa.umich.edu/lsa/news-events/all-news/search-news/national-arab-orchestra-hits-the-right-note.html)), and its next concert is Nov 8, 2026 ([NAO](https://araborchestra.org/)). Ibrahim also gives oud, tabla and riqq lessons at the Arab American National Museum ([AANM](https://arabamericanmuseum.org/music-lessons/)). A new Arab America Ensemble was announced in September 2026 ([Arab America](https://www.arabamerica.com/arab-america-foundation-launches-arab-america-ensemble-to-celebrate-classical-arab-music/)).
- **Dabke.** This is the most accessible thing on this whole list. **Thowra Dabke**, "a community based dabke dance troupe that offers affordable classes," teaches Tuesday-night series in the AANM Annex at 13624 Michigan Ave. It covers both 12-count (two-stomp) and 6-count (one-stomp) Levantine dabke, and series are listed from 2023 through July 2026 ([Arab America](https://www.arabamerica.com/events/dabke-class-series-with-thowra-dabke-at-aanm-5/), [AllEvents](https://allevents.in/dearborn/dabke-class-series-with-thowra-dabke-at-aanm-july-2026/100001993465919227)). Performance troupes include **Mawtini Dabke** and the wedding-oriented **ArabianKnights**, and Palestine's El-Funoun troupe taught a Dearborn workshop in August 2025 ([Eventbrite](https://www.eventbrite.com/e/el-funoun-dance-troupe-dabke-workshop-tickets-1403394975149)).
- **In schools.** Since 2002, Maples Elementary in Dearborn has had an Arabic Music Ensemble ([Dearborn Schools](https://maples.dearbornschools.org/maples-drummers/)). I did not check for counterparts in other school districts.
- **Chaldean weddings** are their own folk circuit: zaffa drum crews, tamboura and keyboard, and the khigga and sheikhani line dances. "Chaldean weddings are the stuff of legends in metro Detroit... everyone wants an invitation" ([Chaldean News](https://www.chaldeannews.com/2024-content/2024/7/1/theres-no-wedding-like-a-chaldean-wedding)). The **Chaldean Cultural Center** in West Bloomfield bills itself as the world's first Chaldean museum ([ClickOnDetroit](https://www.clickondetroit.com/news/2019/12/03/metro-detroit-home-to-worlds-first-and-only-chaldean-museum/)).
- **Yemeni Hamtramck.** The Free Press described a wedding where "men led an ancient wedding dance in a circle... their own traditional blades aloft," which spilled into a procession through Hamtramck's streets ([Freep 2025](https://www.freep.com/story/news/columnists/john-carlisle/2025/05/30/yemeni-immigrants-make-a-new-homeland-in-hamtramck/78513835007/)). This is the bara' dagger dance. I found no public classes.
- **The lost festival.** From 1995 to 2012 the **Arab International Festival** drew about 250,000 people over three days, roughly three times Dearborn's population, to Warren Avenue. It was shut down after anti-Muslim protest groups made it "a costly liability" ([Arab American News](https://arabamericannews.com/2018/09/08/what-ever-happened-to-dearborns-arab-festival/)). Nothing that size has replaced it.
- **Concert of Colors.** Ismael Ahmed of ACCESS founded it in 1993 with New Detroit, partly in response to the 1982 killing of Vincent Chin ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/smithsonian-center-folklife-cultural-heritage/2026/07/15/concert-of-colors-detroit/)). It began as one day at Chene Park and grew to nine days by 2019. The 2026 festival was five free days in 13 Midtown venues, its first without Ahmed (1947–2026), and it ran with a Smithsonian Folklife partnership ([BridgeDetroit](https://www.bridgedetroit.com/detroits-concert-of-colors-enters-new-era-in-first-festival-without-founder/)). Sources disagree on the count: WDET called 2025 the "34th annual," while organizers market 2026 as "35 years."

### Polish Detroit: the dance schools outlived the neighborhood

The best single data point in this thread is a census by *The Polish Weekly*. At the end of 2025 there were **15 Polish folk dance groups with 601 students and 15 choreographers**, and all 15 directors answered the survey. Removing Grand Rapids' Malbork (50 students) leaves about 550 in metro Detroit ([overview](https://polishweekly.com/detroit-area-polish-folk-dance-groups-in-2026-an-overview/), [group reports](https://polishweekly.com/detroit-area-polish-folk-dance-groups-in-2026-individual-reports-from-15-dance-groups/)).

- Ten groups belong to the PRCUA fraternal, three to the PNA, one to a parish (Dunajec, at Our Lady of Częstochowa in Sterling Heights) and one is independent (Wawel).
- The largest are PRCUA Zajączek (99 students; its families include parents from Honduras, the Philippines and China), Dunajec (about 80, with all instruction in Polish) and the PNA Centennial Dancers (60).
- The oldest is **Wieliczka** (Dearborn, 1955), now down to 12 students, "fourth or fifth generation," who practice at a Polish Legion of American Veterans post in Dearborn Heights. Wieliczka and Tatry both blame "the changing demographics" of Dearborn and Dearborn Heights.
- Adult groups: Wawel (1965, the first), Zakopane, Polanie (practicing Saturday mornings at St. Thomas the Apostle, Garden City, and growing to 22 dancers for the Rzeszów world festival in July 2026) and Kozice.
- Eleven of the 15 groups are holding steady or growing. Their worries are PRCUA subsidy cuts, tariffs on costumes and Saturday sports.
- Every year more than 100 dancers in costume have their ensembles blessed at St. Hyacinth in Detroit; 2026 was the 23rd time.

Polka is another story: I would call it **fading**. Polka now shows up mainly as party culture: on Paczki Day, Hamtramck's "favorite party band," **The Polish Muslims** (a parody polka-rock act playing for 45 years), works a circuit of halls starting at Ant Hall at 10:30 a.m. ([Hamtramck Review](https://www.thehamtramckreview.com/hamtramck-gets-ready-for-it-annual-mardi-gras-paczki-day/)). Hamtramck now has a "Bangladesh Avenue" and halal grocers ([NYT 2019](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/03/06/us/paczki-day-fat-tuesday-facts-history.html)). The **Polish Day Parade** began "in the 1930s on Belle Isle," moved to Jefferson Avenue, then to Joseph Campau on Labor Day, and in 2022 left Hamtramck for Warren's Birthday Bash after years of friction over police costs ([parade committee](https://www.polishdayparade.com/a-letter-from-judge-john-m-chmura/), [Hamtramck Review](https://thehamtramckreview.com/losing-the-polish-parade-opens-a-door-for-a-new-one/)). The Slovenian, Cleveland-style polka side had its peak with the Klancnik Brothers Orchestra, founded in 1976 at the Slovene American Club in Melvindale ([MHA](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-60)). One more twist: according to Wikipedia, Macedonians run most of Hamtramck's "Polish" bakeries ([Ethnic groups in Metro Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Metro_Detroit)).

### Eastern Europe in the suburbs: bandura, tamburica and kolo

- **Ukrainian.** In 1949 the whole **Ukrainian Bandurist Chorus** (17 families) was sponsored out of German refugee camps by the impresario Sol Hurok and Detroit lawyer John Panchuk, and most settled in Hamtramck. Today it has about 50 men aged 16 to 70, some of them third-generation bandurists, and its regular concerts draw "hundreds" at the Ukrainian American Archives & Museum, the Ukrainian Cultural Center in Warren (opened 1978) and Orchestra Hall ([MHA](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-85)). The museum was founded in 1958 and is now in Hamtramck ([Freep 2025](https://eu.freep.com/story/news/local/michigan/2025/03/25/ukrainian-museum-hamtramck-detroit/82545211007/)). Ukrainian folk dance looks **niche**: Echoes of Ukraine takes event bookings, and I could not verify a current class schedule.
- **Croatian tamburica.** The **Detroit Tamburitza Orchestra** began as a 1957 note-reading class under Andy Benda at Northern High School and gave its first concert on June 13, 1959. It is still run out of "Zora" Lodge 351 in Warren, together with a junior orchestra (from 1972) and the Nova Nada folklore ensemble, and its spring concerts continue (April 2024, [Croatians Online](https://croatiansonline.com/en/detroit-tamburaski-orkestar-najavio-proljetni-koncert/)). In 1967 director Steve Pavlekovich wrote a "Romance for Balalaika and Tambura" for a joint concert with the Detroit Balalaika Orchestra ([DTO history](https://www.zoralodge351.com/clubs-dto-history-eng/)).
- **Serbian.** **Rastko** (1993) brings together three Serbian parishes and grew out of a group shared with Windsor's St. Dimitrije. It has more than 60 dancers in three age groups, practices on Fridays and hosts the Rastko Winter Dance Festival in Warren ([Dečanski](https://www.decanski.org/folklore); that page is circa 2020).
- **Hungarian.** The Delray Hungarians scattered to Allen Park, Lincoln Park and Taylor after I-75 cut through the neighborhood. Today there is the **Csipke Ensemble**, which runs the week-long Csipke Tábor dance and music camp at Sauk Valley ([HFA](https://hungarianfolklife.org/category/folk-dance-and-music-camp/)), and the Hungarian Arts Club (1958).
- **Slovak.** Milan Straka has led the **Šarišan Slovak Folk Ensemble** since founding it in 1971 ([Šarišan](https://sarisanusa.com/)).
- **Other groups.** Macedonian, Bulgarian and Romanian traditions are mostly **historic**: in the 1920s Detroit had the largest Romanian population in the US, but I found no current ensemble. Albanians have the largest community outside Europe in Macomb County and a Hart Plaza festival, but no ensemble I could verify.

### Other old-stock heritages

- **Scottish.** The St. Andrew's Society of Detroit, founded in 1849 and "the oldest benevolent organization in the State of Michigan," runs what it calls "the longest continuous Games in North America." The 2026 Games were on August 1 at Greenmead in Livonia, preceded by a Friday-night cèilidh in a tent; earlier sites include Belle Isle and Ford Field ([Highland Games](https://highlandgames.com/about-highland-games/)). The 1849 start date is the Society's own claim, so medium confidence. There are three or more pipe bands with weekly practices in Troy: the St. Andrew's band at the Kilgour Scottish Centre, Celtic Pipes and Drums (Tuesdays, 6:30–8:30), and Michigan Scottish Pipes and Drums (1980, a Grade 4 teaching band) ([Michigan Scottish](https://michiganscottish.com/about-us)).
- **Irish.** The Gaelic League, founded in December 1920 and at 2068 Michigan Ave in Corktown since the early 1950s, holds Wednesday céilí and Ardán step-dance nights. It hosts the Detroit Irish Music Association's first-Wednesday sessions and Kathleen O'Neill's Detroit Irish Radio Show ([DIMA](https://www.detroitirishmusic.org/home)). The Layer-1 thread covers the sessions.
- **Armenian.** The Hamazkayin **Arax** dance ensemble (2005, choreographer Nayiri Karapetian) drew more than 400 people to its November 2024 concert. **Armenia Fest** in Royal Oak was in its 17th year in 2025 and ran again in 2026 ([Armenian Weekly](https://armenianweekly.com/2026/10/07/armenia-fest-2026-brought-armenian-culture-to-royal-oak/)).
- **Greek.** Greek dance lives in parish troupes (Nativity in Plymouth fields four age groups), in parish festivals such as Ya'ssoo in Bloomfield Hills, and on Monroe Street. The **Greektown Heritage Festival** returned on July 25, 2026, after a year off, with "the most people we've ever had" ([ClickOnDetroit](https://www.clickondetroit.com/news/local/2026/07/26/most-people-weve-ever-had-greektown-heritage-festival-returns-with-record-crowds-new-pedestrian-monroe-street/)).
- **Finnish.** The Finnish Center in Farmington Hills (1966, more than 500 members) supports FinnFolk/Soittoniekat, a group of woodwinds, strings and accordion; the Finnish American Singers; and Sunday Nordic Heritage Folk Dancers practices with a family hour, then an adult hour ([FCA](https://www.finnishcenter.org/our-story)).
- **Maltese.** The Maltese festa il-Vitorja reached its 15th year in 2024 in Corktown ([Order of Malta](https://orderofmaltaamerican.org/2024/09/12/michigan-area-joins-maltese-american-community-at-15th-annual-festa-il-vitorja-in-detroit/)).
- **Belgian.** Belgian Detroit survives in the **Cadieux Cafe**, which claims the only feather-bowling lanes in the US. Leagues play Tuesday through Thursday, 7–10 p.m., September to April. On Monday nights the same room hosts the "Eastside Blue Mondays" open blues jam ($5) ([Cadieux](https://www.cadieuxcafe.com/featherbowling/), [Detroit Blues Society](https://www.detroitbluessociety.org/open_jams.htm)).

### Latino, Native, Black and Caribbean Detroit

- **Mexican.** Folklórico is **thriving**. Ballet Folklórico de Detroit, founded in 2013 by Lance Rodriguez and described as "one of Michigan's largest volunteer-run folklorico dance groups" ([Freep](https://www.freep.com/story/entertainment/arts/2023/07/07/ballet-folklorico-de-detroit-dance-practice-space/70294293007/)), plays the DIA and the Senate Theater. Wayne State's folklórico ran six Tuesday summer sessions for teenagers in Clark Park in 2025. The **61st Cinco de Mayo Parade** (May 3, 2026) ran 2.4 miles down West Vernor ([MPC](https://www.mpcdetroit.org/cincodemayo/)). Mariachi is **active** (Mariachi Juvenil Detroit, Sunday sets on Bagley). Tejano conjunto is **fading** since Martin Solis Jr. (1929–2019) of Melvindale died; he hand-transcribed about 1,000 songs and was the first Michigan-based inductee of the Tejano R.O.O.T.S. Hall of Fame ([MHA](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-7A)). Grupo Azteca, a band active since 1974, plays on.
- **Not found:** son jarocho fandangos, Danza Azteca circles, Puerto Rican bomba and plena, and Filipino rondalla (the active rondalla is U-M's in Ann Arbor). I am recording these as not verified, which is not the same as absent.
- **Native.** American Indian Health and Family Services runs **Pow Wow in the D** each May, at Wayne State since 2024 ([AIHFS](https://aihfs.org/pow-wow-2025/)). On October 13, 2025, Hart Plaza hosted the first downtown powwow in more than 30 years, with over 200 dancers ([Freep](https://www.freep.com/story/news/local/michigan/detroit/2025/10/14/native-american-powwow-downtown-detroit-2025/86626495007/), [Michigan Public](https://www.michiganpublic.org/arts-life/2025-10-10/powwow-returns-to-downtown-detroit-for-first-time-in-30-years)). The same year American Indian Services closed after 49 years ([Bridge](https://bridgemi.com/children-families/end-era-american-indian-services-closes-after-49-years/)).
- **Black traditions as community practice.**
  - Blues: Lomax recorded Delta blues in Detroit on November 1, 1938, from Calvin Frazier and Sampson Pittman ([LOC](https://www.loc.gov/item/afc1939007_afs02484a/)). The Detroit Blues Society's list today has five weekly blues jams and about eight more general open jams. The only one in a city neighborhood bar is the Thursday jam at First Place Lounge on Harper; most of the rest are in Macomb and Wayne County suburbs. The list is undated, so confidence is low.
  - West African dance: TeMaTe Institute holds weekly classes with live drummers at 3000 Fenkell ([TeMaTe](https://www.tematedance.com/)).
  - Festivals: the African World Festival (Hart Plaza, since 1983 per web summary; July 10–12, 2026; $15) and the Caribbean Cultural and Carnival Organization (formed 1979; its festival was at Marygrove in August 2025).
- **Appalachian.** Between 1940 and 1970 about 3.2 million Appalachian and Southern migrants settled in the Midwest, and in Detroit they concentrated in Hazel Park, Ypsilanti, Taylor and Warren ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Appalachian_people_in_Metro_Detroit)). In 1973 the NYT wrote about Li'l Abner's bar and the "hillbilly" station WEXL ([NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/1973/03/29/archives/appalachias-hillbillies-trek-north-for-jobs-4-million-migrants.html)). Today I found no heritage-identified Appalachian music organization, only the "Taylortucky" label ([Model D](https://www.modeldmedia.com/features/hillbilly-highway-110716.aspx)). I rate it **fading**; any living bluegrass is in the Layer-1 thread's jams.

- **Japanese.** Novi's Japanese auto-industry community supports the **Great Lakes Taiko Center** and, since 2021, a participatory **MI Bon** dance festival ([GLTC](https://michigantaiko.net/mibon)).

## Part 4. History, people, festivals, archives and the fiddle angle

### 1. French Detroit: "a fiddle was in every house"

No source turned up that documents the music of the Huron/Wyandot, Odawa and Potawatomi communities that came to the strait with Cadillac in 1701 (the [French Heritage Corridor](https://frenchheritagecorridor.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/220313-french-foundations-of-detroit-1.pdf) describes the founding as a multi-nation fur-trade enterprise). That gap is worth a dedicated follow-up. The first well-documented musical life is French. Ste. Anne's parish was founded July 26, 1701, and is the second-oldest continuously operating Catholic parish in the US ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_Sainte_Anne_de_D%C3%A9troit)). The habitants lived on long, thin "ribbon farms" running back from the river.

The county histories collected by [michiganfiddle.com](https://www.michiganfiddle.com/french-fiddling-in-southeast-michigan-a-county-histories-collection/) are a delight.

- Judge James May, in Detroit from 1778 to 1830, remembered weekly "Detroit assemblies" and young people "dancing at the farm houses, by turn, which fronted the river, not more than four arpents apart, from the city to Grosse Point. **A fiddle was in every house**, and music would soon bring sufficient numbers together for a dance any pleasant evening."
- A Grosse Pointe history of 1886 describes the Cabaret du Grand Marais (Big Swamp Tavern), where winter sleigh parties ate game pie and whitefish washed down with peach brandy, then joined hands in *la danse ronde* "to the inspiring strains of a cracked fiddle."
- In 1835 Wm. C. Hoyt found four languages on the streets (French, English, Scotch and Indian) and attended a French dance in a one-story hut serving whisky at three cents a glass. The fiddler was a Black man in a stovepipe hat "sawing away on his old fiddle and keeping time with his feet": "Up here! down there! cross over! all hands around!" By 1890 Hoyt wrote that "the French dancing parties have fallen through the trap door."

The French song repertoire of the Detroit River survived longest on the Canadian side. Windsor-area scholar Marcel Bénéteau has recorded *Vieilles Chansons du Détroit* ([Mudcat](https://mudcat.org/thread.cfm?threadid=73729); [Érudit essay](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/cdd/2001-n55-cdd02408/1035604ar/)). That repertoire lives outside the MSA.

### 2. The 19th century: a Detroit schottische goes south

Adam Couse, a dancing master, opened Detroit's first music store in 1844 (Stephen Foster dedicated a ballad to him). In 1854 he published the **"Detroit Schottisch,"** which reportedly sold around 100,000 copies ([WOTFA](https://wotfa.org/piece/detroit-schottische/); [Tune Archive](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Detroit_Schottische)). Fiddle historian Paul Gifford thinks "it seems reasonable to assume" that the Southern old-time staple **"Flop-Eared Mule"** derives from it ([TTA annotation](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Flop_Eared_Mule_(1))). Washington's old-time fiddlers' association argues for a common ancestor instead ("Sorry, Adam Couse"). Either way, a tune many old-time fiddlers think of as Appalachian has a paper trail to a Detroit parlor-dance shop. In the 1880s another Detroit publisher, O. F. "Cub" Berdan, put out the "Grace Waltz," named for his daughter ([TTA](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:Grace_Waltz)).

Berdan's widow married "Col." John A. Pattee of **New Boston (Wayne County)**, a Civil War veteran of the 24th Michigan who in 1904 formed the "Old Soldier Fiddlers" vaudeville act of Union and Confederate veterans ([Library of Congress photo](https://www.loc.gov/item/2017658732/)). When he died in December 1924 he was a radio star whose WEAF barn dance had to be cancelled ([michiganfiddle](https://www.michiganfiddle.com/col-john-a-pattee-wayne-county/)).

Mid-century immigrant institutions date from the same years. The **St. Andrew's Society** and its Highland Games were founded in 1849 and claim the longest continuous Highland Games in North America ([highlandgames.com](https://highlandgames.com/)); the 177th was on Aug 1, 2026 at Greenmead in Livonia ([WAAM](https://waamradio.com/st-andrews-society-of-detroit-announces-the-177th-annual-highland-games-ceilidh/)). Germans founded the Harmonie singing society the same year ([Bentley](https://findingaids.lib.umich.edu/catalog/umich-bhl-2016043)), and the **Gaelic League** followed in 1920 (in Corktown since the early 1950s; [Gaelic League](https://www.gaelicleagueofdetroit.org/iac/)).

### 3. Henry Ford's dance crusade, myth and reality

This is Detroit's most important and most misremembered folk episode.

**What happened.** In 1923 Ford and Edison "discovered" the 80-year-old dance fiddler Jep Bisbee of Paris, Michigan, and Ford sent him in his private car to record for Edison ([NYT, Nov 24, 1923](https://www.nytimes.com/1923/11/24/archives/jep-fiddles-for-edison-records-made-of-oldtime-selections-played-by.html)). In 1924 Ford founded a **Music Department inside the Ford Motor Company**: real employees on the company payroll who for 23 years played for dances, taught throughout Michigan and promoted "old-fashioned dancing" through publications, radio, records and touring ([J. Soc. American Music, "Assembly Lines and Contra Dance Lines"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-society-for-american-music/article/assembly-lines-and-contra-dance-lines-the-ford-motor-company-music-department-and-leisure-reform/F91300432F272FBC9206CDF18814867D)). He brought the Massachusetts dancing master Benjamin Lovett to Dearborn. The Henry Ford dates this to [1924](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/happy-anniversary-lovett-hall); another account says [1928](https://hmi.homewood.net/ford.html). In December 1925 the Maine fiddler Mellie Dunham arrived by Pullman car and became a vaudeville sensation, although Ford had already invited 38 fiddlers before him ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mellie_Dunham)). In 1926 came the dance manual *"Good Morning"* ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/goodmorning0000unse)) and records by Henry Ford's Old Fashioned Dance Orchestra, such as "A Southern Schottische" (Columbia 555-D, recorded Jan 16, 1926; [DAHR](https://adp.library.ucsb.edu/index.php/mastertalent/detail/320850/Henry_Fords_Old_Fashioned_Dance_Orchestra)). The Edison Institute schools taught dancing from 1929, with Lovett as superintendent until 1936 ([AskUs](https://askus.thehenryford.org/researchguides/faq/424645)). **Lovett Hall** opened on Oct 26, 1937: Henry and Clara led the dancing, the Old Time Orchestra played waltzes and quadrilles, and Lovett called for 300 guests ([The Henry Ford](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/artifact/17021)). A Ford "Early American Dance Music" radio show ran until 1945.

**The ideology.** A 1925 editorial in Ford's *Dearborn Independent* praised dancing of "a rational and racially American character" as "an invaluable instrument for social righteousness" against dances "from the African Kongo." Historians read the program as Americanization and leisure reform: contra lines as "an object lesson in the virtues of modern factory work" (same JSAM article).

**The myth.** Country-music lore says Ford sponsored a national fiddlers' contest, and even a "Henry Ford Gold Cup" in Dearborn (repeated by [homewood.net](https://hmi.homewood.net/ford.html)). Paul Gifford's archival study ([JSAM 2010](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-society-for-american-music/article/abs/henry-fords-dance-revival-and-fiddle-contests-myth-and-reality/C42F37BE1EC7AADBE330E597646E1707)) and his later commentary ([michiganfiddle](https://www.michiganfiddle.com/paul-giffords-commentary-on-the-involvement-of-henry-ford-in-fiddle-contests/)) say flatly: **"he never sponsored any fiddle contests."** The contests of January 1926 were run by Ford *dealers* in Tennessee, Kentucky and southern Indiana as a last-minute fix when a broadcast could not reach the Cincinnati area. Uncle Bunt Stephens then toured claiming he had beaten "5,000" fiddlers in a national Ford contest ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunt_Stephens)), and a 1970 article built on those 1926 clippings fixed the story in place.

**The complications.** Gifford argues the impulse was personal (Ford wanted to dance the way he had while courting Clara in the 1880s). The orchestra's "dulcimer" was a Hungarian **cimbalom** played for more than 20 years by **Billy Hallup**, a Romani musician. Sources disagree on his origins: [The Henry Ford](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/artifact/251641) says Cleveland, Wikipedia says Braddock, Pennsylvania, and Gifford says Slovak Romani parents. Lovett taught at **Lincoln School, Detroit's only Black school (1928)**, and Black fiddlers Ed and John Campbell of Dresden, Ontario, were invited to play with Ford's orchestra. None of this erases the antisemitism of the *Dearborn Independent*, which is why a 2025 headline about Detroit's queer square dance could call it ["Dancing on Henry Ford's Grave"](https://pridesource.com/article/detroit-queer-square-dance-society).

### 4. 1938: Lomax in Detroit, and Detroit's real folk music

Alan Lomax's Library of Congress expedition produced about 250 discs and 8 reels of film ([LOC blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2013/11/michigan-i-o-alan-lomax-and-the-1938-library-of-congress-folk-song-expedition/)). I queried the LOC collection API on Oct 30, 2026: of 1,270 online items (442 audio), **116 are tagged Detroit: 52 sound recordings and 64 card-catalog cards** ([LOC](https://www.loc.gov/collections/alan-lomax-in-michigan/?fa=location:detroit)). The Detroit material is not the Anglo-American folk song Ford was promoting. It is:

- **Aug 5, 1938:** Serbian epic song on the gusle by Pajo Tomić (also filmed), "Kraljević Marko," and Romanian/Serbian **tamburitza** groups.
- **Aug 7-8:** Hungarian soldiers' songs, robber ballads and czardas in Delray, plus Polish singers.
- **Oct-Nov 1:** **Calvin Frazier and Sampson Pittman**, Delta bluesmen, recording "Welfare Blues," "Joe Louis," "Highway 61 Blues" and "I'm in the Highway, Man" ([LOC item](https://www.loc.gov/item/afc1939007_afs02484a/)), and the **Frazier family** singing gospel ("Oh Mary, Don't You Weep," "You Got to Move"). These were Pittman's only recordings.

Five years later **John Lee Hooker** took a job with Ford Motor Company and started playing the clubs on Hastings Street ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lee_Hooker)). In 1948 he cut "Boogie Chillen'" at United Sound Systems ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boogie_Chillen%27)). Hastings Street, Paradise Valley and Black Bottom were demolished in the late 1950s and early 1960s for Lafayette Park and I-75 ([Detroit Historical Society](https://www.detroithistorical.org/learn/online-research/encyclopedia-of-detroit/black-bottom-neighborhood)).

In 1939 Wayne State's **Folklore Archive** was founded by Emelyn Gardner and Thelma James. It was later described as "the oldest and largest record of urban folk traditions in the United States" ([Reuther](https://reuther.wayne.edu/node/14668)) and was broken up in 1999 ([MTAP](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-5)). The **E. Azalia Hackley Collection** of African Americans in the performing arts was founded at the Detroit Public Library in 1943 ([DPL](https://detroitpubliclibrary.org/research/e-azalia-hackley-collection)).

### 5. The Hillbilly Highway and a forgotten Cape Breton capital

From the 1920s to the 1960s Kentucky, Tennessee and West Virginia migrants poured into Detroit; one bus line ran daily from Harlan County to Detroit ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Appalachian_people_in_Metro_Detroit)). A 1953 Wayne State survey found residents ranked "hillbillies" and "poor Southern whites" tied with criminals as the city's most "undesirable" people. Their music had a home anyway. In 1952 **Casey Clark and the Lazy Ranch Boys** took over WJR's "Big Barn Frolic," first at the Dairyworkers Hall in Highland Park and then at the Hudson/Chrysler UAW hall at **12101 Mack Ave**, with a weekly barn dance from 1952 to 1957: stage show first, then square and round dances. Gov. "Soapy" Williams called a square dance from that stage around 1955 ([Car City Country](https://carcitycountry.com/2013/09/12101-mack-labor-and-country-music-in-detroit/)). The bluegrass pioneers **Lonesome Pine Fiddlers** were based in Detroit from January to November 1953 ([Pickers Guide](https://pickersguide.com/album/single-pain-in-my-heart/)). **Fortune Records** (1946-1995), run by Jack and Devora Brown on a $300 start, recorded hillbilly and blues alike ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_Records)). Just outside the MSA, the banjo player Wade Mainer worked the Flint GM plant and became an NEA Heritage Fellow in 1987 ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/wade-mainer)).

The fiddle discovery of this thread is that **Detroit was a Cape Breton fiddling center.** Johnny Archie MacDonald, from Little Judique Ponds, moved to Detroit in 1924, became a Detroit police officer, and "nursed Scottish music through a long succession of music crazes enabling it to survive in the Motor City" ([Scotia Sun, 1973](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/canada/macdonald_johnny.htm)). The **Five MacDonald Fiddlers** (John, Hugh, Bernie, Allan and the great composer **Dan R. MacDonald**, with Joan MacDonald on piano) "met in Detroit for the first time" at dances and house parties and recorded *Scottish Reels, Jigs and Strathspeys* around 1960 ([Back to the Sugar Camp](https://www.backtothesugarcamp.com/Mac.htm)). The Beaton Institute has their photo from a Detroit concert on Jan 31, 1953 ([Beaton](https://www.beatoninstitute.com/five-macdonald-fiddlers-2)). Tunes from that world include "Little Judique Jig" (first recorded by Johnny Archie), Joan MacDonald Boes's "Sweetness of Mary," "The Five Fiddlers," and the Cape Breton jig **"The Detroit"** on Natalie MacMaster's *My Roots Are Showing* ([thesession 10509](https://thesession.org/tunes/10509)).

**Howard "Louie Bluie" Armstrong**, a Black string-band fiddler and mandolinist from Tennessee, moved to Detroit after WWII and largely stopped performing until a 1972 reunion with Carl Martin and Ted Bogan. Terry Zwigoff's 1985 film *Louie Bluie* followed, and in 1990 he became an NEA National Heritage Fellow ([MTAP](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-1B); [NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/howard-armstrong)). He is the metro's only fiddler on the NEA list.

### 6. The coffeehouse years (1960-1980)

- **The Raven Gallery** (1960) was opened by Belle and Herb Cohen, a Saks model and a Linotype setter, on James Couzens and later moved to 12 Mile and Greenfield in Southfield. It presented Josh White Sr. and Jr. and a monthly Dorothy Ashby Trio ([Jewish News](https://www.thejewishnews.com/opinion/the-raven-gallery-a-family-story-of-spilling-the-honey/article_b1048122-7e24-55f7-80b1-d38914d79701.html)). It closed around 1979-80, and a 1991 *Jewish News* piece said fans then had to drive "to Ann Arbor or East Lansing."
- **The Chess Mate** (1963-1971; some say 1973), on Livernois near the University of Detroit, began as an actual chess club run by Michigan chess champion Morrie Widenbaum, and Bobby Fischer played 51 boards there in 1964. It became a 200-seat, no-alcohol room for Gordon Lightfoot, Doc Watson, Buffy Sainte-Marie, Phil Ochs, Rev. Gary Davis, Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf. Neil Young wrote "The Old Laughing Lady" on a napkin at the White Tower across the street after thieves disabled his car. **Joni Mitchell** married Chuck Mitchell in Rochester, Mich. on June 19, 1965, played more than 60 Chess Mate dates in 12 months, and wrote "The Circle Game" in Detroit as an answer to Young's "Sugar Mountain." The building is now a laundromat ([Free Press via jonimitchell.com](https://jonimitchell.com/library/view.cfm?id=4927); [Detroit News](https://www.detroitnews.com/story/entertainment/books/2018/10/31/joni-joni-mitchell-book-excerpt-two-single-acts-survive-marriage-susan-whitall/1825175002/)).
- **The Living End** (c.1964-68, on the Lodge freeway, with a liquor license) and **the Retort** (Mount Royal Hotel, Woodward; [1964 schedule](https://www.flickr.com/photos/muycool/2652624193)) completed the circuit.
- **The Ark** (outside the MSA) was founded in Ann Arbor in December 1965 by four churches as a drug- and alcohol-free student coffeehouse, with shape-note singing from early on ([The Ark](https://theark.org/history/)). Its **Ann Arbor Folk Festival** fundraiser began in 1976 according to The Ark (many sources say 1977). The 49th was in Jan 2026, and the 50th is set for Jan 29-30, 2027 with Jeff Daniels as emcee ([Detroit News gallery](https://www.detroitnews.com/picture-gallery/entertainment/music/2026/09/21/lineup-for-50th-annual-ann-arbor-folk-festival-announced/91829661007/)). Unlike every Detroit room above, The Ark survived; it now owns its building.

### 7. The dance revival began in a museum

According to Paint Creek Folklore Society's own history ([PDF](https://paintcreekfolkloresociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/History-of-the-SNFAR-and-PCCDO.pdf)), the Oakland County contra scene began at the **"Galactic Steam Dulcimer Circus"** at the **Detroit Historical Museum on Nov 13, 1976**. Caller Dale Johnson lined up the jamming musicians and visitors for an impromptu contra. Burt Schwartz, the CDSS contact for Detroit since 1972, called a contra at a Birmingham party in 1977. That December he and Paul Tyler started the **Detroit Country Dance Society**, and from May 1979 it ran a monthly contra and square dance at **St. Timothy's Church, Detroit**, with the Olde Michigan Ruffwater Stringband (formed Sept 26, 1978). Judi Morningstar's "March of St. Timothy" (Oct 2, 1984) is named for that hall ([TTA](https://tunearch.org/wiki/Annotation:March_of_St._Timothy)). "Starry Night for a Ramble" began in Pontiac in 1984 and has moved through Waterford, Royal Oak and Clawson to Troy. COVID cancelled it for 2021-2023; it returned on Feb 10, 2024 at $10.

The Wayne County fiddle contest makes the same point about dance. The **Michigan State Championship Old-Time Fiddlers Contest** at the Huron Township Applefest in New Boston began in October 1985. Since 2006 its director, Jim McKinney, has required a waltz, schottische, jig and reel, uses blind judges and gives points for Michigan repertoire. As he put it, the only common thread of a "Michigan style" is "the use of fiddling to accompany dancing" ([michiganfiddle](https://www.michiganfiddle.com/the-michigan-state-championship-old-time-fiddlers-contest/)). I found no 2024-26 evidence that the contest still runs.

### 8. Heritage bearers the state and nation recognized

The NEA list for Michigan ([arts.gov](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/list?state=MI)) includes these metro-connected fellows: **Howard Armstrong** (1990), **Nadim Dlaikan**, a Lebanese nay player and maker from Southgate (2002), **Yang Fang Nhu**, a Hmong weaver (1988, Detroit), and the **Ukrainian Bandurist Chorus** (2025). The chorus's 17 families immigrated as one ensemble in 1949, sponsored by Sol Hurok and Detroit lawyer John Panchuk, and mostly settled in Hamtramck ([NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/heritage/ukrainian-bandurist-chorus-north-america)). The state's [Michigan Heritage Awards](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/programs/michigan-heritage-awards/) (since 1985) add these metro names:

- Irish fiddler **Mick Gavin** of Redford (2018). Born in Clare, in Detroit since 1975, he started the state's longest-running session (Conor O'Neill's, Ann Arbor) and taught Jeremy Kittel and Tyler Duncan ([MTAP](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-77)).
- **Deacon Albert Likely** (2000), a master of lined-out metered hymns at Hartford Memorial Baptist ([MTAP](https://traditionalarts.msu.edu/mha-awardee?kid=A2-369-3A)).
- Blues singer **Sippie Wallace** (1985), harmonica player **Little Sonny** (1992), **Eddie Burns** (1994), **Alberta Adams** (2010), **Johnnie Bassett** (2012) and **Rev. Robert Jones** (2025).
- Gospel's **Mattie Moss Clark** (1990), **Thomas Kelly** (2016) and **Deborah Smith Pollard** (2021).
- Slovenian polka bandleader **Gaylord Klancnik** (2011), Polish accordionist **Stas Wisniach** (2004), conjunto's **Martin Solis Jr.** (2019), Armenian kanun player **Ara Topouzian** (2022) and oud player **Abdul Karim Bader** (2006).
- Storyteller and singer **Neil Woodward** of Howell (2018).

### 9. COVID and after

The Ark closed in March 2020, put the folk festival online in January 2021, and reopened on July 29, 2021 ([mlive](https://www.mlive.com/news/ann-arbor/2021/07/first-in-person-concert-since-march-2020-fills-the-ark-with-music.html)). It marked its 60th anniversary in 2025 with a kickoff show of 1965 songs ([Michigan Daily](https://www.michigandaily.com/news/ann-arbor/finding-your-folk-the-arks-60th-anniversary-celebrates-music-and-community/)). The most interesting Detroit development is the **Detroit Square Dance Society**. It held queer potluck square dances at Vámonos in Southwest Detroit through 2024 and **joined the new Detroit Folk School in January 2025** ([Substack archive](https://detroitfolkschool.substack.com/archive)). In 2025-26 the school ran monthly Queer Square Dances, a Detroit Old Time Jam, a Cantastoria festival, Cajun dances, the Black Sweet Tea Stringband at Juneteenth, and workshops with Hubby Jenkins, Brittany Haas and Nic Gareiss. The University Musical Society now books the society for square dances at the Ypsilanti Freighthouse ([UMS](https://ums.org/performance/square-dancing/)). In East Lansing, the **Red Cedar Roots Festival** (Aug 14-15, 2026) revived the legacy of the Great Lakes Folk Festival (2002-2017), itself the successor to the Festival of Michigan Folklife (1987-98) and the National Folk Festival (1999-2001) ([Ciesa](https://ciesadesign.com/great-lakes-folk-festival/); [City Pulse](https://www.lansingcitypulse.com/red-cedar-roots-festival-east-lansing-michigan-august-2026/)). The Motor City Irish Fest (since 2011) and the Highland Games keep Livonia's Greenmead busy every summer.

## All surprises

1. Washtenaw County (Ann Arbor, ~370,000 people, a separate MSA) runs about as many trad events a month as the entire 4.4-million Detroit MSA; the Detroit Irish Music Association, the local Comhaltas branch, has held its weekly flagship session in Ann Arbor since 2010.
2. Inside the MSA there is one weekly Irish pub session (Seán O'Callaghan's, Plymouth, since 2023) and one monthly contra (Troy); the busiest trad activity is twice-weekly, Balkan-heavy international folk dance.
3. Henry Ford never sponsored a fiddle contest (the national 'Ford contest' is a myth built on Ford-dealer contests in 1926), but he kept dance musicians on the Ford Motor Company payroll for 23 years, and his 'American' old-time orchestra featured a Romani cimbalom player, Billy Hallup.
4. Lovett Hall in Dearborn, the ballroom Ford built for quadrilles in 1937, hosted a contra series from 1981 until The Henry Ford ended it on four days' notice in February 2005; it now hosts weddings.
5. 'Flop-Eared Mule' may descend from the 'Detroit Schottische', published in 1854 by Detroit dancing master Adam Couse and reportedly sold ~100,000 copies (fiddle historian Paul Gifford; others argue a shared ancestor).
6. Lomax's 1938 Detroit recordings (116 Detroit-tagged LOC items) are mostly Serbian gusle epics, tamburitza and Hungarian songs, plus Delta blues by Calvin Frazier and Sampson Pittman a decade before John Lee Hooker's 'Boogie Chillen''.
7. Detroit was a Cape Breton fiddling hub: the Five MacDonald Fiddlers, including composer Dan R. MacDonald, formed there, and Detroit police officer Johnny Archie MacDonald ran Saturday 'down east' dances featuring Buddy MacMaster and Winston Fitzgerald. No Cape Breton session survives.
8. The SE Michigan contra revival began with an impromptu contra at the Detroit Historical Museum's 'Galactic Steam Dulcimer Circus' on Nov 13, 1976.
9. The newest dance in the city is the Detroit Folk School's monthly Queer Square Dance in Southwest Detroit, which a local paper described as 'dancing on Henry Ford's grave'.
10. There is no Sacred Harp singing in the MSA, yet The Sacred Harp contains a tune called 'Detroit' (39t), and Ann Arbor's singing meets inside The Ark folk club.
11. Detroit's public radio station WDET has no local folk show; Detroit's best-known folk DJ, Matt Watroba, broadcasts from WKAR in East Lansing.
12. The Gospel Music Workshop of America was founded in Detroit in 1967; gospel is the metro's largest singing tradition, and since 2024 ten-plus quartets sing outside the Motown Museum to reclaim the quartet-Motown link.
13. Seven of roughly 24 music/dance Michigan Heritage Award winners in the metro are blues artists; the Ukrainian Bandurist Chorus, which immigrated as a whole choir of 17 families in 1949, became Detroit's first ensemble NEA Heritage Fellow in 2025.
14. About 550 students still attend 14 Polish folk-dance schools in metro Detroit (2025 census), while Hamtramck, now majority Arab and South Asian, cheers a parody polka band called 'The Polish Muslims'.
15. A Dearborn public elementary school has had an Arabic music ensemble since 2002, and the Arab International Festival drew ~250,000 people before anti-Muslim protest costs ended it in 2012.
16. The Cadieux Cafe claims the only feather-bowling lanes in the US and hosts a Monday blues jam in the same room.
17. Joni Mitchell wrote 'The Circle Game' in Detroit; the Chess Mate, a real chess club where Bobby Fischer played 51 boards in 1964 before it became a folk room, is now a laundromat.

## Curiosities for phase two

1. Does a college-town satellite out-dance its core metro elsewhere (Ann Arbor vs Detroit; Boulder vs Denver; Cambridge vs Boston; Berkeley vs SF; Chapel Hill vs Raleigh)?
2. Is international folk dance stronger than contra in other Rust Belt metros (Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Chicago)?
3. Why did Boston keep its Cape Breton scene while Detroit's (1920s-70s) vanished?
4. Do Hillbilly Highway metros (Detroit, Chicago Uptown, Cincinnati, Dayton, Akron) keep denser old-time and bluegrass jam scenes in their old migrant suburbs, or did that music vanish as it did here?
5. Which 1930s field-recorded ethnic traditions (Detroit's tamburitza and gusle) survive as living practice in each metro, and which metro has the most active tamburica orchestras today?
6. How many big-metro NPR stations have no local folk show, and does that track with weaker listening scenes?
7. Can gospel quartet anniversary programs be counted and compared across Detroit, Memphis, Birmingham, Chicago and Houston?
8. When an ethnic enclave turns over (Hamtramck, Delray), do heritage institutions follow the diaspora to the suburbs or vanish? Compare Pilsen, Slavic Village, Polish Hill.
9. Did any other industrialist put folk musicians and dance teachers on the payroll as Ford did, and with what legacy?
10. Where and when did queer and gender-neutral square dances start, and are they drawing younger dancers faster than mainstream contras?
11. Does any other state's heritage-award roster tilt as far toward blues for its biggest city?
12. Is the schottische more central to Midwestern contest and dance repertoire than elsewhere (Michigan contest rules require one)?

## Data quality and method

web_search was heavily rate-limited for all Detroit threads; agents used direct fetches, DuckDuckGo/Brave result pages and organization APIs (Gaelic League WordPress feed, AACTMAD iCal, LOC API). Undercounted: Facebook-only events (gospel quartet anniversaries, church concerts, ethnic dance rehearsals, some pub sessions), house concerts. Blues jams come from an undated Detroit Blues Society list and an aggregator; excluded from totals. Detroit Old Time Jam has no posted 2026 dates (medium). Detroit Folk School Queer Square Dance added by the merger from the school's events page and a search summary (Oct 17, 2026 at Vámonos; cadence assumed monthly, medium confidence). Ukrainian, Hungarian and Serbian rehearsal schedules lack 2024-26 evidence. Michigan State Championship Old-Time Fiddlers Contest: no evidence since 2014. tunearch.org blocked direct access (quoted via search snippets). No source found on Anishinaabe/Wyandot music in early Detroit. Founding-date disagreements: Ann Arbor Folk Festival 1976 (The Ark) vs 1977/78; Concert of Colors 1992 vs 1993; Chess Mate closing 1971 vs 1973; Highland Games 1849 is the Society's founding year (first Games unverified). Michigan Heritage Award counts (43 of 147 in the MSA) are the heritage agent's tally. Spot checks by the merger: Ghost Light is open with a weekly Tuesday songwriter night (corrected a 'closed Aug 2026' note).

This dossier was assembled from four parallel research threads (trad/dance, song/singing, heritage, history), each checked against primary pages where possible, then merged and de-duplicated. The structured data is in `detroit.json` (53 recurring-event entries, 46 festivals, 61 organizations, 102 people, 115 timeline entries, 225 source URLs). Per-thread coverage notes are preserved in the JSON `data_quality_notes` field.
