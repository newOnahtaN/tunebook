A field survey · October 2026

Where the tunes live

An attempt to count every Irish session, old-time jam, contra dance, shape-note singing, song circle and heritage practice in America's 20 largest metro areas, and then to ask what the counts mean.

Ten research sessions spent a day reading organizer calendars, association jam lists, church bulletins, Meetup pages, radio schedules, archives, obituaries and oral histories for two metros each. They wrote a dossier and a structured census of recurring events for every metro. A coordinator then pulled in the Census, the full thesession.org database, every NEA National Heritage Fellow since 1982, national dance and singing directories, and four follow-up studies. In total: 1,502 counted series, 944 dead ones, 1,984 people and 7,430 cited sources.

"Folk" here means four layers: tune sessions and social dance; song circles, folk clubs and open mics; traditional singing; and the living heritage practice of each metro's communities. A meeting counts if it recurs, is open to the public, meets inside the metro's counties, and had evidence in 2024–26. Modern western club square dancing is reported separately. "Trad meetings" means the first three layers.

Part 1The scoreboard

Raw counts favor big cities, so the fair comparison is per person. On that measure, Boston leads with 64.8 trad meetings a month per million residents. Inland Empire trails at 2.5. A fiddler moving from Riverside to Boston gets about 26 times as many chances to play per neighbor.

New York has the most of everything (294 meetings a month, 69 of them Irish sessions) but spreads it over 20 million people, so it ranks 13th. The three metros that punch furthest above their size are Boston, the SF Bay and Washington. All three are old, rich, highly educated metros, a pattern Part 3 picks up.

Part 2Every city's folk fingerprint

The same word, "folk," covers very different weeks. In Denver, 58% of all meetings are tune sessions and jams. In San Diego and Los Angeles, dancers dominate. In Orlando, 49% of the calendar is heritage practice: Puerto Rican bomba and plena, Colombian cumbia, Haitian twoubadou, mariachi, Greek dance, powwow.

The jam scene alone splits the country three ways. Plot every city by its share of Irish sessions, bluegrass jams and old-time jams and you get a map without geography. The Northeast and Chicago crowd the Irish corner; the Sun Belt slides toward bluegrass; and almost nobody is near the old-time corner. Old-time, the music this tune book is fullest of, is the rarest of the three in 17 of the 20 metros. The exceptions are a surprise: in Miami and Tampa Bay old-time jams outnumber Irish sessions, and in Atlanta they tie. By raw count its strongest showings are the Twin Cities (18 jams a month), Boston (17) and Seattle (16.7).

Dance is just as regional. Contra, the New England dance that most of these scenes grew from, is the biggest dance form only in a handful of cities. Los Angeles, San Diego and San Francisco are international and Israeli folk dance towns; Philadelphia and Chicago lean on céilí and Scottish country dance; Seattle and the Twin Cities have real Scandinavian scenes.

Then there is the other square dance. Modern western square dancing, the club form with callers, levels and petticoats, is the largest dance form in much of the Sun Belt and in the Bay Area. In SF Bay its clubs list 102 club nights a month. It is counted separately because only some dossiers listed every club.

Part 3Twelve questions the research raised

Every dossier ended with questions. Many recurred across cities, so these twelve got follow-up research and data of their own.

Question 1What actually predicts a big folk scene?

The obvious guesses are size, Irish heritage or a southern fiddling past. None of them is the strongest predictor. That is education and money, which travel together. The share of adults with a bachelor's degree explains about 67% of the variation in trad density (r = 0.82), and median household income does about as well (r = 0.84). By rank, which is less swayed by Boston and the Bay Area, education is the clearer signal (0.85 vs. 0.74). Irish ancestry, owned buildings and latitude come next. Population size doesn't predict density at all.

Answer: the revival scenes (sessions, contra, shape-note, song circles) behave like a college-educated pastime. Heritage practice doesn't: heritage events per million have almost no correlation with education (r = 0.05). The revival and heritage layers are two separate folk worlds with different social bases.

Question 2Do folk scenes follow the U-Haul?

Since 2020 Americans have poured into Dallas, Houston, Orlando, Phoenix and Tampa, and out of New York, LA, San Francisco and Chicago. If musicians move with everyone else, the boomtowns should be catching up.

Answer: not yet. Four of the five fastest-growing metros (Dallas–Fort Worth, Orlando, Houston, Phoenix) rank 14th to 19th for density, and the fifth, Tampa Bay, is 10th (r = -0.45). The dossiers suggest why: scenes take decades and institutions to build, and Sun Belt metros grew by suburbs. Their jams run on weekday mornings at senior centers and RV resorts (Phoenix), in parking lots (Orlando's Friday jam behind a Pizza Hut since about 1990), and around the winter snowbird calendar. Florida's numbers swell from November to April.

Question 3Does owning the building matter?

The Twin Cities dossier noticed that its densest block, Minnehaha Avenue, is anchored by a hall the dance community owns. Detroit's scene lost a 24-year contra series when Henry Ford's museum gave it four days' notice. A follow-up survey catalogued every folk facility a community owns or controls across the 20 metros: 52 facilities (49 owned, long-leased or public), plus 17 documented losses.

Answer: mostly yes (r = 0.49 for the raw count, 0.58 per capita). Ownership seems to buy protection from landlords rather than guarantee success: Chicago's Swedish Club owned its building for 88 years and still closed. The losses read like a list of folk landmarks: Gerde's Folk City (rent nearly tripled), Peoples' Voice Cafe (its church was sold for a condo tower), Lovett Hall (four days' notice), DC's Potter's House (closed January 2026). A third model works too: public land plus a nonprofit partner. Glen Echo Park, Balboa Park's international cottages and Phoenix's Irish Cultural Center all run that way.

Question 4Where does folk actually happen?

Every counted event's venue was sorted into kinds by keyword. Each city's folk has a home address, and they differ a lot.

Answer: pubs in the Irish North (35% of Chicago's meetings, 33% of Boston's); churches in the South (31% in Houston, 31% in Atlanta, where fellowship halls host jams and the Sacred Harp lives in country churches); halls and lodges where communities own them (54% in the Twin Cities). The Inland Empire meets in parks and schools, and Phoenix and LA in senior centers and retirement villages.

Question 5Is the real scene in the college town next door?

Three dossiers independently said that their metro's most intense scene sits just outside the Census boundary, in a university town.

Answer: yes, dramatically. Boulder County runs about 198 meetings per million residents a month, roughly 5 times the Denver metro. Ann Arbor out-plays the whole Detroit metro per person by about 7 to 1; Detroit's own Comhaltas branch has met there since 2010. Inside the metros the same thing happens at smaller scale: Cambridge and Somerville for Boston, Berkeley for the Bay. The college effect from Question 1 shows up at town level too.

Question 6Did contra dancing survive COVID?

Several dossiers described contra as hit hard: SF had none from 2020 to 2024, and San Diego went from weekly to twice a month. A follow-up study rebuilt each metro's 2019 contra calendar from Wayback Machine snapshots of organizer sites and contradancelinks.com.

Answer: in the strong scenes, yes. Every flagship weekly dance in Boston, Seattle, Chicago, the Twin Cities, Philadelphia and Atlanta is still weekly. Where contra was already thin it shrank further: Miami, Dallas–Fort Worth, San Diego and Denver lost about half or more. The study counted 9 series born since 2020 and 14 that died. Several of the "COVID deaths" were declining before 2020. Gender-neutral calling (larks and robins) is now near-universal, but it spread before the pandemic: Boston's gender-free contra dates to 1988, and Philadelphia scheduled its vote for March 2020.

Question 7Where did the fiddle contests go?

Fiddle contests were the original public stage for American fiddling: Atlanta's Georgia Old-Time Fiddlers' Conventions filled a 5,000-seat auditorium for 22 years. Is contest culture still urban?

Answer: mostly not. 9 of the 20 metros have no fiddle contest inside them, including Chicago, Houston, Philadelphia, the Bay Area and Seattle. Contests survive in the West's fiddlers'-association country (Phoenix, Denver, LA's Topanga since 1961), in Texas, and in Minnesota. Elsewhere state associations are run from small towns 100–300 miles away. A small counter-tradition: Berkeley (1968) and San Diego's Balboa Park (1970s) both ran mock contests with rutabagas as prizes. The Balboa Park contests ended when the park stopped supplying power for the PA.

Question 8When does America play?

Every event with a day of the week was pooled across all 20 cities.

Answer: Sunday is session day: 33% of Irish sessions fall on Sunday, often in the afternoon. That is why NFL Sundays keep evicting them (see Patterns). Weeknights belong to dancers, and Saturday is contra's night. Song circles, old-time jams and shape-note singings are the most daytime traditions, with about half starting before 5 pm. Seattle's best-documented old-time jams meet at 2 pm on weekdays, and Phoenix's bluegrass meets on weekday mornings. Open mics and contra are almost entirely evening events.

Question 9How long do things last, and why do they die?

The dossiers recorded 944 dead sessions, venues, dances, festivals and radio shows; 623 have start and end years.

Answer: a pub session that ended lasted a median of five years, and it usually ended because the pub changed, not because the players quit. Tampa's dossier found that every session that died was cut by the owner. Dallas lost one to a BMI licensing threat; New York's Cabaret Law limited unlicensed rooms to three musicians and no wind instruments. Of today's series with a known start date, 46% began after 2010 and 30% after 2020. The scene is old in its traditions but young in its institutions, constantly rebuilt by new hosts.

Question 10Does thesession.org tell the truth?

Thesession.org is the closest thing to a national census of Irish sessions. Several dossiers found it stale; Phoenix, the fifth-largest metro, has one listing.

Answer: it's a good relative guide (listings per capita correlate at r = 0.89 with verified sessions per capita) but a poor absolute one. It misses a fifth to a half of the sessions in Chicago, Boston and the Bay Area. In New York it errs the other way: its 38 listings outnumber the 24 sessions verified at medium or high confidence, because dead sessions linger. Half the current listings in these metros (86 of 168) were added or edited since 2022, which suggests renewed community upkeep.

Question 11Who gets the medals?

The NEA National Heritage Fellowship is the country's highest honor for traditional artists: 524 people (502 awards) since 1982. 149 of them lived in one of these 20 metros.

Answer: honors and everyday activity barely overlap. New York has 40 fellows (klezmer and Yiddish song, Irish, Greek, Balkan, Jewish liturgical music) but low per-capita density. Boston, the densest scene, has 8; Seattle has 2; Denver has 1; Phoenix has none, because all five Arizona fellows live in Tucson or on reservations. The fellowship rewards tradition bearers in heritage communities, while the busiest calendars belong to the revival, so the two measure different things. Bess Lomax Hawes, who created the fellowship, had taught mass guitar classes in 1950s Los Angeles.

Question 12What does it cost, and who's on the radio?

Folk is overwhelmingly free: 71% of priced meetings cost nothing, because sessions, jams and song circles don't charge. Dances do. Contra admission runs $10–20 everywhere, which in the Bay Area is about 18 minutes of median household income and in Detroit about 20.

Answer: radio is splitting into haves and have-nots. Boston (WUMB) and the Twin Cities have full-time folk stations; Orlando has no FM folk show at all, and its best-known folk "program" is Roger McGuinn's monthly Folk Den website. Public stations keep dropping folk shows (WFUV in 2023 and 2026, WDET in 2009), while community and college stations keep them.

Part 4Patterns that kept turning up

Some patterns were too odd to chart and appeared in city after city.

Football vs. the session

NFL Sundays have evicted Irish sessions in Houston (at least five pubs), Phoenix (the session moved to Saturday after a pub's NFL package ousted it in 2013) and Denver (the Abbey Tavern session shifts to 6 pm in season and cancels for Broncos playoff games).

One building carries the scene

Glen Echo (DC), the Irish Center in Mount Airy (Philadelphia), Minnehaha Avenue (Twin Cities), First Lutheran in Midtown Houston (five traditions in one church), the Canadian American Club (Watertown), Balboa Park (San Diego). Many scenes depend on one roof.

One family, one host

One family's three pubs host about 80 Boston sessions a month. One host runs all three of Tampa's Irish sessions. Orlando's session hosts bought their pub, and rebuilt it when it was demolished.

Tunes from unlikely places

"Waiting for Nancy" (San Diego, 1972). "Spotted Pony" (a Seattle LP editing error). "Flop-Eared Mule" possibly from the 1854 "Detroit Schottische". "Orange Blossom Special" (a Miami resident). Boston has 24 tune titles on The Session.

Firsts in unexpected cities

The first country fiddle record (New York, 1922), the first radio barn dance (Fort Worth, 1923), the word "hootenanny" (Seattle, 1941), the word "zydeco" on record (Houston, c. 1947), US international folk dance (San Francisco, 1938), Western swing named (LA, 1942).

Queer dance as a revival engine

Gender-free contra began in Jamaica Plain (1988); the international gay square-dance association was founded in SF (1983). Today there are LGBTQ+ square clubs in Atlanta, NYC, LA and Miami, a monthly Queer Square Dance in Detroit, and queer contras in Chicago and the Twin Cities.

Heritage traditions: the dossiers documented 799 heritage traditions and rated each by vitality. The SF Bay and New York have the most thriving or active traditions, and the count tracks foreign-born share (r = 0.47). Even the quietest trad metros are busy here: the Inland Empire has Cahuilla bird singing, Phoenix O'odham waila and Yaqui pascola, Houston zydeco, Miami Haitian and Afro-Cuban drumming, Tampa Greek Dodecanese music, Detroit Arab and Chaldean music.

AppendixHow this was made

  • Geography. The 20 largest Metropolitan Statistical Areas by Census Bureau Vintage 2025 estimates (July 1, 2025), from New York (20.1M) to Orlando (2.96M; Charlotte missed by 19,000). Only events inside each MSA's counties count; college towns just outside (Boulder, Ann Arbor, Athens, Princeton, Palo Alto) are reported separately.
  • Research. Ten parallel research sessions, each covering two metros with four sub-threads (trad and dance, song and media, heritage, history and people), read primary sources: organizer and association calendars, church and hall schedules, Meetup and event pages, radio schedules, Library of Congress and state folklife archives, newspapers and oral histories. Every series needed a URL and 2024–26 evidence. Each wrote a 7,000–25,000-word dossier.
  • Counting. Frequency is converted to meetings a month (weekly 4.33, twice-monthly 2, monthly 1, annual 1/12). Headline figures use only medium- and high-confidence series; 384 meetings a month of low-confidence listings are excluded. Also excluded: online-only events, listen-only concerts, series with stale evidence, and modern western club square dancing (317 club nights a month where enumerated), whose coverage varied by dossier. "Trad" = sessions, jams, social dance and singing; open mics and heritage events are in totals but not in trad density.
  • Central datasets.
